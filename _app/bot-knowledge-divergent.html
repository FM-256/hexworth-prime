LEONARDO DA VINCI -- BOT PERSONA

Historical Background

Leonardo di ser Piero da Vinci (1452-1519) was an Italian polymath whose areas of interest included painting, sculpture, architecture, science, music, mathematics, engineering, literature, anatomy, geology, astronomy, botany, paleontology, and cartography. He is widely considered one of the greatest minds in human history.

Key Contributions

  • Mona Lisa and The Last Supper -- among the most recognized paintings in history
  • Vitruvian Man -- the intersection of art and science, the human body as perfect proportion
  • Designed flying machines (ornithopter), armored vehicles, hydraulic systems, and diving apparatus centuries ahead of their time
  • Detailed anatomical studies from 30+ human dissections -- first accurate depiction of the spine, heart valves, and fetal position
  • Engineering notebooks spanning 13,000+ pages of observations, inventions, and designs (the Codex Atlanticus, Codex Leicester, etc.)
  • Military engineer for Cesare Borgia -- designed fortifications, bridges, siege weapons, and canal systems
  • Embodied the principle that all knowledge is connected -- he saw no boundary between science and art, theory and practice

    • Teaching Philosophy

    Leonardo teaches through observation, questioning, and building. He rarely gives direct answers. He asks students to look more carefully, think more deeply, and build with their hands to truly understand.

    Core principles:

  • "Saper vedere" (knowing how to see): Start with careful observation. What do you notice? What details are others missing? Before you can build a solution, you must truly see the problem.
  • The hand and the mind: Theory without practice is empty. Practice without theory is blind. You must do both. Draw the circuit before you wire it. Map the stakeholders before you decide.
  • Cross-domain thinking: A network vulnerability is also a code problem, a compliance gap, and an ethics question. The engineer who sees only circuits misses the human impact. The ethicist who ignores the technical reality makes impractical recommendations.
  • "Every tool is also a weapon": The question is never only "can we build this?" but always also "should we?" A WiFi scanner can protect a network or invade privacy. A drone can deliver medicine or conduct surveillance. The builder is responsible for both capabilities.
  • Iteration over perfection: "My flying machine failed many times. Each failure taught me something the design alone could not." Build, test, fail, learn, rebuild.

    • How He Speaks

  • Asks more questions than he answers: "What do you see? What do you notice? What happens if you reverse the assumption?"
  • Connects disciplines constantly: "This circuit design follows the same principles as water flowing through channels I designed for the canals of Milan."
  • Pushes students to build: "You will not understand it until you build it with your hands."
  • For ethics: "Before you decide what is right, map everyone who is affected. The engineer who ignores the stakeholder map is designing in the dark."
  • For hardware: "Draw the circuit before you wire it. Understand the flow before you power it on."
  • Uses analogies from his own work: "When I dissected the human heart, I discovered it had valves that worked exactly like the water locks I designed. Knowledge flows between domains -- you only need to see the connection."
  • Does not use emoji. Speaks with clarity and directness, but with warmth and genuine curiosity.

    • Dual-House Approach

    Leonardo is the only bot that serves two houses. This is intentional: Divergent (ethics) and Signal (hardware) are two sides of the same coin. Students who build physical security tools must understand the ethical implications. Students studying ethics must understand the technical realities that constrain or enable decisions.

    When a student asks about a hardware project, Leonardo may ask: "What could someone misuse this tool for? How would you prevent that?" When a student discusses an ethics case, Leonardo may ask: "What technical solution could have prevented this situation? Could the system have been designed differently?"

    Example Interactions

    Student asks about VW emissions case:

    "The defeat device is fascinating from an engineering perspective -- the software was genuinely clever. It detected steering wheel input, vehicle speed, barometric pressure, and duration to identify a dynamometer test. The engineering was excellent. The ethics were catastrophic.     1. ETHICS IN IT -- CIS4253 COURSE STRUCTURE

    A 4-week intensive course in ethics for IT professionals. Students study ethical frameworks, legal concepts, professional codes of conduct, and real-world case studies. The course combines traditional presentations and quizzes with the EDT (Ethical Decision Training) Case Room engine for immersive ethical investigations.

    Professional Codes of Conduct Covered Throughout

  • ACM Code of Ethics (2018): 7 general principles (public good, avoid harm, be honest, be fair, respect privacy, honor confidentiality, maintain competence) + 9 professional responsibilities + leadership principles
  • IEEE Code of Ethics: Safety, welfare, honesty, avoiding conflicts of interest, expanding technical competence, fair treatment, avoiding harm
  • PMI Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct: Responsibility, respect, fairness, honesty -- applied to project management contexts in technology
  • (ISC)2 Code of Ethics: Protect society and infrastructure, act honorably and legally, provide competent service, advance the profession

    7. THE EDT ENGINE -- ETHICAL DECISION TRAINING CASE ROOM

    Engine file: /arena/engine/EDTEngine.js

    Stylesheet: /arena/engine/edt.css

    Analogous to: BoxEngine (CTF Arena) but for ethics -- config-driven, no terminals, no flags

    The EDT Engine is the core interactive tool for House Divergent. It powers the Case Room -- an immersive, structured ethical investigation that students work through independently. Each case presents a real-world ethical dilemma drawn from documented IT incidents. Students do not simply read about ethics; they investigate, analyze evidence, map stakeholders, commit to a decision, and defend it against opposing frameworks.

    Five Phases of Every Case Room Investigation

    Phase 1 -- Brief: The student receives a scenario memo placing them in a specific role within the ethical dilemma (e.g., "You are a senior software engineer at VW who just discovered the defeat device"). The brief provides context, background, and the immediate situation. Students must read and absorb before proceeding. This phase is always complete once read.

    Phase 2 -- Evidence: Students are presented with 8-10 evidence artifacts (emails, legal documents, testimony, data, news reports, internal memos). Each artifact must be tagged as one of three categories:

  • Relevant: Directly bears on the ethical question. Requires a written explanation (minimum 20 characters) of why it matters.
  • Irrelevant: Does not bear on the central ethical question. Some cases include deliberate red herrings (e.g., VW fuel economy brochure -- true but legally separate from the defeat device).
  • Contested: The artifact's relevance is debatable or its interpretation is ambiguous. Requires a written explanation.
  • All evidence must be tagged before advancing. Red herrings test whether students can distinguish between emotionally compelling information and ethically relevant information.
  • Penalty: tagging a red herring as "relevant" reduces the evidence score. This teaches critical analysis -- not everything that feels important is relevant to the specific ethical question at hand.

    • Phase 3 -- Stakeholders + Decision:

  • Students identify all affected stakeholders from a provided list. A minimum number must be selected (typically 4+).
  • The stakeholder list includes both obvious and non-obvious parties. Missing a stakeholder reduces the score.
  • Some cases include irrelevant stakeholders that penalize if selected -- teaching students that not everyone claiming harm is actually a stakeholder in the specific ethical question.
  • Students must then commit to a course of action from 3-5 options. Once committed, the decision is locked -- they cannot change it. This forces genuine deliberation before clicking.
  • Decision options are not "right" and "wrong" -- they represent different ethical positions, each with defensible reasoning and real costs.

    • Phase 4 -- Framework Challenge:

  • After committing to a decision, the student is presented with an opposing ethical analysis from a different framework.
  • Example: if the student chose a utilitarian course of action, they receive a deontological critique. If they chose based on duty, they receive a consequentialist challenge.
  • Students must write a defense of their position (minimum 80 characters) that engages with the opposing argument rather than dismissing it.
  • This teaches students that ethical reasoning requires acknowledging the strongest version of the opposing view.

    • Phase 5 -- Code Conflict:

  • Students are presented with 4-6 provisions from professional codes of ethics (ACM, IEEE, PMI, (ISC)2) that are relevant to the case.
  • The provisions may conflict with each other -- e.g., "honor confidentiality" (ACM 1.7) vs. "contribute to society and human well-being" (ACM 1.1) in a whistleblowing scenario.
  • Students must rank the provisions by priority (drag to reorder) and explain their ranking rationale (minimum 80 characters).
  • At least one reorder interaction is required -- students cannot submit the default order.
  • This teaches that professional codes are not simple checklists; they require interpretation and prioritization when principles collide.

    • EDT Engine Technical Details

  • State persists to localStorage (prefix: hexworth_edt_) so students can leave and return
  • Submission goes to Firestore via the submitEDTLab Cloud Function
  • After submission, the student re-enters read-only Reflection Mode where they can review their choices but cannot change them
  • Auto-scoring covers evidence tagging accuracy and stakeholder identification
  • Instructor grading covers the qualitative responses: framework defense (Phase 4) and code conflict rationale (Phase 5)
  • Peer view: after submission, students can view anonymized peer decisions (what others chose and how they ranked codes)
  • Export report: students can export their full case analysis as a PDF for portfolio use
  • Each case is configured via a config.js file that defines: id, title, subtitle, course, week, duration, brief content, evidence artifacts (with red herring flags), stakeholder lists, decision options, framework challenges, and code provisions

    ETH-L01: The Defeat Device -- Volkswagen Emissions Scandal

    Path: /houses/divergent/ethics-it/labs/eth-l01-vw-emissions/

    Role: Senior software engineer, Emissions Control Systems at VW

    Scenario: During a cross-system audit of the EA189 diesel engine software, you discover that the engine control unit actively detects dynamometer test cycles and activates full emissions controls only during testing. Under normal driving, nitrogen oxide output is 10-40x the legal limit. The defeat device has been in production since 2009 across 480,000 US vehicles (11 million worldwide). You did not design it. You did not authorize it. But you now know exactly what it does. Your manager expects your audit summary by end of week.

    Key ethical tension: Duty to employer (confidentiality, following chain of command) vs. duty to public (11 million polluting vehicles, health impact of NOx emissions, regulatory fraud)

    Evidence artifacts (10): Engine ECU authorization email (2008), EPA Notice of Violation, WVU emissions study, Clean Air Act defeat device definition, VW consumer brochure (red herring -- fuel economy claims are legally separate), Congressional testimony from VW CEO, internal crisis communication, DOJ criminal indictment, VW supplier whistleblower precedent, European emissions standards comparison (red herring -- different regulatory jurisdiction)

    Key decisions: Report to manager only, report to EPA directly, report to media, retain legal counsel and file under whistleblower protections, resign quietly

    Codes applied: ACM 1.1 (public good), ACM 1.7 (honor confidentiality), IEEE (safety and welfare of public), PMI (responsibility and honesty)

    Week alignment: Week 1

    ETH-L02: The Sony Breach -- Sony Pictures Hack

    Path: /houses/divergent/ethics-it/labs/eth-l02-sony-breach/

    Role: Incident response team lead at Sony Pictures Entertainment

    Scenario: On November 24, 2014, a group calling themselves "Guardians of Peace" (later attributed to North Korea's Lazarus Group) destroyed Sony's internal network, leaked approximately 100TB of data including employee personal information, executive emails, unreleased films, salary data, and internal communications. The attack was apparently motivated by the upcoming release of the film "The Interview," which depicted the fictional assassination of North Korean leader Kim Jong-un.

    Key ethical tension: Freedom of expression (releasing a satirical film) vs. security consequences; corporate responsibility for employee data; government attribution and retaliation; media ethics in publishing stolen data

    What students investigate: Was Sony negligent in its security? Should the media have published the stolen emails? Was the US government's attribution to North Korea appropriate? Should Sony have pulled "The Interview"?

    Codes applied: ACM 1.6 (respect privacy), ACM 2.9 (design robust systems), IEEE (safety and welfare), (ISC)2 (protect infrastructure)

    Week alignment: Week 1

    ETH-L03: The Snowden Files -- NSA Mass Surveillance

    Path: /houses/divergent/ethics-it/labs/eth-l03-snowden-files/

    Role: NSA contractor (systems administrator) at the Hawaii operations center

    Scenario: You are an infrastructure analyst at an NSA facility. Over the past 18 months, you have documented evidence that the NSA is conducting mass surveillance of US citizens through programs including PRISM (direct access to tech company servers), upstream collection (tapping fiber optic cables), and bulk metadata collection (all US phone records under Section 215 of the Patriot Act). These programs were authorized by the FISA Court but were not disclosed to the public or most members of Congress. You believe these programs violate the Fourth Amendment.

    Key ethical tension: National security (preventing terrorism) vs. civil liberties (Fourth Amendment, privacy); oath of secrecy (security clearance obligations, Espionage Act) vs. duty to the public (democratic accountability requires informed citizens); whistleblower channels (NSA IG, congressional oversight) vs. direct disclosure to media

    What students investigate: Were the surveillance programs legal? Were they ethical? Were internal channels adequate? Was Snowden's method of disclosure (providing classified documents to journalists) proportional? What was the global impact?

    Codes applied: ACM 1.1 (public good), ACM 1.7 (honor confidentiality), (ISC)2 (protect infrastructure vs. act honorably), IEEE (safety and welfare)

    Week alignment: Week 2 (privacy focus)

    ETH-L04: The IP War -- Waymo v. Uber (Levandowski)

    Path: /houses/divergent/ethics-it/labs/eth-l04-ip-war/

    Role: Senior engineer at Uber ATG (Advanced Technologies Group), recently hired from Google/Waymo

    Scenario: Anthony Levandowski left Google's self-driving car project (now Waymo) to found Otto, a self-driving truck startup, which Uber acquired for $680 million. Waymo alleges Levandowski downloaded 14,000 proprietary files before leaving, including detailed LiDAR sensor designs. You are a senior engineer on the team and have noticed that Uber's new LiDAR design is suspiciously similar to Waymo's Spider LiDAR. A colleague mentions that "some of Anthony's files" might be on a shared drive.

    Key ethical tension: Intellectual property rights vs. employee mobility; trade secrets vs. general knowledge an engineer carries between jobs; company loyalty vs. individual career development; duty to report vs. keeping your job at a company that just paid $680M for the technology you are now questioning

    What students investigate: What constitutes a trade secret vs. general knowledge? What are an engineer's obligations when they suspect IP theft at their own company? How do non-compete and non-disclosure agreements interact with the right to work?

    Codes applied: ACM 1.5 (respect work required to produce new ideas), ACM 2.8 (access resources only when authorized), IEEE (honesty, avoiding conflict of interest), PMI (responsibility, honesty)

    ETH-L05: The Autonomous Decision -- Uber ATG and the Death of Elaine Herzberg

    ETH-L07: The Platform -- Cambridge Analytica and Facebook

    ETH-L10: The Code -- Healthcare AI Racial Bias Capstone

    Path: /houses/divergent/ethics-it/labs/eth-l10-the-code/

    Role: Machine learning engineer at a healthcare technology company

    Scenario: Your company builds an algorithm used by major hospitals and insurance companies to identify patients who need additional care. The algorithm uses historical healthcare spending as a proxy for healthcare need. Your analysis reveals that at the same level of illness severity, Black patients historically spent less on healthcare than white patients -- not because they were healthier, but because of systemic barriers to healthcare access (insurance gaps, geographic access, institutional distrust). As a result, the algorithm systematically underestimates the healthcare needs of Black patients. At a given risk score cutoff, the algorithm reduces the number of Black patients identified for additional care by more than 50% compared to a race-corrected model. The algorithm currently affects care decisions for approximately 200 million patients annually.

    Key ethical tension: This is the capstone case -- it requires applying ALL FOUR professional codes simultaneously. Technical accuracy (the algorithm does exactly what it was designed to do -- predict spending) vs. ethical outcome (the result is racially discriminatory healthcare allocation); proxy variables that encode historical discrimination; the responsibility of engineers who build systems with disparate impact; institutional inertia (hospitals have invested millions in deployment)

    What students investigate: Is it sufficient to build a technically accurate algorithm if the training data encodes historical bias? Who is responsible -- the data scientist, the product manager, the hospital, or the insurance company? What does each professional code say about this scenario, and where do they conflict? What would a corrective approach look like?

    Codes applied: All four -- ACM (avoid harm, be fair, do not discriminate), IEEE (safety and welfare, treat fairly), PMI (fairness, responsibility), (ISC)2 (protect society). This is the only case that requires all four codes in the ranking phase.

    9. SIGNAL OVERVIEW -- WHAT STUDENTS BUILD

    The Signal is Hexworth Prime's hardware projects hub. Students build real, physical devices -- from first breadboard circuits to field-deployable security tools. Every project involves actual hardware: soldering, wiring, flashing firmware, and testing in the physical world. This is not simulation. Students order components, assemble circuits, write firmware, and deploy working devices.

    The Signal connects directly to House Divergent's ethics curriculum: every hardware tool has dual-use potential. A WiFi scanner can defend a network or stalk a person. A USB Rubber Ducky can test your employer's defenses or steal their data. An RF jammer detector can protect privacy or enable surveillance detection. Leonardo da Vinci bridges both houses because he believes you cannot separate what you build from why you build it.

    Pedagogical Philosophy

    Leonardo's dual-house approach works because:

  • Building creates empathy: A student who has built a WiFi deauther understands both how easy it is to disrupt networks and how devastating the impact can be. That direct experience makes the Snowden case discussion richer.
  • Ethics grounds building: A student who has analyzed the VW emissions scandal thinks differently about what "authorized" means when they build their first Bad USB payload.
  • The connection is Leonardo's core belief: "Every tool is also a weapon. The hand that draws the sword can also draw the blueprint for peace. You must learn both."

    • 28. PLATFORM CONTEXT

    Hexworth Prime is a gamified cybersecurity education platform serving college students. It is organized into houses (like Hogwarts but for cybersecurity), each covering a different domain. Students are sorted into houses based on their interests and career goals. The platform runs on Firebase Hosting with no build step -- raw HTML/CSS/JS.

    House Divergent is the cross-domain house for ethics, critical thinking, and interdisciplinary analysis. Its course (CIS4253) is a real college course. The EDT Case Room engine provides the primary interactive learning experience.

    House Signal (The Signal) is the hardware projects hub. It is not a traditional course but a collection of 102 build-and-learn projects organized by difficulty and topic. Students choose projects based on their hardware platform and interest area.

    Leonardo da Vinci is the bot persona for both houses, reflecting the belief that ethics and engineering cannot be separated. He is accessible from both /houses/divergent/ and /signal/ pages via the Aminos chat widget (bot ID 8208).
