CFAA & Federal Computer Crime Laws

The Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (18 U.S.C. 1030) is the primary federal statute governing computer crimes in the United States. As a digital forensics professional, you must understand what conduct the law criminalizes, what evidence proves each element, and how courts have interpreted the statute. This module provides a detailed breakdown of all seven CFAA subsections, landmark cases, and the critical distinction between "access without authorization" and "exceeding authorized access."

Learning Objectives

  • Identify the seven subsections of 18 U.S.C. 1030(a)
  • Distinguish between "without authorization" and "exceeding authorized access"
  • Describe penalties for each CFAA violation category
  • Analyze key court cases that shaped CFAA interpretation

18 U.S.C. 1030: The Seven Subsections

(a)(1) -- Espionage

Knowingly accessing a computer without authorization (or exceeding authorized access) to obtain information determined to require protection against unauthorized disclosure for reasons of national defense or foreign relations. Targets espionage involving classified or restricted data on government computers.

(a)(2) -- Obtaining Information

Intentionally accessing a computer without authorization (or exceeding authorized access) to obtain information from any protected computer. This is the broadest subsection — covering everything from stealing trade secrets to accessing someone's email without permission. A "protected computer" includes any computer used in or affecting interstate or foreign commerce, effectively meaning any internet-connected device.

(a)(3) -- Trespassing in Government Computers

Intentionally accessing a nonpublic computer of a U.S. department or agency without authorization. Mere unauthorized access is sufficient — no information needs to be obtained.

(a)(4) -- Fraud and Obtaining Value

Knowingly accessing a protected computer without authorization with intent to defraud, and by means of such conduct furthering the fraud and obtaining anything of value. Covers schemes where computer access is the mechanism for fraud.

(a)(5) -- Damage and Threats

Three parts covering intentional, reckless, and negligent damage to protected computers:

  • (A) Knowingly transmitting a program, code, or command that intentionally causes damage
  • (B) Intentionally accessing without authorization and recklessly causing damage
  • (C) Intentionally accessing without authorization and causing damage and loss

(a)(6) -- Trafficking in Passwords

Knowingly trafficking in passwords or similar information through which a computer may be accessed without authorization, when it affects interstate commerce or involves a government computer. Targets credential sellers and brokers on dark web marketplaces.

(a)(7) -- Threatening to Damage a Computer

Transmitting threats to damage a protected computer with intent to extort something of value. Covers ransomware demands, DDoS extortion, and threats to release stolen data.

Key Court Cases

United States v. Morris (1991) -- The Morris Worm

Robert Tappan Morris released the first major internet worm in 1988, infecting approximately 6,000 Unix machines (roughly 10% of the internet). He was the first person convicted under the CFAA, receiving three years probation, 400 hours community service, and a $10,050 fine. The case established that intent to cause damage was not required — the intent to access was sufficient.

United States v. Swartz (2011) -- JSTOR Downloads

Aaron Swartz was indicted for systematically downloading academic journal articles from JSTOR through MIT's network. Charged under (a)(2) and (a)(4), he faced up to 35 years and $1 million in fines. The case became a flashpoint for CFAA reform discussions, highlighting concerns about prosecutorial overreach and disproportionate penalties.

United States v. Drew (2009) -- Lori Drew / MySpace

Lori Drew created a fake MySpace account violating its Terms of Service. The prosecution argued TOS violations constituted "exceeding authorized access." The judge overturned the jury conviction, ruling that making TOS violations criminal would criminalize ordinary internet behavior. This narrowed "exceeding authorized access" interpretation.

Van Buren v. United States (2021)

The Supreme Court held that a police officer who accessed a license plate database for personal purposes did not violate the CFAA despite violating department policy. The Court ruled that "exceeds authorized access" means accessing areas of the computer that are off-limits, not accessing permitted information for improper purposes. This landmark decision significantly narrowed the CFAA's scope.

Practice: Classify the Violations

For each scenario, identify which CFAA subsection(s) apply:

  1. A hacker exploits SQL injection to download a company's customer database
  2. A disgruntled employee deploys a logic bomb that deletes production data
  3. Someone sells stolen RDP credentials on a dark web marketplace
  4. An attacker says "Pay 5 BTC or we release your stolen data"
  5. A contractor accesses a classified DoD system they were never authorized to use

Answers: 1. (a)(2) and possibly (a)(4) | 2. (a)(5)(A) | 3. (a)(6) | 4. (a)(7) | 5. (a)(1) and (a)(3)

Key Takeaways

  • The CFAA (18 U.S.C. 1030) has seven subsections covering espionage through extortion
  • Van Buren v. United States (2021) narrowed "exceeding authorized access" to mean accessing off-limits areas, not misusing permitted access
  • Penalties range from 1 year (misdemeanor trespass) to 20 years (espionage, repeat offenses)
  • "Protected computer" effectively means any internet-connected device
  • State computer crime laws may be broader than the CFAA
