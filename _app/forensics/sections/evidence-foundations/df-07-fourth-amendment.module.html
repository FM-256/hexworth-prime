Fourth Amendment & Digital Search

The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures. In the digital age, courts have grappled with applying 18th-century language to smartphones, cloud storage, and location data. This module covers landmark Supreme Court decisions that define the boundaries of digital searches and the doctrines every forensic examiner must understand.

Learning Objectives

  • Apply the Katz reasonable expectation of privacy test to digital scenarios
  • Explain the holdings in Riley v. California and Carpenter v. United States
  • Identify when the plain view doctrine applies to digital forensics
  • Describe exceptions to the warrant requirement for digital evidence

The Fourth Amendment Text

"The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated, and no Warrants shall issue, but upon probable cause, supported by Oath or affirmation, and particularly describing the place to be searched, and the persons or things to be seized."

The Katz Test: Reasonable Expectation of Privacy

In Katz v. United States (1967), the Supreme Court established the two-prong test for determining whether a search has occurred:

  1. Subjective prong: Did the individual exhibit an actual expectation of privacy?
  2. Objective prong: Is that expectation one that society recognizes as reasonable?

If both prongs are satisfied, a warrant is generally required. The Katz test replaced the older property-based "trespass" analysis, though the Court has sometimes returned to property concepts in digital cases.

Third-Party Doctrine

Under the third-party doctrine (Smith v. Maryland, 1979; United States v. Miller, 1976), information voluntarily shared with third parties carries no reasonable expectation of privacy because the individual "assumed the risk" of disclosure. Historically, this meant:

  • Phone numbers dialed (shared with phone company) — no warrant needed
  • Bank records (shared with bank) — no warrant needed
  • IP addresses (shared with ISP) — typically no warrant needed

However, Carpenter v. United States significantly narrowed this doctrine for digital data.

Riley v. California (2014)

The Supreme Court unanimously held that police must generally obtain a warrant before searching the digital contents of a cell phone seized during an arrest. Chief Justice Roberts wrote that cell phones are "such a pervasive and insistent part of daily life that the proverbial visitor from Mars might conclude they were an important feature of human anatomy."

Key Holdings

  • The search-incident-to-arrest exception does not apply to cell phone data
  • Cell phones differ from physical objects because of their immense storage capacity
  • Digital data on phones can reveal far more than the most exhaustive physical search
  • Police may examine the phone's physical aspects (remove battery to prevent remote wipe) but may not access digital contents without a warrant

For Forensic Examiners: Riley means that even when a phone is lawfully seized (e.g., during arrest), you cannot begin forensic examination of its contents until a warrant is obtained. Secure the device, prevent remote wipe (Faraday bag, airplane mode), and wait for the warrant.

Carpenter v. United States (2018)

The Court held (5-4) that the government generally needs a warrant to access historical cell-site location information (CSLI) from wireless carriers. The decision marked the first time the Court recognized a Fourth Amendment interest in data held by a third party in the digital context.

Key Holdings

  • Historical CSLI provides an "intimate window into a person's life" and is protected by the Fourth Amendment
  • The third-party doctrine does not automatically apply to digital records that provide comprehensive surveillance capabilities
  • Cell phone location data is not truly "voluntarily" shared — phones log location passively
  • The decision was explicitly narrow — the Court left open questions about real-time CSLI, tower dumps, and other surveillance techniques

Plain View Doctrine in Digital Forensics

Under the plain view doctrine, if an officer is lawfully in a position to observe evidence, the evidence can be seized without a warrant. In digital forensics, this applies when:

Immediately apparentContraband visible on a tableCSAM filename visible during authorized keyword search
Inadvertent discoveryNot specifically looking for this evidenceFound evidence of different crime while executing warrant for fraud

Digital Scope Limits: Courts are divided on how broadly examiners can search digital media. Some circuits require "search protocols" that limit the examiner to files relevant to the warrant. If you discover evidence of a crime outside the warrant's scope, document it, stop, and seek an additional warrant before further examination of that evidence.

Border Search Exception

The border search exception allows customs officers to search persons and property entering the United States without a warrant or probable cause. The extent to which this applies to digital devices has been contested:

  • Basic search (scrolling through photos, checking recent calls): Generally allowed without any suspicion
  • Advanced/forensic search (connecting to forensic tools, copying entire device): Most circuits require at least reasonable suspicion
  • The Ninth Circuit (United States v. Cotterman, 2013) and Fourth Circuit (United States v. Kolsuz, 2018) require reasonable suspicion for forensic device searches at the border

Practice: Fourth Amendment Scenarios

For each scenario, determine whether a warrant is required:

  1. Police arrest a suspect and want to search the photos on their iPhone
  2. FBI requests 6 months of cell tower location data from Verizon for a robbery suspect
  3. An examiner executing a fraud warrant finds child exploitation material in an unrelated folder
  4. CBP wants to forensically image a traveler's laptop at an airport customs checkpoint
  5. A company's IT department searches an employee's company-issued laptop for policy violations

Answers: 1. Yes (Riley) | 2. Yes (Carpenter) | 3. Document and get new warrant (plain view + scope issue) | 4. Reasonable suspicion likely required (Cotterman/Kolsuz) | 5. No — Fourth Amendment applies to government action, not private employers (but check state laws and employment agreements)

Key Takeaways

  • The Katz test (subjective + objective expectation of privacy) determines if a search occurred
  • Riley v. California (2014): warrant required to search cell phone contents, even during lawful arrest
  • Carpenter v. United States (2018): warrant required for historical CSLI; narrowed third-party doctrine for digital data
  • Plain view doctrine applies to digital forensics but with scope limitations
  • Border search exception: basic phone search OK; forensic examination likely requires reasonable suspicion
