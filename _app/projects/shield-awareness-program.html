Subject

Build a Security Awareness Program

Operative 1.5 hr 500 XP
Mission Brief

People are the largest attack surface in any organization — and the security leader's job is to build the human firewall. You will design a security awareness program: who it targets, what it teaches, a simulated-phishing plan, the metrics that prove it works, and a rollout calendar.

This is how a leader changes behavior at scale and shows the business a return on the security spend — the difference between a poster on a wall and a measurable drop in risk.

By the end you will have a complete, defensible awareness program you could pitch to leadership tomorrow.

Agent Requirements
  • No technical prerequisites
  • An organization (real or fictional) to design for
  • Familiarity with phishing as a concept
  • A document and a spreadsheet

Segment the Audience & Baseline Risk
A core SETA principle — reinforced throughout NIST SP 800-50 Rev. 1 (2024) — is that an awareness program must be tailored to the roles and risk profiles of different groups, not delivered uniformly. Your first deliverable is a segmentation table that names each audience and its specific human-layer threats.
  1. List every distinct user population in your organization. Start with the broadest categories, then refine. Common starting segments are: General Staff, Finance & Accounting, IT & Security, Executives and Board Members, Developers, and Remote or Third-Party Contractors. A company of 200 people might have 4–6 meaningful segments; a 10,000-person enterprise might have 10+.
  2. For each segment, name its top 2–3 human-layer risks using the specific attack or mistake name, not just "phishing" for everyone. Use the table below as your starting template and replace the example rows with your organization's actual segments.
    AUDIENCE SEGMENTATION TABLE Organization: [Acme Corp / replace with yours] Segment | Size | Top Risk 1 | Top Risk 2 | Top Risk 3 -------------------|-------|-----------------------------------|------------------------------------|--------------------------- General Staff | 850 | Phishing (credential harvest) | Removable-media malware | Password reuse / weak creds Finance & Acctg | 40 | Business Email Compromise (BEC) | Invoice-redirect fraud | Spear-phishing impersonation Executives / Board | 12 | Whaling (exec impersonation) | Pretexting / social engineering | Personal-device data exposure IT & Security | 25 | Privilege abuse / insider risk | Spear-phishing for cred access | Misconfiguration from urgency Developers | 60 | Secrets in source code (Git) | Supply-chain / dependency attacks | Phishing via fake CI/CD alerts Remote Contractors | 120 | Unmanaged-device data leakage | VPN credential phishing | Shoulder surfing / home-office eavesdropping
    Why specificity matters: A foundational SETA principle is that one-size-fits-all delivery fails — a point explicit in NIST SP 800-50 Rev. 1 (2024), which grounds effective programs in role-based audience analysis. If finance and general staff receive the same module on "phishing," you will miss Business Email Compromise entirely — a scenario that exploits finance-specific authority and approval workflows, not just curiosity clicks.
  3. Score each segment's risk exposure on a simple 1–3 scale (Low / Medium / High) based on data sensitivity accessed, external-facing functions, and previous incident history. High-scoring segments get more training touchpoints per year and are prioritized for phishing simulations. Add a "Risk Level" column to your table.
  4. Draft a one-paragraph needs-assessment statement for the program charter. This is the "why" sentence your leadership sign-off is built on. Paste it below the table.
    NEEDS ASSESSMENT STATEMENT (paste into your charter) [Organization] employs [N] people across [X] functional segments. A review of the past 12 months identified [Y] phishing attempts, [Z] credential-related incidents, and [W] BEC attempts targeting the Finance team. General Staff and Finance represent the highest human-layer risk surface due to [data access / approval authority / external communication volume]. This program will reduce the click rate on simulated phishing from a baseline of [establish at Month 1] to below 5% within 12 months, and will increase the suspicious-email reporting rate from [baseline] to above 40%.
Common mistake — treating all staff as one segment: A single "annual security awareness training" pushed to all 800 employees with identical content satisfies a compliance checkbox but does not reduce risk. Executives clicking on whaling lures and finance staff wiring money to fraudulent accounts require entirely different training narratives, examples, and cadences. The segmentation table is the structural fix — it forces you to confront what each group actually needs to know.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • At least 3 audience segments
  • Each has its top 2 human risks named specifically, not just "phishing" for everyone

Plan the Training Content
NIST SP 800-50 Rev. 1 (2024) guidance calls for organizing awareness and training content according to the roles and responsibilities of each audience. You will build a topic-by-segment matrix that specifies which modules each audience receives, the delivery format, and the estimated annual hours — keeping all-staff mandatory training lean while adding depth for high-risk groups.
  1. Select your topic library. The six core topics below cover the threats most organizations face. Add or remove topics based on your segmentation table from Phase 1.
    CORE TOPIC LIBRARY ID | Topic | What it teaches ------|--------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------- T-01 | Phishing & Email Threats | Recognize lures, check senders, verify links before clicking T-02 | Passwords & MFA | Passphrase creation, password manager use, authenticator apps T-03 | Data Handling & Classification | What counts as sensitive data, how to share/store it safely T-04 | Physical Security | Tailgating, clean-desk policy, screen locking, visitor escorts T-05 | Incident Reporting | What to report, who to contact, no-blame culture, the 10-minute rule T-06 | Social Engineering | Pretexting, vishing, impersonation, urgency manipulation tactics T-07 | Business Email Compromise (BEC) | Wire-transfer verification, dual-approval, out-of-band confirmation T-08 | Secure Development Basics | Secrets in code, dependency scanning, secure-commit hygiene T-09 | Executive Threat Awareness | Whaling, personal-device risk, social-media profiling, travel threats
  2. Build the topic-by-segment matrix. Mark each cell with the delivery format and frequency: M = mandatory module, S = simulation, B = briefing, - = not required. Customize the template with your actual topics and segments.
    TOPIC-BY-SEGMENT MATRIX Topic | General Staff | Finance | Executives | IT/Security | Developers | Contractors ------|---------------|---------|------------|-------------|------------|------------ T-01 | M (annual) | M (2x) | M (annual) | M (annual) | M (annual) | M (annual) T-02 | M (annual) | M (2x) | B (annual) | M (annual) | M (annual) | M (annual) T-03 | M (annual) | M (2x) | M (annual) | M (annual) | M (annual) | M (annual) T-04 | M (annual) | - | - | M (annual) | - | M (annual) T-05 | M (annual) | M (2x) | B (annual) | M (annual) | M (annual) | M (annual) T-06 | M (annual) | M (2x) | B (2x) | - | - | M (annual) T-07 | - | M (qtr) | B (2x) | - | - | - T-08 | - | - | - | M (annual) | M (2x) | - T-09 | - | - | M (qtr) | - | - | - Key: M = online module, B = executive briefing (30 min), qtr = quarterly, 2x = twice/year
    Keep mandatory all-staff modules short. NIST SP 800-16 recommends awareness materials that fit the audience's attention window. A 45-minute all-hands module generates resentment and checkbox-clicking. Target 10–15 minutes per core module for general staff; add depth in role-specific supplements. Completion rates drop sharply above 20 minutes for voluntary-feeling material.
  3. Write a one-sentence learning objective for each module you will deploy. Objectives follow the pattern: "After completing this module, [audience] will be able to [observable action] when [specific scenario]." Measurable objectives let you design quiz questions that actually test the behavior.
    SAMPLE LEARNING OBJECTIVES T-01 (General Staff): After completing this module, employees will be able to identify at least 3 indicators of a phishing email and use the "Report Phishing" button rather than replying or clicking links. T-07 (Finance): After completing this module, Finance staff will be able to apply the dual-approval procedure and out-of-band verification call before processing any wire transfer request received by email, regardless of apparent sender authority. T-09 (Executives): After completing this briefing, executives will be able to describe 2 methods attackers use to harvest personal information from social media for use in whaling campaigns, and will review their LinkedIn/X privacy settings within 30 days.
  4. Select delivery channels for each segment. Match the channel to the audience and to what actually produces behavior change, not just awareness.
    DELIVERY CHANNEL GUIDE Channel | Best for | Notes -------------------------|-----------------------------------|------------------------------- LMS online module | All-staff mandatory compliance | Trackable completion, quiz gate Phishing simulation | All segments, scaled difficulty | Hands-on, immediate feedback Executive briefing | Board, C-suite | 30-min live session, no LMS Lunch-and-learn | Developers, IT | Voluntary, topic-driven, Q&A Digital poster / screensaver | Reinforcement, all staff | 3-4 per year, tip-of-the-month Intranet security portal | Reference, all staff | Policy links, "what to report" Table-top scenario | IT, Security, Executives | Crisis-decision rehearsal
Common mistake — no "how to report" module for every segment: Organizations that train staff to recognize threats but give them no clear reporting path see zero improvement in reporting rates — the leading indicator of a healthy security culture. T-05 (Incident Reporting) must appear in every segment's matrix. The reporting path should be a single action: one button, one email alias, one phone number. If the path is unclear, staff default to ignoring the threat or deleting the email.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • A topic-by-segment matrix
  • Every segment has at least a phishing module and a "how to report" module

Design a Simulated Phishing Plan
Phishing simulation is the most powerful behavior-change tool in a SETA program — because it makes the training real. A well-designed simulation gives staff a controlled, low-stakes encounter with an actual lure, then teaches immediately at the moment of failure. A poorly designed simulation punishes staff and destroys the reporting culture you are trying to build.
  1. Set the simulation scope and authorization. Phishing simulations must be run against your own organization only and must be authorized in writing by the CISO, legal counsel, and (in many jurisdictions) HR. Document the scope before running a single campaign.
    PHISHING SIMULATION AUTHORIZATION RECORD Program Owner : [CISO name and title] Legal Sign-Off : [General Counsel / Legal name] HR Notification : [HR Director name] -- HR must know to prevent disciplinary confusion Union / CBA Review: [Labor Relations / Union Steward name] -- REQUIRED in unionized workplaces; running simulations without CBA review can constitute an unfair labor practice. Confirm with Legal if a CBA applies. Scope : All employees at [domain] -- contractors on separate schedule Exclusions : Executive assistants handling wire transfers (separate BEC-specific program) Platform : [GoPhish (self-hosted) / KnowBe4 / Proofpoint Security Awareness] Notification to Staff : General awareness that simulations occur periodically (no specific dates) Data Retention : Click/report data retained 24 months for trend analysis; no PII in reports Punitive Use : PROHIBITED. Simulation results are used for targeted training only.
  2. Build a difficulty ramp across four quarters. Start easy so staff build confidence catching lures. Escalate to sophisticated spear-phish by Q4. A staff member who fails a Q1 basic lure needs different remediation than one who fails a Q4 spear-phish — track them separately.
    ANNUAL SIMULATION CADENCE Quarter | Difficulty | Lure Type | Personalization --------|------------|----------------------------------------|-------------------- Q1 | Level 1 | Generic IT help-desk "reset your password" | None (mass blast) Q2 | Level 2 | Familiar vendor impersonation (FedEx, DocuSign) | First name in greeting Q3 | Level 3 | Internal department impersonation (IT, Finance) | Dept name, plausible sender Q4 | Level 4 | Spear-phish: specific role, real context | Name, role, recent org event Frequency : 1 campaign per quarter (minimum); high-risk segments receive 2/quarter Campaign Size : Stagger sends over 2 weeks per campaign -- do not blast all at once Lure Sources : Rotate themes: credential harvest, malicious attachment, fake MFA prompt
  3. Design the clicked-the-link response flow. This is the most important design decision in the program. The response when a staff member clicks a simulated lure must be educational, immediate, and explicitly non-punitive. Shame destroys the reporting culture.
    CLICKED-THE-LINK RESPONSE FLOW Trigger : User clicks link in simulated phishing email Step 1 -- Immediate landing page (shown within 2 seconds of click): "This was a simulated phishing test run by the Security team. You clicked a link that, in a real attack, could have compromised your credentials. You are not in trouble. Take 3 minutes to learn what to look for next time." [Link to 3-minute micro-module on what to look for in this lure type] Step 2 -- Micro-module (3--5 minutes, not 45): Shows the actual lure with annotations: fake sender domain, urgency language, suspicious URL on hover. Ends with a 2-question quiz to confirm the lesson landed. Step 3 -- Manager notification: NONE for a single click. Manager is notified only if the same individual clicks on 3+ campaigns in 12 months AND has not completed remediation modules. This threshold prevents treating normal human error as a performance issue. Step 4 -- Repeat clicker remediation: Offer 1-on-1 security coaching session (voluntary framing). Not a disciplinary action. Never disclosed to HR without a separate investigation trigger (actual incident, not simulation). WHAT NOT TO DO: - Do not email the person's manager after a single click - Do not post click rates by name on a team dashboard - Do not reduce simulation difficulty to make numbers look good - Do not run simulations during high-stress periods (year-end close, open enrollment, mergers) -- stress increases click rates and the data is not representative
    Why non-punitive is not optional: Research across Fortune 500 awareness programs consistently shows that punitive phishing programs produce staff who hide clicks rather than report them — destroying your most valuable leading indicator, the reporting rate. The goal is a culture where staff forward suspicious emails immediately. That culture requires psychological safety.
  4. Write the simulation plan summary in one page that you can attach to the program charter and share with HR to prevent confusion when a manager receives a complaint.
    PHISHING SIMULATION PROGRAM SUMMARY (for charter appendix) Purpose : Provide realistic, low-stakes practice to build phishing recognition skills across all staff. Measure baseline and track improvement. Operator : Information Security team (not HR, not management) Cadence : Quarterly for all staff; bi-monthly for Finance and IT segments Difficulty: Escalates from generic lures (Q1) to targeted spear-phish (Q4) Response : Educational landing page + 3-min micro-module at moment of click No punitive action for simulation clicks Manager notification only at 3+ clicks/year after remediation offered Reporting : Aggregate metrics reported to leadership quarterly (never by name) Individual data visible only to Security team and the individual Review : Program difficulty and lure types reviewed annually with CISO + HR
Common mistake — punitive simulation destroys reporting culture: An organization that fires or disciplines employees for clicking simulated phishing lures will see reporting rates collapse to near zero within two quarters. Staff learn to ignore suspicious emails rather than forward them, because forwarding creates a record that could be used against them. The simulation then measures fear of consequences, not phishing recognition skill. Positive reinforcement — praising reports, celebrating near-misses — produces 3–5x higher reporting rates than punitive programs according to multiple enterprise SETA program studies.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • A simulation plan with a cadence and an escalating difficulty
  • The clicked-the-link response is teaching-first and explicitly not punitive

Define Metrics & Reporting
A program without metrics is just spend. NIST SP 800-50 Rev. 1 (2024) directs organizations to define metrics and conduct regular evaluations to measure program effectiveness against defined objectives. You will define five metrics with targets, a baseline-collection plan for Month 1, and a leadership dashboard that shows progress in business-risk language rather than security jargon.
  1. Define your five core metrics using the table below. Set a realistic target for 12 months. Every target should be grounded in your Month 1 baseline — you cannot set "reduce click rate to 5%" if you do not know the current rate. The baseline is collected from the first phishing simulation (Q1, Level 1 difficulty).
    METRICS DEFINITION TABLE NOTE: The targets below are EXAMPLE targets for planning purposes only. Your actual targets must be set against your Month 1 baseline -- they are not authoritative NIST figures. Industry benchmarks (e.g., Proofpoint State of the Phish report) show wide variance by organization size and sector; treat the values below as calibration anchors, not pass/fail lines. Metric ID | Metric Name | What it measures | Baseline Source | Example 12-Month Target ----------|--------------------------|------------------------------------------|-----------------------|------------------------ M-01 | Phishing Click Rate | % staff who click a simulated lure | Q1 sim (Level 1) | Below 5% M-02 | Suspicious Email Report Rate | % staff who report a sim or real lure | Q1 sim + helpdesk | Above 40% M-03 | Training Completion Rate | % assigned staff completing modules on time | LMS tracking | Above 95% M-04 | Repeat Clicker Rate | % staff clicking 3+ sims/year | 12-month cumulative | Below 2% M-05 | Time-to-Report | Median minutes from receipt to report | Q1 real-lure reports | Below 15 minutes WHY REPORT RATE IS THE PRIMARY SUCCESS METRIC: Click rate measures failure. Report rate measures the behavior you actually want: staff who recognize a threat and act. An organization with a 12% click rate but a 60% report rate has a healthier security culture than one with a 4% click rate and a 5% report rate -- because the second organization's staff have learned to stay quiet, not to stay safe. Track both. Lead with report rate in leadership conversations.
  2. Design the Month 1 baseline collection sprint. You cannot manage what you have not measured. The first month of the program is data-collection only — no scoring, no performance pressure.
    MONTH 1 BASELINE SPRINT Week 1--2: Deploy Q1 Level-1 phishing simulation to all staff. Record: click rate, report rate, time-to-report per simulation. Do NOT announce results publicly yet. Week 2--3: Run a 5-question anonymous survey via HR tool (not security tool): - "Do you know who to contact if you receive a suspicious email?" - "Have you forwarded a suspicious email to IT/Security in the past 6 months?" - "How confident are you identifying phishing? (1-5 scale)" - "Did you complete security training in the past 12 months?" - "Do you feel comfortable reporting a security mistake without fear of punishment?" Week 3--4: Pull LMS data for training completion rates from prior 12 months (if no LMS exists, this is the first data point -- record 0%). Review helpdesk ticket volume for password-reset and account-lockout requests as a proxy for credential-attack frequency. Output: A one-page Baseline Report with values for M-01 through M-05. This becomes the denominator for all future progress claims.
  3. Build the quarterly leadership dashboard. One page, no tables of raw percentages — lead with trend lines and business-language interpretation.
    LEADERSHIP DASHBOARD TEMPLATE (one page, quarterly) SECURITY AWARENESS PROGRAM -- Q[N] DASHBOARD Period: [Month] to [Month] [Year] KEY INDICATORS Phishing Click Rate : [X]% (target: <5%) [arrow up/down vs last quarter] Suspicious Email Reports: [X]% (target: >40%) [arrow up/down vs last quarter] Training Completion : [X]% (target: >95%) [arrow up/down vs last quarter] Repeat Clickers : [X]% (target: <2%) [arrow up/down vs last quarter] Avg Time-to-Report : [X] min (target: <15 min) [arrow up/down] TREND CHART: [Bar or line chart, click rate vs. report rate across 4 quarters] WHAT THIS MEANS IN BUSINESS TERMS: "This quarter, [X]% of staff who received a simulated phishing email reported it to the Security team -- up from [Y]% in Q1. A 40% report rate means that in a real attack, our Security team would receive an alert within [Z] minutes on average, allowing us to block the campaign before it reached the majority of staff. Each percentage-point improvement in report rate reduces the expected window for a credential-harvest attack to go undetected." INCIDENTS LINKED TO AWARENESS GAPS THIS QUARTER: [N] ACTION ITEMS FOR NEXT QUARTER: [2--3 bullet points]
  4. Write the measurement plan section of your program charter so leadership approves the data-collection methods before you run the first simulation.
    MEASUREMENT PLAN (for charter) Data Sources: - Phishing simulation platform: click, report, time-to-report per campaign - LMS: module completion rates, quiz pass rates per segment - IT helpdesk: password-reset volume, account-lockout tickets - Anonymous staff survey: confidence self-assessment, cultural indicators Reporting Cadence: - Quarterly dashboard: CISO + Executive Sponsors - Annual summary: Board / Audit Committee - Real-time alerts: Security team only (never shared raw by name) Data Privacy: - Individual click data not disclosed outside Security team - Survey data aggregated (n<5 per group suppressed) - Retention: 24 months rolling
Common mistake — measuring click rate alone and ignoring report rate: Click rate is a lagging indicator of failure. It tells you who fell for a lure after the fact. Report rate is a leading indicator of readiness: it tells you how quickly your human sensors will alert the Security team when a real attack lands. Organizations that optimize only for low click rates (by running very easy simulations or over-training until staff memorize lure patterns) can achieve a 2% click rate while their reporting rate stays below 5% — meaning 95% of real threats will land silently. Treat report rate as the primary success metric in every leadership conversation.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • At least 4 metrics defined with targets
  • Report rate is explicitly treated as a primary success metric (not click rate alone)

Build the Rollout Calendar & Pitch
A program without a calendar is a plan that will not be executed. Monthly themes give staff predictable, digestible content rather than a once-a-year compliance dump. The leadership pitch frames the program in business-risk language, not security jargon, because the people approving budget are not security professionals.
  1. Assign a monthly awareness theme tied to your topic library from Phase 2. Use the 12-month calendar below as a starting template. National Cybersecurity Awareness Month in October is a natural anchor for a high-visibility campaign.
    12-MONTH CONTENT CALENDAR Month | Theme | Primary Topic | Segment Focus | Deliverable / Activity -----------|--------------------------------|---------------|----------------------|---------------------------------- January | New Year, New Habits | T-02 Passwords | All Staff | MFA enrollment push; passphrase tip sheet February | Think Before You Click | T-01 Phishing | All Staff | Q1 Simulation (Level 1, generic lure) March | Clean Desk, Clear Mind | T-04 Physical | All Staff + Facilities | Poster campaign; clean-desk audit April | Your Data, Your Responsibility | T-03 Data Class| All Staff + Finance | Module deploy; Finance: BEC micro-module May | Recognize the Con | T-06 Social Eng| All Staff | Q2 Simulation (Level 2, vendor impersonation) June | Security Is Everyone's Job | T-05 Reporting | All Staff | Reporting-path reminder; recognition for reporters July | Exec Threat Awareness | T-09 Executive | Executives / Board | 30-min executive briefing; personal LinkedIn privacy guide August | Secure by Default | T-08 Dev Sec | Developers + IT | Dev lunch-and-learn; secrets-in-code module September | Protect the Money Flow | T-07 BEC | Finance + Exec Asst | Q3 Simulation (Level 3, internal impersonation) October | Cybersecurity Awareness Month | All topics | All Staff | Themed campaign; town hall; Q&A with CISO; trivia/prize November | Social Engineering Season | T-06 Social Eng| All Staff | Q4 Simulation (Level 4, spear-phish); holiday-fraud tip December | Year in Review + What's Next | Program recap | All Staff + Leadership | Dashboard presentation; survey; set targets for Year 2
    Spacing matters: Monthly themes prevent the "annual dump" pattern — a single 2-hour training session followed by 11 months of silence. NIST SP 800-50 Rev. 1 (2024) frames effective learning programs around continuous reinforcement and behavior change over time, not single-event delivery. Twelve short touchpoints outperform one long one.
  2. Write the leadership pitch. This is the one paragraph you will use to request budget and secure executive sponsorship. Keep it in business-risk language. Every security term must map to a dollar value or a business consequence.
    LEADERSHIP PITCH (one paragraph, for budget request or Board summary) "The human element is a contributing factor in approximately 68% of security breaches (Verizon Data Breach Investigations Report, 2024), yet [Organization] currently has no formal mechanism to measure or reduce that risk. This program will run four phishing simulations per year against our highest-risk segments, deploy 12 monthly awareness touchpoints to all [N] staff, and establish measurable targets: a phishing click rate below 5% and a suspicious-email reporting rate above 40% within 12 months -- both are example targets to calibrate against your own Month 1 baseline (see Phase 4). The reporting rate target is the critical one -- a 40% report rate means our Security team receives an alert on a credential- harvest campaign within an average of 15 minutes of it landing, which is the window between 'threat contained' and 'breach requiring external notification and potential regulatory fine.' The annual program budget of [$X] compares favorably to the average cost of a credential-compromise incident at [$Y] per event, and to the regulatory exposure under [HIPAA / PCI-DSS / GDPR / applicable framework] for failing to demonstrate staff security training. We are requesting approval to proceed with [Platform / LMS selection] and a dedicated [0.25 / 0.5 / 1.0] FTE for program coordination."
  3. Assemble the complete program charter by combining the outputs of all five phases into a single document. The charter is the artifact you hand to a new CISO, an auditor, or an executive sponsor and they can understand the entire program in under 10 minutes.
    PROGRAM CHARTER TABLE OF CONTENTS 1. Executive Summary (1 page) -- from Phase 1 needs-assessment statement + Phase 5 leadership pitch 2. Audience Segmentation Table (Phase 1) 3. Topic-by-Segment Matrix (Phase 2) 4. Learning Objectives per Module (Phase 2) 5. Phishing Simulation Plan (Phase 3) 5a. Authorization Record 5b. Annual Cadence & Difficulty Ramp 5c. Clicked-the-Link Response Flow 5d. Non-Punitive Policy Statement (signed by CISO + HR) 6. Metrics Definition Table (Phase 4) 7. Month 1 Baseline Sprint Plan (Phase 4) 8. Quarterly Dashboard Template (Phase 4) 9. 12-Month Content Calendar (Phase 5) 10. Budget Request Summary (Phase 5) Appendix A: Platform / LMS evaluation notes Appendix B: Regulatory and policy references (NIST SP 800-50 Rev. 1 (2024), company policy #X)
Common mistake — training as a once-a-year compliance checkbox: A program that deploys one mandatory annual module satisfies the letter of many compliance frameworks but produces near-zero behavioral change. The research on security awareness consistently shows that single-event training degrades to baseline within 30–90 days (the "forgetting curve"). A monthly cadence of short, relevant content maintains readiness. If the program budget only allows one annual touchpoint, spend the first planning cycle making the case for monthly micro-content — which costs a fraction of one annual 2-hour session because it can be produced internally and distributed via existing channels (intranet, screensaver, email newsletter).

  • A leadership pitch that ties the program to measurable risk reduction in business language
