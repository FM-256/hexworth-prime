Subject

Write an Incident Response Plan

Operator 2 hr 1,000 XP
Mission Brief

When — not if — an incident hits, the difference between control and chaos is a plan written in advance. You will author an incident response plan following NIST SP 800-61: the response team and authority, a severity classification scheme, the full IR lifecycle, a communication plan, and one concrete runbook.

A plan written during a breach is written too late. This is the document that lets a tired on-call act decisively at 3 a.m. instead of guessing.

By the end you will have a real IR plan and at least one runbook ready to exercise.

Agent Requirements
  • Basic understanding of common incidents (ransomware, phishing/BEC, data breach)
  • NIST SP 800-61 as reference
  • A document
  • "Build a Security Awareness Program" pairs well with this

Define the Team & Authority
An IR plan with no named decision-maker is a liability document, not a response tool. This phase produces the first section of your plan: a roles table, an authority statement, and an on-call contact list. Every step below maps directly to NIST SP 800-61 Section 2.4 (Incident Response Team Structure).
  1. Open your document and create a top-level section titled Section 1 — Incident Response Team & Authority. Add a one-sentence scope statement that names the organization and the systems this plan covers.
    INCIDENT RESPONSE PLAN Organization: [Your Organization Name] Document Owner: [CISO / Security Manager name and title] Version: 1.0 | Effective Date: [YYYY-MM-DD] Review Cycle: Annual or after any declared incident SCOPE This plan governs the detection, containment, eradication, and recovery from cybersecurity incidents affecting information systems owned or operated by [Organization Name], including on-premises infrastructure, cloud workloads (AWS us-east-1, Azure East US), and SaaS platforms used in production.
  2. Build the Roles Table. Copy the template below and fill in your organization's actual names, titles, and 24/7 contact numbers. Every role must have a primary and a backup.
    ROLES & RESPONSIBILITIES --------------------------------------------------------------- ROLE | PRIMARY | BACKUP --------------------------------------------------------------- Incident Commander| [Name, Title] | [Name, Title] | [Phone] [Email] | [Phone] [Email] | Authority: Declares | | incidents; approves | | containment actions; | | is the single point | | of command. | --------------------------------------------------------------- Technical Lead | [Name, Title] | [Name, Title] | [Phone] [Email] | [Phone] [Email] | Authority: Directs | | forensic analysis, | | containment steps, | | and system recovery. | --------------------------------------------------------------- Communications / | [Name, Title] | [Name, Title] Legal Liaison | [Phone] [Email] | [Counsel Phone] | Authority: Approves | | all external | | statements; triggers | | legal hold; advises | | on notification law. | --------------------------------------------------------------- Scribe | [Name, Title] | [Name, Title] | [Phone] [Email] | [Phone] [Email] | Authority: Maintains | | the incident log and | | chain of custody | | documentation. | ---------------------------------------------------------------
    Why four roles? NIST SP 800-61 Section 2.4.3 describes IR team roles and organizational models. This plan operationalizes those descriptions into four functions (IC / Tech Lead / Comms-Legal / Scribe) — a practical minimum for most organizations, not a specific roster mandated by NIST. The Incident Commander role is the most commonly missing in real plans — without it, containment decisions stall because no single person has authority to pull the trigger.
  3. Write the Authority Statement. This is the single most important paragraph in your plan. Copy and adapt:
    AUTHORITY STATEMENT The Incident Commander is the sole authority to: (a) formally declare a cybersecurity incident, (b) authorize isolation or shutdown of production systems, (c) engage external IR retainer (vendor: [Retainer Name], contact: [24/7 hotline]), (d) authorize public or regulatory notification. If the Incident Commander is unreachable for more than 30 minutes, authority passes to the Technical Lead. If both are unreachable, the [CISO / CTO / CEO] assumes command. No containment action affecting production availability may be taken without Incident Commander approval except in cases of active, confirmed ransomware propagation, in which case the Technical Lead may isolate affected segments immediately and notify the Incident Commander within 15 minutes.
  4. Add the Out-of-Band Contact Protocol. If your primary communication channel (email, Slack, Teams) is compromised during an incident, the team needs a pre-agreed fallback. Write it now.
    OUT-OF-BAND COMMUNICATIONS Primary channel: [Slack #ir-response or Teams channel] Secondary channel: [Signal group "IR Team" - all 4 roles added] Tertiary channel: Direct mobile call to Incident Commander If corporate email is suspected as compromised or unavailable, all IR communications shift immediately to the secondary channel. Do NOT send incident details via corporate email until forensics confirms email infrastructure is unaffected.
    Why plan this here? In a BEC (Business Email Compromise) or Exchange Server attack, the attackers are reading your email. Sending containment plans over a channel the attacker controls is catastrophic. Pre-agreed out-of-band comms are explicitly recommended in NIST SP 800-61 Section 3.2.7 (Incident Notification).
GRC Trap: No clear decision authority. The single most common IR plan failure is a list of roles with no authority statement. When ransomware hits at 2 a.m. and the on-call engineer asks "can I cut this server off the network?", the answer "check with your manager" is not a plan. Fix: the Authority Statement in Step 3 must be explicit about who can say yes, what the threshold is, and what happens if that person is unreachable. If you cannot name the person today, the plan is not done.
GRC Trap: Single contact per role. People travel, get sick, and lose phones. Every role in the table needs a named backup with working contact details. Verify the phone numbers are mobile numbers reachable 24/7, not desk phones at an empty office.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • An IR roles section with four roles, primary and backup contacts for each
  • An explicit statement of who declares an incident and who authorizes containment actions, with a succession chain if the primary is unreachable

Build the Severity Classification
Without classification criteria, every incident is treated as SEV1, which burns the team out and trains leadership to ignore alerts. This phase produces Section 2 of your plan: a four-tier severity matrix with objective criteria, response-time targets, and the triage questions that assign a severity at first notice. Grounded in NIST SP 800-61 Section 3.2.6 (Incident Prioritization).
  1. Create Section 2 — Severity Classification. Start with a one-paragraph explanation of how severity drives resource allocation, then paste and fill the matrix below.
    SECTION 2 — SEVERITY CLASSIFICATION Severity is assigned at first report and may be upgraded or downgraded as analysis reveals new information. The Incident Commander makes the final severity call. All times below are measured from the moment the incident is first reported. SEVERITY MATRIX --------------------------------------------------------------- SEV | LABEL | CRITERIA | RESPONSE TARGET --------------------------------------------------------------- 1 | CRITICAL | Active data exfiltration in | Incident | | progress; ransomware | Commander paged | | confirmed spreading on | immediately; | | production network; | all-hands in | | complete loss of a | 15 minutes; | | business-critical service | status update | | (payment processing, | every 30 min. | | patient records, 911 | | | dispatch); external | | | attacker with confirmed | | | root on production systems. | --------------------------------------------------------------- 2 | HIGH | Suspected breach under | Incident | | investigation; single | Commander | | system compromised with | notified in | | lateral movement possible; | 30 minutes; | | significant service | Technical Lead | | degradation affecting | engaged; | | external customers; | status update | | malware detected and | every 60 min. | | contained but not yet | | | eradicated. | --------------------------------------------------------------- 3 | MEDIUM | Malware quarantined on | Technical Lead | | isolated workstation; | notified in | | phishing email opened but | 2 hours; | | no credentials harvested; | normal business | | vulnerability exploited on | hours response; | | non-production system; | daily status | | failed brute-force with | update. | | account lockout triggered. | --------------------------------------------------------------- 4 | LOW | Phishing email reported | Ticket created; | | (not opened); failed | handled in | | external port scan with no | next business | | impact; policy violation | day; weekly | | (USB drive use) with no | batch review. | | data exfiltration. | ---------------------------------------------------------------
  2. Add the First-Triage Decision Questions. These five questions are asked at initial report to assign severity before full analysis is complete.
    FIRST-TRIAGE QUESTIONS (ask at initial report) --------------------------------------------------------------- Q1. Is data actively leaving the network right now? YES to Q1 = minimum SEV2; escalate to SEV1 if confirmed. Q2. Are production systems unavailable or degraded? YES to Q2 = minimum SEV2. Q3. Is malware confirmed on the network (not just email)? YES to Q3 = minimum SEV2; SEV1 if lateral movement seen. Q4. Could a third party (customer, patient, partner) be harmed? YES to Q4 = minimum SEV2; legal/comms notified immediately. Q5. Is this isolated to a single endpoint with no network reach? YES to Q5 and NO to Q1-Q4 = SEV3 or SEV4. If none of Q1-Q4 triggers: assign SEV3. If all activity is in email with no payload execution: SEV4. When in doubt, assign one severity higher and downgrade later.
    The last rule is critical. It is always safer to escalate and stand down than to under-classify and under-respond. A SEV1 that was downgraded to SEV3 after 20 minutes costs a 20-minute page. A SEV3 that should have been SEV1 can cost millions in dwell time.
  3. Add the Escalation Trigger Table. Conditions that automatically upgrade severity mid-incident.
    AUTOMATIC SEVERITY ESCALATION TRIGGERS (upgrade without waiting for Incident Commander approval) - Confirmed lateral movement to a second system: +1 level - PII, PHI, PCI, or credentials confirmed exfiltrated: SEV1 - Attacker persistence confirmed (scheduled task, backdoor): +1 level - External media outlet or customer reports breach before internal detection: SEV1 - Law enforcement contacts the organization about an incident: SEV1; legal counsel notified immediately
GRC Trap: No severity criteria (everything becomes SEV1). Without a matrix, stressed analysts always call everything critical. The result: leadership gets paged for a phishing test email at 3 a.m., stops responding to pages, and then misses a real SEV1. Fix: the triage questions in Step 2 must be answerable in under two minutes at first report. If any question requires analysis to answer, the classification is too complex for triage — simplify it.
GRC Trap: Response times with no owner. "SEV1 responds in 15 minutes" means nothing without naming who must respond and what "respond" means (paged? on a call? actively working?). Your matrix must say "Incident Commander paged, Technical Lead online, status bridge open" — actions, not abstractions.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • A severity table with four levels (SEV1–SEV4), specific criteria for each, and named response actions and time targets
  • Specific enough that two different team members, given the same scenario, would independently classify it the same way using the triage questions

Document the IR Lifecycle
NIST SP 800-61 defines four phases: Preparation; Detection & Analysis; Containment, Eradication & Recovery; and Post-Incident Activity. This phase produces Section 3 of your plan: a per-phase runbook checklist with concrete actions and explicit "done when" exit criteria. A phase without an exit criterion is a phase you can never leave.
Version note: This lifecycle follows NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2 (August 2012), which is the version taught in most current certification curricula (Security+, CySA+, CISSP). r2 was officially withdrawn April 3, 2025 and superseded by SP 800-61 Revision 3, which reframes IR as a CSF 2.0 Community Profile. The four-phase r2 lifecycle remains widely used and practically sound; if you are applying this plan professionally, verify alignment with r3 and your organization's CSF 2.0 implementation.
  1. Create Section 3 — IR Lifecycle. Add an intro paragraph, then document each NIST phase using the template structure below. Copy each phase block and fill in your organization's specifics.
    SECTION 3 — IR LIFECYCLE (NIST SP 800-61r2) This section follows the four NIST phases. Each phase includes the entry condition (when we enter it), the required actions, and the exit criterion (when we can leave it). Phases may overlap; Preparation is ongoing. ================================================================= PHASE 1 — PREPARATION ================================================================= Entry: Always active. This is the pre-incident state. Purpose: Ensure the team, tools, and authority are ready before an incident occurs. No amount of technical skill compensates for a team that has never practiced the plan. Actions: [ ] IR plan reviewed and approved annually (last review: ___) [ ] IR team trained on this plan (tabletop date: ___) [ ] IR retainer contract in place (vendor: ___, SOW on file) [ ] SIEM / EDR alerts tuned and monitored 24/7 [ ] Asset inventory current (systems in scope documented) [ ] Backup integrity verified (last test restore: ___) [ ] Legal hold process documented with counsel [ ] Forensic toolkit pre-staged: - Disk imaging: [e.g., FTK Imager, dd] - Memory capture: [e.g., Magnet RAM Capture, LiME] - Log aggregation: [e.g., Splunk, Elastic] [ ] Communication channels tested (primary + out-of-band) [ ] Contact list verified (all phone numbers dialed, working) Exit criterion: Preparation has no exit; it is re-evaluated after every declared incident and annually on [calendar date]. ================================================================= PHASE 2 — DETECTION & ANALYSIS ================================================================= Entry: A potential incident is reported or detected. Purpose: Determine whether an event is an incident, assign severity, and gather enough information to begin containment. Detection Sources (document yours): - SIEM alert (tool: ___, monitored by: ___) - EDR alert (tool: ___, monitored by: ___) - Employee report (to: helpdesk@[org] or [phone]) - Threat intelligence feed (source: ___) - External report (customer, partner, law enforcement) Triage Actions (complete in order): [ ] Record the time and method of initial detection in the incident log (Scribe opens log immediately) [ ] Apply the five first-triage questions from Section 2 [ ] Assign initial severity (SEV1–SEV4) [ ] Notify the Incident Commander (SEV1/2) or Technical Lead (SEV3) within the time targets in Section 2 [ ] Preserve initial evidence: take screenshots of alerts, export SIEM query results, do NOT reboot suspected systems [ ] Identify affected systems by hostname, IP, and owner [ ] Determine incident category: - Malware / ransomware - Unauthorized access - Denial of service - Data breach / exfiltration - Insider threat - Physical / supply chain Analysis Actions: [ ] Collect and correlate logs (firewall, EDR, authentication) [ ] Identify attack vector (phishing, exposed service, creds) [ ] Determine scope: how many systems, accounts, data types? [ ] Confirm or rule out active exfiltration [ ] Document all findings in the incident log with timestamps Exit criterion: The incident is declared (Incident Commander signs off), severity is assigned, scope is documented, and the team has enough information to begin containment. ================================================================= PHASE 3 — CONTAINMENT, ERADICATION & RECOVERY ================================================================= Entry: Incident declared; scope known well enough to act. Purpose: Stop the bleeding (containment), remove the threat (eradication), and restore systems to normal operation (recovery) — in that order. Short-Term Containment (immediate, may impact availability): [ ] Isolate affected systems from the network Method: [VLAN isolation / firewall ACL / physical unplug] Approval required: Incident Commander [ ] Disable compromised accounts in Active Directory / IdP Method: [Disable in AD / revoke SSO session tokens] [ ] Block attacker IP ranges at perimeter firewall [ ] Preserve system state BEFORE reimaging: - Capture memory image (do not reboot first) - Take forensic disk image - Export authentication logs Long-Term Containment (stable while eradication proceeds): [ ] Deploy clean, patched replacement systems if needed [ ] Apply emergency patches to close the exploited vector [ ] Reset passwords for all accounts with access to affected systems (force re-auth on all sessions) [ ] Increase logging verbosity on adjacent systems Eradication: [ ] Identify and remove all malware artifacts (scheduled tasks, registry run keys, startup scripts, rogue admin accounts, backdoors, webshells) [ ] Patch or remediate the root-cause vulnerability [ ] Scan adjacent systems for signs of lateral movement [ ] Verify clean state: re-run EDR scan, review logs Recovery: [ ] Restore systems from known-good backup (backup date: ___) [ ] Validate backup integrity before restoring to production [ ] Monitor restored systems for 48–72 hours [ ] Confirm with business owner that functionality is normal [ ] Obtain Incident Commander sign-off before closing Exit criterion: All malware removed (confirmed by EDR clean scan), exploited vector patched, systems restored from clean backup, and business owner confirms normal operation. Incident Commander signs "Recovery Complete" in the incident log. ================================================================= PHASE 4 — POST-INCIDENT ACTIVITY ================================================================= Entry: Recovery Complete signed off by Incident Commander. Purpose: Learn from the incident, close documentation, and make the organization harder to breach the next time. Actions: [ ] Hold After-Action Review (AAR) promptly after recovery [Recommend within 5 business days — this is an organizational target; NIST 800-61 says "within several days"; choose a window that fits your ops tempo and document it as your standard] Attendees: all four IR roles + affected system owners Agenda: timeline reconstruction, what worked, what failed, root cause, prevention [ ] Produce written AAR report (owner: Incident Commander) [ ] Update this IR plan with lessons learned [ ] File all evidence per retention policy (minimum 3 years per NARA GRS 24 guidance; note GRS schedules bind U.S. federal agencies — private-sector orgs should confirm retention with legal counsel based on applicable regulations, litigation holds, and organizational policy) [ ] Update the contact list if anyone left the organization [ ] Brief leadership on findings and prevention investments Exit criterion: AAR report signed, this plan updated, evidence archived, and date set for next tabletop exercise. =================================================================
  2. Review your draft for coverage. Every phase must have at least one action you can do right now, today, in your actual environment. If any action says "use [TOOL]" and you do not have that tool, replace it with the tool you actually have or note it as a gap to close.
GRC Trap: No "recovered" definition. Without an exit criterion for Recovery, incidents never officially close. The team keeps investigating forever, or someone quietly stops logging and calls it done. Fix: the Recovery exit criterion must be a signed-off checklist item, not a subjective "we think it's clean." A second EDR scan and a business owner confirmation are the minimum bar.
GRC Trap: Skipping memory capture before reimaging. The most common evidence-destruction mistake in IR is rebooting or reimaging a suspected system immediately. Malware in memory, active network connections, and running process lists disappear on reboot. Your containment phase must explicitly say: capture memory, then image disk, then act. If you do not have memory-capture tools today, add procurement to your Preparation checklist.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • All four NIST phases documented with concrete, checkable actions
  • Each phase has an explicit entry condition and "done when" exit criterion — not just a phase name

Write the Communication Plan
Communication failures during incidents cause as much damage as the breach itself: premature public disclosure, missed regulatory deadlines, and statements that create legal liability. This phase produces Section 4 of your plan: a communications matrix, a holding-statement template, legal and regulatory notification triggers, and the evidence-preservation rule. Grounded in NIST SP 800-61 Section 3.2.7 (Notifying the Appropriate Organizations) and common regulatory frameworks (GDPR 72-hour, HIPAA 60-day, SEC 4-business-day post-materiality-determination).
  1. Create Section 4 — Communication Plan. Start with the internal communications matrix. Every row is a stakeholder group; every column answers: who tells them, what do we say, and when.

  2. Write the Holding Statement Template. A holding statement is the only approved external statement before Legal and Comms have coordinated. It acknowledges the situation without admitting scope or cause.
    HOLDING STATEMENT TEMPLATE (approved for use before Legal/Comms sign-off on full statement) "[Organization Name] is aware of a cybersecurity incident affecting [general system/service description, e.g., 'some of our internal systems']. We have activated our incident response team and are working to investigate and address the situation. The security and privacy of those we serve is our top priority. We will provide updates as our investigation progresses. If you have questions, please contact: [designated point of contact name, phone, email]." WHAT THIS STATEMENT DOES NOT SAY: - It does not confirm data was stolen - It does not name the attack type or attacker - It does not give a timeline for resolution - It does not make promises about future impact This statement may be used verbatim for press, partner, and customer inquiries until the full statement is approved by Legal and the Communications Liaison.
    Why this matters. In a real incident, reporters, partners, and customers will ask questions before you have answers. An improvised response to a journalist from a stressed engineer has caused multi-million-dollar legal liability. The holding statement is pre-approved, legally reviewed, and available immediately.
  3. Write the Evidence Preservation Rule. Paste this as a standalone subsection titled "Evidence Handling and Chain of Custody."
    EVIDENCE HANDLING & CHAIN OF CUSTODY THE GOLDEN RULE: Do not alter, wipe, or reimage any system involved in a declared incident until the Technical Lead explicitly authorizes it. Evidence destroyed before capture cannot be recovered. For each piece of evidence collected, the Scribe records: Evidence Item #: [sequential number] Description: [system hostname, log file, storage media] Collected by: [Name] Collection time: [YYYY-MM-DD HH:MM UTC] Collection method: [FTK Imager v4.7 / dd if=/dev/sda / export] Hash (SHA-256): [hash value - computed immediately after capture] Storage location: [encrypted drive / case folder path] Chain of custody: [list everyone who accessed this item + time] Evidence is retained for a minimum of 3 years for all declared incidents, per NARA General Records Schedule (GRS) 24 guidance for IT security records. Note: GRS schedules are issued by the National Archives and Records Administration (NARA) and bind U.S. federal agencies; they do not directly govern private-sector organizations. Private-sector orgs should set retention based on applicable regulations (e.g., HIPAA, PCI-DSS, state law), litigation-hold requirements, and legal counsel guidance. Three years is a reasonable default starting point, but confirm with counsel. For incidents involving potential criminal activity, regulatory notification, or litigation hold, retain until counsel confirms it is safe to destroy. Legal Hold: When Legal Counsel is engaged, a legal hold notice is issued immediately. Under legal hold, no evidence may be destroyed regardless of normal retention schedules.
GRC Trap: No out-of-band comms plan. If an attacker compromised your email server or Slack workspace, your IR team is coordinating via the channel the attacker is reading. This is not hypothetical — it happened in multiple high-profile Exchange Server and Microsoft 365 compromises. You built your Out-of-Band Contact Protocol in Phase 1, Step 4 — verify here that it covers: (a) a Signal group or equivalent with all four IR roles enrolled, (b) a tested conference bridge number, and (c) that the channel is documented in the communications matrix above. If Phase 1 Step 4 is complete, you do not need to recreate it here — reference it by name in Section 4 so any reader can find it quickly.
GRC Trap: Missing legal and regulatory notification triggers. Regulatory clocks start earlier than most plans assume. GDPR's 72-hour window runs from awareness of the breach. The SEC's 4-business-day window (Form 8-K Item 1.05) runs from the date the company determines the incident is material — not from the date it first becomes aware; a company can be aware of an incident for weeks before completing a materiality determination, but the moment that determination is made, the clock is running. HIPAA's 60-day individual-notification window runs from discovery, regardless of breach size. If your plan says "notify regulators after the investigation is complete," you will miss every deadline. Fix: the external communications matrix must list the clock-start trigger and deadline for each applicable regulation, and Legal must be notified at SEV1 declaration, not at recovery.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • A communications matrix (who / what / when) covering both internal and external stakeholders, with named owners and trigger conditions
  • A holding-statement template approved for immediate use without further sign-off
  • An explicit evidence-preservation rule with chain-of-custody documentation format

Write One Runbook
A runbook translates the IR plan's generic lifecycle into a scenario-specific checklist a tired on-call can follow at 3 a.m. without making judgment calls. This phase produces Appendix A of your plan: a complete ransomware runbook. It references your Sections 1–4 throughout, tying the runbook to the authority structure, severity matrix, and communications plan you already wrote. Grounded in NIST SP 800-61 Section 3.3 (Containment, Eradication, and Recovery).
  1. Add Appendix A — Ransomware Runbook to your document. Begin with the trigger and initial conditions.
    APPENDIX A — RANSOMWARE RUNBOOK TRIGGER: This runbook activates when any of the following occur: - EDR alert: ransomware family detected (e.g., LockBit, ALPHV/BlackCat, Cl0p, Black Basta) - Files on a network share renamed with an unknown extension (e.g., .locked, .encrypted, .crypt) - Ransom note file (README.txt, HOW_TO_DECRYPT.txt, etc.) appears on a file server or workstation desktop - User reports "all my files are gone / replaced" INITIAL SEVERITY: SEV2 until lateral movement confirmed, then automatically escalate to SEV1 (see Section 2). OWNER: Technical Lead (page Incident Commander within 15 min)
  2. Write the numbered containment steps. These must be granular enough for a non-expert to execute.
    PHASE 1 — IMMEDIATE CONTAINMENT (first 30 minutes) All steps require Incident Commander approval unless marked [AUTO]. Step 1. STOP THE SPREAD [AUTO - no approval needed] Physically unplug the network cable or disable the Wi-Fi adapter on the first confirmed infected system. Do NOT shut it down — memory evidence will be lost. Confirm isolation: the system should no longer appear active in your network monitoring tool. Step 2. PAGE THE INCIDENT COMMANDER Use the out-of-band channel from Section 1. Say: "Ransomware confirmed on [HOSTNAME]. Isolated. SEV2 declared. Request you join IR bridge now." Bridge number / Signal group: _______________ Step 3. OPEN THE INCIDENT LOG Scribe: open the incident log template and record: - Detection time (UTC): _______________ - Detected by: _______________ - First affected system: [HOSTNAME / IP] - Detection method: [EDR alert / user report / other] - Ransom note filename (if visible): _______________ Step 4. IDENTIFY THE BLAST RADIUS Do NOT touch the infected system yet. From a clean analyst workstation, query your SIEM or EDR console: - Which systems show the same process execution pattern? - Which network shares are accessible from the infected host? - Which accounts were logged into the infected host in the last 24 hours? (These accounts are likely compromised.) Document findings in the incident log. DECISION POINT A: If lateral movement confirmed to 2+ systems: escalate to SEV1, page all IR team roles, activate IR retainer. If contained to single system: maintain SEV2, continue. Step 5. ISOLATE ADDITIONAL SYSTEMS For each system identified in Step 4 as likely compromised: - Isolate at firewall (block all traffic from host IP) OR - Move to quarantine VLAN - Approval: Incident Commander must confirm each isolation Record isolated systems in incident log with timestamp. Step 6. DISABLE COMPROMISED ACCOUNTS Disable every Active Directory / IdP account that was logged into any infected system in the last 72 hours. Method: [Disable in AD / revoke OAuth tokens in Okta] Notify account owners via phone (not email — out-of-band). Record disabled accounts in incident log. Step 7. CAPTURE FORENSIC EVIDENCE Before any reimaging occurs, on EACH isolated system: a. Capture memory image: [Tool: Magnet RAM Capture / LiME / WinPMEM] Save to encrypted forensic drive. b. Capture disk image: [Tool: FTK Imager / dd if=/dev/sda of=/mnt/evidence/hostname.img] c. Compute SHA-256 hash of disk image immediately. d. Record evidence item in chain-of-custody log (Section 4). Step 8. NOTIFY LEGAL AND CYBER INSURANCE Comms Liaison contacts Legal Counsel (phone, not email). IC contacts cyber insurance carrier within 2 hours of SEV1. Do NOT pay any ransom without Legal and insurance sign-off.
  3. Add the eradication and recovery steps.
    PHASE 2 — ERADICATION (after containment confirmed) Step 9. IDENTIFY THE ENTRY POINT Review firewall, VPN, and authentication logs for the 72 hours before first detection. Look for: - Unusual external RDP or VPN connections - Phishing email with attachment opened (check mail gateway) - Exploitation of internet-facing service Document the confirmed or probable entry vector in the log. Step 10. BLOCK THE ENTRY VECTOR Patch exploited software, disable the exposed service, or revoke the compromised credentials. The patch must be applied before any recovery begins — restoring to a vulnerable system means ransomware round two. Step 11. REMOVE MALWARE ARTIFACTS Run a full EDR scan on ALL systems (not just confirmed-infected). Manually check: - Scheduled tasks (Task Scheduler / cron) - Registry Run keys (HKLM\SOFTWARE\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Run) - Services installed since [date of suspected compromise] - Admin accounts created since [date] - Outbound firewall rules added since [date] Remove every artifact found. Re-scan after removal. DECISION POINT B: If EDR scan is clean on all systems: proceed to recovery. If any system still shows indicators: re-isolate and repeat Steps 10–11 before proceeding. PHASE 3 — RECOVERY Step 12. RESTORE FROM BACKUP Restore affected systems from the most recent clean backup (backup taken before the suspected compromise date: _______). Confirm backup integrity BEFORE restoring to production: - Compare backup hash to recorded value - Test restore to an isolated environment first Approval required: Incident Commander + business owner. Step 13. VALIDATE RECOVERY After restore, monitor for 48–72 hours: - EDR running and reporting clean - No unusual outbound connections (check firewall logs) - No re-encryption events - Business owner confirms data integrity and functionality Step 14. CLOSE THE INCIDENT Incident Commander signs "Recovery Complete" in incident log.
