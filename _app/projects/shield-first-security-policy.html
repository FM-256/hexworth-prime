Subject

Write Your First Security Policy

Recruit 45 min 250 XP
Mission Brief

Every security program rests on policy — the written rules that say what is allowed, who is responsible, and what happens when the rules are broken. You will draft two foundational policies from scratch, an Acceptable Use Policy and a Password Policy, and learn the anatomy every real policy shares.

Policy is the document a GRC analyst lives in and the artifact an auditor asks for first. Write it clearly enough that a new hire understands it and specifically enough that you can tell when it has been violated.

By the end you will have two real, enforceable policies and understand the structure, roles, and review cycle behind every governance document you will ever write.

Agent Requirements
  • A word processor or plain text editor
  • Willingness to write clearly and specifically
  • A real or fictional organization to scope the policies to
  • No prior security background required

Learn the Anatomy of a Policy
Every well-formed security policy contains the same eight sections regardless of topic. Auditors check for these sections first. Missing even one — especially enforcement or review date — is an automatic finding under ISO/IEC 27001 Annex A and NIST SP 800-53. This phase maps the skeleton before you write a single word of actual policy.
  1. Learn what each section does. A policy is a directive, not a description. Every section below serves a specific governance function — skip any one and the policy breaks in a predictable place.
    Purpose — one sentence stating WHY the policy exists and what risk it addresses. Without this, no one knows whether a situation is in scope.

    Scope — exactly who and what the policy covers: which people, which systems, which locations. Ambiguous scope is the most common audit finding because it lets anyone argue they are exempt.

    Definitions — terms that carry specific meaning in this document. If you write "sensitive data" you must define it here, or enforcement becomes subjective.

    Policy Statements — the actual rules, written with shall or must language. This is the heart of the document.

    Roles and Responsibilities — who owns the policy, who enforces it, and what employees are required to do. Ownership without accountability is theater.

    Exceptions — the formal process for requesting a temporary waiver. Every real policy needs one or people work around the policy in silence.

    Enforcement and Consequences — what happens when the policy is violated. Without stated consequences, the policy has no teeth under ISO 27001 A.6.4 (Disciplinary process).

    Review Cadence and Version Control — how often the policy is reviewed, who approves changes, and the current version number and approval date.
  2. Create the policy skeleton. Copy this template into your word processor. Every policy you write this week starts from this structure. Replace the bracketed values with your own organization name and details.
    POLICY TITLE: [Your Policy Name] Policy Number: [e.g., SEC-001] Version: 1.0 Effective Date: [YYYY-MM-DD] Last Reviewed: [YYYY-MM-DD] Next Review Date: [YYYY-MM-DD] Policy Owner: [Job title, e.g., Chief Information Security Officer] Approved By: [Job title, e.g., Chief Executive Officer] Classification: Internal --- 1. PURPOSE [One to two sentences. State the risk this policy addresses and why the organization has chosen to address it with a formal policy.] 2. SCOPE [State exactly who this policy applies to. Name roles, employee categories, contractors, systems, and any geographic limitations. "All employees" is acceptable if that is accurate; be explicit.] 3. DEFINITIONS [List any term that has a specific meaning in this document. Format: TERM -- definition.] 4. POLICY STATEMENTS [Numbered rules. Each rule uses "shall" or "must" language and is specific enough that a manager could decide objectively whether it was violated.] 5. ROLES AND RESPONSIBILITIES [For each role listed, state what they are required to do or ensure.] 6. EXCEPTIONS [State the process for requesting a waiver: who submits the request, who approves it, how long a waiver lasts, and how it is documented.] 7. ENFORCEMENT AND CONSEQUENCES [State what happens when the policy is violated. Reference the disciplinary process. Be specific about severity levels if applicable.] 8. REVIEW CADENCE AND VERSION HISTORY [State how often this policy is reviewed. List prior versions in a table: Version | Date | Author | Summary of Changes]
    ISO/IEC 27002:2022 alignment: This structure satisfies the policy documentation requirements under clause 5.1 (Policies for information security). That same clause requires that policies be reviewed at planned intervals, approved by authorized management, and maintained with version history — which is exactly what the version table and approval fields above implement.
  3. Read one real policy to calibrate your sense of specificity. The SANS Institute publishes a free library of policy templates at sans.org/information-security-policy/. Open the Acceptable Use Policy template. Notice that each rule is a complete, unambiguous sentence. A vague rule like "use systems responsibly" does not appear. Every rule states a specific prohibited or required behavior. Keep this standard in mind for the phases ahead.
Common trap: confusing a policy with a procedure. A policy states WHAT must happen and WHY. A procedure states HOW to do it. If you catch yourself writing numbered technical steps ("click Start, then open Control Panel..."), you are writing a procedure, not a policy. Policies are technology-neutral and do not specify implementation steps. Keep the two documents separate.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • You can name the standard policy sections and point to each one in a sample policy
  • You have a blank policy skeleton saved in your word processor with all eight section headings

Draft an Acceptable Use Policy
An Acceptable Use Policy (AUP) is typically the first policy a new employee signs. It defines what users may and may not do with company systems — email, web, devices, data, and personal devices brought to work. The test for every rule you write: could a manager read this sentence and decide objectively whether it was violated? If not, rewrite it.
  1. Fill in the header block. Use your skeleton from Phase 1. Below is a worked example for a fictional company called Meridian Financial. Use this as your model — adapt every value to your own organization.
    POLICY TITLE: Acceptable Use Policy Policy Number: SEC-001 Version: 1.0 Effective Date: 2026-07-01 Last Reviewed: 2026-06-19 Next Review Date: 2027-06-19 Policy Owner: Chief Information Security Officer Approved By: Chief Executive Officer Classification: Internal
  2. Write the Purpose section. One to two sentences only. State the specific risk this policy addresses.
    1. PURPOSE This policy establishes the conditions under which Meridian Financial employees, contractors, and third-party vendors may use company-owned or company-managed information systems. Its purpose is to protect the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of Meridian Financial data and to reduce the risk of unauthorized access, data loss, and legal liability arising from misuse of company resources.
    Why this works: it names the specific risk (unauthorized access, data loss, legal liability) rather than saying something generic like "to keep our systems safe." An auditor can match this purpose against the company's risk register.
  3. Write the Scope section. Be explicit about who is covered. Gaps here are exploited in disciplinary disputes.
    2. SCOPE This policy applies to: - All full-time and part-time Meridian Financial employees - Contractors and temporary workers with access to company systems - Third-party vendors granted access to Meridian Financial networks or data under a signed agreement - All company-owned, company-leased, and personally owned devices (BYOD) used to access Meridian Financial systems or data - All geographic locations, including remote work environments This policy applies regardless of whether access occurs on-site or remotely, and regardless of the device or connection method used.
  4. Write the Definitions section. Define every term that could be disputed.
    3. DEFINITIONS COMPANY SYSTEMS -- any hardware, software, network, or cloud service owned, leased, or operated by Meridian Financial, including but not limited to laptops, mobile devices, email, VPN, cloud storage, and business applications. SENSITIVE DATA -- any data classified as Confidential or Restricted under the Meridian Financial Data Classification Policy (SEC-002), including but not limited to customer personally identifiable information (PII), financial records, and authentication credentials. BYOD (Bring Your Own Device) -- a personally owned device used by an employee or contractor to access Meridian Financial systems or data. AUTHORIZED USE -- use of company systems for legitimate Meridian Financial business purposes within the boundaries defined in this policy.
  5. Write the Policy Statements. This is the most important section. Every statement must be specific and testable. Copy this worked example and then extend it with rules for your own organization.
    4. POLICY STATEMENTS 4.1 ACCEPTABLE USE 4.1.1 Employees shall use company systems primarily for authorized Meridian Financial business purposes. 4.1.2 Incidental personal use of company email and internet access is permitted provided it does not interfere with work duties, consume excessive bandwidth, or violate any provision of this policy. 4.2 PROHIBITED USE -- the following activities are strictly prohibited: 4.2.1 Employees shall not use company systems to access, download, store, or distribute material that is illegal, obscene, harassing, or discriminatory. 4.2.2 Employees shall not use company systems to send unsolicited bulk email (spam) or conduct any activity that constitutes a violation of the CAN-SPAM Act or applicable anti-spam regulations. 4.2.3 Employees shall not install software on company devices without prior written approval from the IT Department. 4.2.4 Employees shall not attempt to circumvent, disable, or bypass any security control, monitoring system, or access restriction on company systems. 4.2.5 Employees shall not share their authentication credentials (usernames, passwords, MFA tokens) with any other person, including colleagues and IT support staff. 4.2.6 Employees shall not connect unauthorized external storage devices (USB drives, external hard drives) to company systems without prior written approval from the IT Department. 4.3 DATA HANDLING 4.3.1 Employees shall not transmit Sensitive Data to personal email accounts, personal cloud storage, or any system not approved by the IT Department. 4.3.2 Employees shall not store Sensitive Data on personally owned devices unless written authorization has been granted by the CISO and the device is enrolled in Meridian Financial's mobile device management (MDM) platform. 4.4 BYOD 4.4.1 Employees who access Meridian Financial systems from a personally owned device must enroll that device in the company MDM platform prior to first access. 4.4.2 Upon separation from Meridian Financial, the employee consents to remote wipe of company data from any enrolled personal device. 4.5 MONITORING AND CONSENT 4.5.1 Meridian Financial reserves the right to monitor, log, and audit all activity on company systems. Users have no expectation of privacy when using company systems or when accessing Meridian Financial systems from any device. 4.5.2 Accessing company systems constitutes consent to this monitoring.
    Why "shall" and "must" matter: modal verbs create legal obligation. "Should" is a recommendation. "Shall" is a requirement. NIST SP 800-53 uses this distinction throughout. Auditors look for modal verb consistency — a policy that mixes "should" and "shall" for the same type of rule is internally inconsistent and weakens enforceability.
  6. Write the Enforcement and Consequences section. Without this, the policy has no teeth.
    7. ENFORCEMENT AND CONSEQUENCES Violations of this policy may result in disciplinary action up to and including termination of employment or contract, in accordance with Meridian Financial's Human Resources Disciplinary Policy (HR-001). Severity levels: - MINOR violation (first offense, unintentional): documented warning and mandatory security awareness retraining. - MODERATE violation (repeat offense or intentional minor violation): formal written warning, escalation to HR, and possible suspension. - SEVERE violation (intentional misuse, data exfiltration, or illegal activity): immediate suspension pending investigation; potential termination and referral to law enforcement. Violations that constitute a criminal offense will be reported to appropriate law enforcement agencies. Meridian Financial reserves the right to pursue civil remedies for damages caused by policy violations.
Common trap: writing unenforceable rules. "Employees must use the internet responsibly" is not an enforceable policy statement. It is a slogan. Ask yourself: if a manager brought this sentence to HR as evidence of a violation, could HR act on it? If not, rewrite it with a specific behavior. Compare: "Employees shall not use company systems to access, download, or distribute material that is illegal, obscene, harassing, or discriminatory." That sentence is testable.
Common trap: no BYOD coverage. A policy that only covers company-owned devices has a blind spot that covers roughly half the real risk surface in any hybrid workforce. If employees can access email on their phone, the phone is in scope whether the policy says so or not. Silence does not equal exclusion in an audit.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • Your AUP covers acceptable use, prohibited use, BYOD, monitoring/consent, and consequences
  • Pick any rule from your AUP and confirm you could objectively decide whether it was violated — if it contains words like "responsibly," "appropriately," or "as needed," rewrite it with a specific behavior

Draft a Password Policy
Password policy is where most organizations are still writing rules from 2010. NIST SP 800-63B (published 2017, revised 2024) reversed several widely held assumptions: mandatory periodic rotation, complexity rules that force predictable substitutions, and maximum length limits all increase risk rather than reduce it. NIST 800-63B is mandatory for U.S. federal agencies and their contractors; for private-sector organizations it is voluntary, though it is widely adopted as the de facto best-practice baseline and increasingly referenced in audits and vendor assessments. Your policy must reflect current guidance or it will fail a NIST-aligned audit.
  1. Understand the four NIST SP 800-63B rules your policy must reflect. These are not optional preferences — they are the normative baseline for federal contractors and widely adopted as best practice by ISO 27002:2022.
    Rule 1: Minimum length over complexity. Memorized secrets shall be at least 8 characters for user accounts. Longer is always better. NIST recommends supporting passphrases up to 64 characters. A 4-word passphrase ("correct horse battery staple") is stronger than a 10-character password with symbols.

    Rule 2: Screen against known-breached passwords. Passwords shall be checked against a list of commonly used and previously compromised passwords. NIST calls this a "blacklist." In practice, this means checking against HaveIBeenPwned or an equivalent service during account creation and password reset.

    Rule 3: Require multi-factor authentication (MFA). MFA shall be required for all remote access, privileged accounts, and any system that stores or processes Sensitive Data. NIST 800-63B AAL2 requires at least two authentication factors.

    Rule 4: No mandatory periodic rotation without cause. Passwords shall NOT be required to expire on a fixed schedule unless there is evidence of compromise. Forced rotation causes predictable patterns (Password1 becomes Password2) that reduce security. Rotation is triggered by compromise events, not calendars.
  2. Write the Password Policy document. Use your skeleton from Phase 1. Below is a complete worked example.
    POLICY TITLE: Password and Authentication Policy Policy Number: SEC-003 Version: 1.0 Effective Date: 2026-07-01 Last Reviewed: 2026-06-19 Next Review Date: 2027-06-19 Policy Owner: Chief Information Security Officer Approved By: Chief Executive Officer Classification: Internal --- 1. PURPOSE This policy establishes minimum requirements for the creation, management, and protection of passwords and authentication credentials used to access Meridian Financial systems and data. This policy aligns with NIST Special Publication 800-63B (Digital Identity Guidelines, 2024 revision) to reduce the risk of unauthorized access due to weak, reused, or compromised credentials. 2. SCOPE This policy applies to all Meridian Financial employees, contractors, and third-party vendors who are issued or create credentials to access any Meridian Financial system, application, or data resource. 3. DEFINITIONS MEMORIZED SECRET -- a password, PIN, or passphrase known only to the user, used as an authentication factor. PRIVILEGED ACCOUNT -- any account with elevated access rights, including administrator, root, service, and shared functional accounts. MFA (MULTI-FACTOR AUTHENTICATION) -- authentication that requires two or more independent factors: something you know (password), something you have (hardware token, authenticator app), or something you are (biometric). CREDENTIAL COMPROMISE -- confirmed or suspected unauthorized disclosure of authentication credentials, including exposure in a third-party data breach. 4. POLICY STATEMENTS 4.1 PASSWORD CREATION 4.1.1 All user account passwords shall be a minimum of 12 characters in length. Passphrases (sequences of four or more random words) are encouraged. 4.1.2 Meridian Financial systems shall support passwords of up to 64 characters without truncation. Systems that silently truncate passwords shall be flagged to the IT Department for remediation. 4.1.3 Passwords shall not be required to include specific character types (uppercase, lowercase, digits, symbols). Systems that enforce mandatory complexity rules shall be updated to remove this requirement during the next maintenance window. 4.1.4 Users shall not choose passwords that appear on the Meridian Financial prohibited password list, which includes the 10,000 most commonly used passwords, all passwords previously exposed in known data breaches (sourced from HaveIBeenPwned or equivalent), and any password containing the user's name, username, or organization name. 4.2 PASSWORD MANAGEMENT 4.2.1 Users shall use a unique password for each Meridian Financial system. Reuse of passwords across systems is prohibited. 4.2.2 Passwords shall be stored exclusively in the company-approved password manager. Writing passwords on physical media or storing them in unencrypted files is prohibited. 4.2.3 Passwords shall NOT expire on a fixed schedule. Mandatory periodic rotation is not required under this policy. 4.2.4 Passwords shall be changed immediately upon any of the following events: (a) suspected or confirmed compromise, (b) notification from the IT Security team that credentials may have been exposed, (c) employee separation or role change that reduces access requirements. 4.3 MULTI-FACTOR AUTHENTICATION 4.3.1 MFA shall be required for all remote access to Meridian Financial systems, including VPN, cloud applications, and remote desktop connections. 4.3.2 MFA shall be required for all privileged accounts without exception. Service accounts that cannot support MFA shall be approved by the CISO in writing and reviewed annually. 4.3.3 SMS-based one-time codes are acceptable as a second factor only when no stronger option (authenticator app, hardware security key) is available. Authenticator apps (TOTP-based) are the minimum recommended second factor for new deployments. 4.3.4 Hardware security keys (FIDO2/WebAuthn) shall be used for all administrator-level access to production systems. 4.4 PASSWORD SHARING 4.4.1 Users shall not share passwords with any other person, including IT support personnel. IT staff shall never ask a user for their password; any such request shall be reported immediately to the CISO. 4.4.2 Shared functional accounts (e.g., admin@domain.com) are prohibited for production systems. Where a shared account cannot be eliminated, each authorized user shall authenticate individually via a privileged access management (PAM) system that logs individual actions.
  3. Verify your policy against NIST 800-63B. Check each item in this list against your draft. If any item is missing or contradicts your policy, fix it before moving on.
    Minimum length is at or above the NIST 800-63B floor of 8 (the example org chose 12 as an org uplift): YES / NO
    Passphrase support up to 64 characters: YES / NO
    No mandatory complexity rules: YES / NO
    Breached-password screening: YES / NO
    MFA required for remote and privileged access: YES / NO
    No mandatory periodic rotation (unless compromised): YES / NO
    Password sharing explicitly prohibited: YES / NO
Common trap: still mandating 90-day password rotation. Forcing rotation every 90 days is directly contradicted by NIST SP 800-63B Section 5.1.1.2: "Verifiers SHOULD NOT require memorized secrets to be changed arbitrarily." If your organization currently enforces 90-day rotation, this policy should include a remediation note and a timeline for disabling the rotation requirement in Active Directory or your identity provider. A policy that mandates the impossible creates shadow practices — users write passwords on sticky notes rather than memorizing a new one every 90 days.
Common trap: forgetting service accounts. Service accounts often have the weakest passwords in the environment because no human manages them day-to-day. Your policy must explicitly address them: who owns them, when their credentials are rotated, whether they can be used interactively, and how their access is reviewed.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • The policy reflects NIST 800-63B: minimum length, breached-password screening, MFA requirement, no mandatory periodic rotation
  • It names who it applies to (scope), what the rules are (policy statements), and how it is enforced

Add Scope, Roles & Enforcement
A policy without named owners is a document with no one accountable. ISO/IEC 27001:2022 clause 5.1 requires that information security policies define responsibilities explicitly. This phase adds the Roles and Responsibilities section, the Exceptions process, and the Review cadence to both policies — turning each into a complete, audit-ready governance document.
  1. Add the Roles and Responsibilities section to both policies. Each entry must name a specific job title (not a person's name, since people leave) and their specific obligation. Copy this worked example for your AUP and adapt it.
    5. ROLES AND RESPONSIBILITIES CHIEF INFORMATION SECURITY OFFICER (CISO) - Owns this policy and is accountable for its accuracy and currency. - Reviews and approves this policy annually or following a material change in the risk environment. - Approves all exceptions to this policy. - Ensures this policy is communicated to all covered parties. IT DEPARTMENT - Implements technical controls that enforce the rules in this policy (e.g., web filtering, email scanning, endpoint management). - Reviews and investigates reported policy violations. - Maintains the BYOD enrollment platform and MDM infrastructure. HUMAN RESOURCES DEPARTMENT - Ensures all employees acknowledge this policy in writing at hire. - Ensures all employees re-acknowledge this policy following material updates. - Administers disciplinary action in partnership with IT and legal. MANAGERS AND SUPERVISORS - Ensure team members have read, understood, and acknowledged this policy. - Report suspected policy violations to IT Security without delay. ALL EMPLOYEES, CONTRACTORS, AND VENDORS - Read and acknowledge this policy before accessing company systems. - Comply with all policy requirements. - Report suspected violations or security incidents to IT Security at security@meridianfinancial.com or ext. 5555. - Complete annual security awareness training that covers this policy.
    Why job titles, not names: a policy that says "John Smith in IT" becomes stale the first time John changes roles. Policies are durable documents — write them to survive personnel changes.
  2. Add the Exceptions section to both policies. Every real policy needs a formal exception process. Without one, people work around the policy silently, which is far more dangerous than a documented exception.
    6. EXCEPTIONS Requests for an exception to any provision of this policy shall be submitted in writing to the CISO using the Exception Request Form (available on the company intranet at IT Security > Forms). An exception request must include: - The specific policy statement for which an exception is requested - The business justification for the exception - The duration of the requested exception (maximum 12 months) - Proposed compensating controls that mitigate the risk created by the exception Exceptions are approved at the sole discretion of the CISO. Approved exceptions are documented in the Policy Exception Register and reviewed at each annual policy review cycle. No exception is permanent; all approved exceptions must be renewed or closed.
  3. Add the Review Cadence and Version History section to both policies. This section satisfies ISO 27001 clause 5.1's requirement that policies be reviewed at planned intervals.
    8. REVIEW CADENCE AND VERSION HISTORY This policy shall be reviewed annually by the CISO, or immediately following any of the following events: - A material change in the organization's risk profile - A significant change in technology, regulation, or business model - A security incident in which the absence or inadequacy of this policy was a contributing factor - An external audit finding that references this policy Any material change to this policy requires CISO review and CEO approval before the revised version becomes effective. Minor editorial corrections (typographical errors, formatting) may be made by the CISO without CEO approval and are noted in the version history. Version History: | Version | Effective Date | Author | Summary of Changes | |---------|---------------|--------------|------------------------| | 1.0 | 2026-07-01 | CISO | Initial release |
    What "annually" means in practice: the review date in your policy header is not a suggestion. Under ISO 27001, auditors check whether the review date has passed and whether a review was conducted. A policy with an expired review date is a nonconformity. Schedule the review as a recurring calendar event the day the policy is published.
  4. Confirm your policy header fields are all filled in. Run through this checklist for each policy you have drafted.
    Policy Owner (job title): ________________ FILLED / MISSING Approved By (job title): ________________ FILLED / MISSING Effective Date: ________________ FILLED / MISSING Next Review Date: ________________ FILLED / MISSING Policy Number: ________________ FILLED / MISSING Version: ________________ FILLED / MISSING Classification: ________________ FILLED / MISSING
Common trap: "TBD" in the owner or approver fields. A policy submitted for audit with "TBD" in the ownership fields has no legal authority and generates a finding. If you do not yet know who the CISO is at your organization, use the role title and leave the name for the cover sheet. The role is what matters in the policy body.
Common trap: no exception process. Some first-time policy writers omit the exceptions section because it feels like you are giving people a way out. The opposite is true. Without a formal exception process, employees who cannot comply with a rule will either violate it silently or escalate to management, who will grant an informal verbal exception that is never documented. Informal undocumented exceptions are the biggest compliance liability you can create.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • Each policy names an owner, an approver, an enforcement mechanism, and a review date
  • None of those four are left as "TBD" or implicit — every field in the policy header is filled with a specific job title or date

Review, Socialize & Get Sign-off
A policy that no one has read, acknowledged, or been trained on is not enforceable. This phase closes the loop: you test your AUP with a non-expert reader, document the acknowledgement mechanism, and write the rollout note that explains how the policy will be communicated to all covered parties. These steps are required by ISO/IEC 27001:2022 clause 7.4 (Communication) and clause 7.3 (Awareness).
  1. Conduct a plain-language readability test on your AUP. Ask someone who has never worked in IT or security to read your Acceptable Use Policy. Do not coach them. After they read it, ask them three questions: (1) Name three things you are not allowed to do. (2) What do you do if you think your password was stolen? (3) If you break a rule, what could happen to you? If they cannot answer all three from the text alone, rewrite the sections that failed. This is the most reliable quality gate for policy clarity.
    What to look for in the responses: if they say "I'm not sure" about prohibited actions, your Section 4.2 is too vague. If they say "I'd call IT" but your policy does not give a contact (phone or email), add it. If they cannot state a consequence, your Section 7 is missing specificity.
  2. Design the acknowledgement mechanism. Acknowledgement is the evidence that a covered person has received, read, and understood the policy. Write the acknowledgement record into your policy or a companion document. A complete acknowledgement captures the elements shown below.
    POLICY ACKNOWLEDGEMENT RECORD I, _________________________ [printed name], acknowledge that I have received, read, and understand the following Meridian Financial policy: Policy Title: ________________________________ Policy Number: ________________________________ Version: ________________________________ Effective Date: _______________________________ I understand that compliance with this policy is a condition of my continued employment or engagement with Meridian Financial. I understand that violations of this policy may result in disciplinary action up to and including termination. Signature: _________________________ Date: _____________ Employee/Contractor ID: ____________ Department: ________
    Where this lives in practice: for new hires, the acknowledgement is collected during onboarding via the HR system (Workday, BambooHR, etc.) or a signed paper form retained in the employee file. For policy updates, re-acknowledgement is typically collected through the same system via an annual training completion event. The mechanism itself does not need to be in the policy body — a reference to the procedure is sufficient.
  3. Write the policy rollout communication. When a new policy is published or an existing one is materially updated, employees need a plain-language summary explaining what changed and what they need to do. Write a short rollout note for your AUP using the template below.
    TO: All Meridian Financial Employees and Contractors FROM: Office of the CISO SUBJECT: New Policy: Acceptable Use Policy (SEC-001) -- Action Required DATE: 2026-06-30 Meridian Financial is publishing its Acceptable Use Policy (SEC-001), effective 2026-07-01. This policy describes how company systems may be used and what activities are prohibited. KEY POINTS: - You must use a unique password for each company system. - Personal devices used for work email must be enrolled in MDM. - Sending company data to personal email or cloud storage is prohibited. - IT will never ask for your password -- report any such request. ACTION REQUIRED: By 2026-07-15, please complete the following: 1. Read the policy at [intranet link]. 2. Complete the 15-minute awareness module in the LMS at [LMS link]. 3. Sign the acknowledgement form in Workday under My Documents. Questions? Contact security@meridianfinancial.com or ext. 5555. Thank you for helping keep Meridian Financial secure.
  4. Complete your final policy checklist. Before calling the AUP and Password Policy complete, verify every item below. A single "NO" is a gap that would generate an audit finding.
    AUP COMPLETENESS CHECK Section 1 Purpose: clearly states the risk being addressed YES / NO Section 2 Scope: names all covered parties explicitly YES / NO Section 3 Definitions: all contested terms are defined YES / NO Section 4 Policy Statements: all rules use shall/must YES / NO Section 4 Policy Statements: every rule is objectively YES / NO testable (no "responsibly," "appropriately," etc.) Section 5 Roles: every named role has specific obligations YES / NO Section 6 Exceptions: formal exception process documented YES / NO Section 7 Enforcement: specific consequences stated YES / NO Section 8 Review: annual review date and version table YES / NO Header: Owner, Approver, Effective Date, Version filled YES / NO Readability test passed (non-expert can answer 3 questions) YES / NO Acknowledgement mechanism defined YES / NO Rollout communication drafted YES / NO
Common trap: publishing a policy without a communication plan. ISO 27001 clause 7.3 requires that relevant personnel be "aware of the information security policy." Publication on an intranet is not sufficient awareness. Employees must be notified, trained, and have acknowledged. A policy that employees cannot find and have never been told about provides no protection in a disciplinary or legal proceeding.
Common trap: copying a template without tailoring it. SANS and other organizations publish excellent policy templates. The failure mode is treating a template as a final product. Every placeholder (company name, contact information, system names, thresholds) must be replaced. An audit will find "XYZ Corporation" in your policy if you forgot to replace it, and that finding is embarrassing enough to have derailed real audits. Search your final document for common template artifacts before publishing: "[Company]," "TBD," "INSERT," "TODO," and generic email addresses like "security@company.com."
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • A non-expert reads your AUP and can state three things they are not allowed to do
  • You have defined an acknowledgement mechanism and a rollout communication — the policy has a path from "document" to "everyone knows about it"
