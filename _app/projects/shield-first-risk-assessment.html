Subject

Run Your First Risk Assessment

Operative 1.5 hr 500 XP
Mission Brief

Risk assessment is the engine of GRC — you cannot protect what you have not valued, or defend against threats you have not named. You will run a complete qualitative risk assessment on a bounded scope: inventory assets, identify threats and vulnerabilities, score likelihood × impact, build a risk register, and propose treatments.

The risk register you produce is the document that drives every security investment decision — it is how a security program decides where to spend its next dollar.

By the end you will have run a real assessment end to end and be able to defend why one risk gets mitigated and another gets accepted.

Agent Requirements
  • "Write Your First Security Policy" recommended first
  • A spreadsheet (Google Sheets, Excel, or LibreOffice Calc)
  • A bounded environment to assess (a small business or your own home network, real or fictional)
  • NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1 as reference — free PDF at nvlpubs.nist.gov

1
Define Scope & Inventory Assets
Before scoring a single risk, you must establish the boundary of what you are assessing and catalog every asset inside it. An asset without an owner or a criticality rating is unscoreable.
  1. Write a one-paragraph scope statement. Name the system, location, or business unit you are assessing and explicitly state what is excluded. A scope statement that says "the whole company" is not a scope statement.
    Example scope: "This assessment covers the customer-facing order management web application (app01.internal), its MySQL database (db01.internal), and the two staff laptops used by the order-processing team. Out of scope: the company ERP system, HR systems, and physical facilities."
  2. Open a new spreadsheet tab named "Asset Inventory." Create these columns exactly:
    Asset ID | Asset Name | Asset Type | Owner | Criticality (H/M/L) | Justification
  3. List every asset inside your scope. Aim for at least 8. Asset types include: Data, System/Server, Application, Network Device, Workstation/Endpoint, People/Role, Facility. Fill in every column for each row.
  4. Assign criticality using this scale. Write it in a comment or sidebar so your reasoning is auditable:
    High (H) -- Loss or compromise would cause severe operational, financial, legal, or reputational harm. Recovery difficult or impossible. Medium (M) -- Loss or compromise would cause significant disruption but the organization can recover within days to weeks. Low (L) -- Loss or compromise would have minor impact; recovery is straightforward and cost is low.
  5. Worked example rows to fill in (replace with your own assets):
    A-001 | Customer Order DB | Data/System | IT Manager | H | Contains PII and payment refs; breach triggers breach-notification law A-002 | Order Management App | Application | Dev Lead | H | Primary revenue channel; downtime = lost orders A-003 | Staff Laptop (Jane) | Endpoint | Jane Smith | M | Access to order system; full disk encryption in place reduces impact A-004 | Local Network Switch | Network | IT Manager | M | Disruption isolates office but VPN failover available A-005 | Customer Email Archive | Data | Office Mgr | M | Sensitive but not regulated; 30-day backup retention A-006 | Backup Drive (external) | Data/Storage | IT Manager | H | Only copy of database backups; loss = no recovery A-007 | Company Wi-Fi Router | Network | IT Manager | L | Guest and staff segments; disruption inconvenient, not critical A-008 | Order Processing SOP | Documentation| Office Mgr | L | Process guidance; staff know procedures from memory
Common trap: scope too broad to finish. If your asset list exceeds 20 rows in your first assessment, your scope is too wide. Pick the 8-12 assets that matter most to the mission and draw a hard boundary. A completed narrow assessment is worth more than an incomplete broad one.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • A written scope statement with explicit in/out boundaries
  • An asset inventory spreadsheet tab with at least 8 rows, every column populated, and a criticality rating of H, M, or L with a one-line justification for each

2
Identify Threats & Vulnerabilities
For each High-criticality asset, identify the realistic threats that could harm it and the vulnerabilities those threats would exploit. Ground every threat in the NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1 taxonomy, not invented language.
  1. Open the NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1 threat taxonomy. Appendix D (Threat Sources) and Appendix E (Threat Events) are the reference. NIST Table D-2 defines four threat-source categories:
    ADVERSARIAL -- Individuals, groups, organizations, or nation-states that seek to exploit the organization's dependence on cyber resources. Characterized by Capability, Intent, Targeting. Examples: external attackers, malicious insiders, competitors. ACCIDENTAL -- Erroneous actions taken by individuals in the course of executing their everyday responsibilities. Examples: developer pushes untested code, admin misconfigures a firewall rule, user emails PII to wrong address. STRUCTURAL -- Failures of equipment, environmental controls, or software due to aging, resource depletion, or other circumstances exceeding expected operating parameters. Examples: hard drive failure, UPS battery depletion, HVAC breakdown, software memory leak causing system crash. ENVIRONMENTAL -- Natural disasters and failures of critical infrastructures on which the organization depends, but which are outside the control of the organization. Examples: flood, earthquake, ISP outage, power-grid failure. STRUCTURAL vs. ENVIRONMENTAL: Structural = failure of equipment the organization controls (your hardware, your HVAC). Environmental = failure of infrastructure outside your control (ISP, utility, natural disaster).
  2. Add a new spreadsheet tab named "Threat-Vuln Pairs." Create these columns:
    Asset ID | Threat Source | Threat Event | Vulnerability Exploited | Notes
  3. Write at least two threat/vulnerability pairs for each High-criticality asset. A threat event is a specific action; a vulnerability is a specific weakness that makes it possible. "Hackers" is a threat source, not a threat event. Be specific.
    Good vs. bad threat language:
    BAD: "Hackers might attack the database."
    GOOD: "Adversarial insider (disgruntled employee) exploits over-privileged database account to exfiltrate customer PII."
  4. Worked example rows for the High assets from Phase 1:
    A-001 | Adversarial / External | SQL injection attack against order app exposes customer records | Input validation not enforced on order search endpoint A-001 | Adversarial / Insider | Privileged DB user exports full customer table to personal cloud storage | No DLP controls; DB accounts not scoped to least privilege A-002 | Accidental / User | Developer pushes untested code to production branch causing application crash | No staging environment; no mandatory code review gate A-006 | Structural | External backup drive fails due to age / write-cycle exhaustion before restore | No drive health monitoring; no second backup copy to verify against A-002 | Environmental | ISP outage takes down app server because no failover exists | Single internet connection; no secondary ISP or CDN [ISP outage = Environmental: the ISP is infrastructure outside the organization's control. If a company-owned router or on-premises server failed instead, that would be Structural.] A-007 | Environmental | Extended ISP or power-grid outage disrupts guest Wi-Fi access | No redundant uplink; guest segment on same ISP circuit as staff A-006 | Adversarial / External | Ransomware encrypts primary server and detects network-attached backup drive | Backup drive always mounted; no offline or immutable backup copy
Common trap: vague threats stall scoring. If your threat event is "unauthorized access," you cannot score likelihood meaningfully because that phrase covers everything from a brute-force attack to a misconfigured permission. Every threat event must describe WHO does WHAT to WHICH asset through WHICH weakness. If you cannot answer all four, rewrite it before moving to Phase 3.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • A Threat-Vuln Pairs tab with at least two rows per High-criticality asset
  • Every threat event is specific enough to name who, what action, which vulnerability — no row says only "hackers" or "unauthorized access"
  • At least one row in each of the four NIST threat-source categories (Adversarial, Accidental, Structural, Environmental)

Score Likelihood × Impact
Define your scoring scale explicitly, then score every threat/vulnerability pair from Phase 2 on both dimensions. Risk = Likelihood × Impact. Consistent, defined scales are what make a risk register defensible.
  1. Add a "Scoring Scale" tab. Paste and save the following definitions before scoring a single item — your scores are only meaningful if the scale is declared:
    LIKELIHOOD SCALE (1-5) 1 - Rare Threat event is highly unlikely given existing controls; no known incidents in similar environments in 5+ years. 2 - Unlikely Threat event could occur but controls make it improbable; fewer than one incident per 3-5 years in similar environments. 3 - Possible Threat event may occur; controls partially effective; incidents occur occasionally in similar environments (~1/year). 4 - Likely Threat event is probable; controls are weak or absent; incidents are common in similar environments. 5 - Almost Threat event is expected to occur without intervention; Certain no effective controls exist. IMPACT SCALE (1-5) 1 - Negligible No operational disruption; no regulatory exposure; cost to remediate is trivial (<$500). 2 - Minor Short disruption (<4 hours); limited data exposure; remediation cost manageable (<$5,000). 3 - Moderate Multi-day disruption or measurable data breach; regulatory notice may be required; cost $5K-$50K. 4 - Significant Extended outage (>1 week) or confirmed PII breach; regulatory fines likely; cost $50K-$500K. 5 - Critical Catastrophic: irreversible data loss, major breach, regulatory action, or organizational survival at risk. RISK SCORE = Likelihood x Impact Risk Tier: 1-4 Low 5-9 Medium 10-19 High 20-25 Critical
  2. Add a "Risk Scoring" tab. Create these columns:
    Risk ID | Asset ID | Threat Event (short) | Likelihood (1-5) | L Rationale | Impact (1-5) | I Rationale | Risk Score | Risk Tier
  3. Score every row from your Threat-Vuln Pairs tab. Write a one-sentence rationale for each score. Do not guess — consider existing controls, incident history, and asset exposure.
  4. Worked example rows showing rationale:
    R-001 | A-001 | SQL injection on order app L: 4 (Likely) -- No input validation on endpoint; SQLi is trivially automated; similar apps breached monthly I: 5 (Critical) -- Customer PII + payment refs; breach triggers GDPR/state law notification; class-action exposure Score: 20 | Tier: CRITICAL R-002 | A-001 | Insider DB exfiltration L: 2 (Unlikely) -- Only 2 DB users; both long-tenured; no recent grievances; but no monitoring exists I: 5 (Critical) -- Full customer dataset; same regulatory exposure as R-001 Score: 10 | Tier: HIGH R-003 | A-002 | Ransomware + mounted backup L: 3 (Possible) -- SMB phishing campaigns common; no email filtering; backup drive permanently mapped I: 5 (Critical) -- No offline copy means no recovery option; full data loss likely Score: 15 | Tier: HIGH R-007 | A-007 | ISP/power outage disrupts guest Wi-Fi L: 1 (Rare) -- Grid and ISP outages are infrequent; UPS on router; historically <1 event per 5 yrs I: 2 (Minor) -- Visitor internet only; no internal systems reachable from guest segment; trivial productivity loss Score: 2 | Tier: LOW
  5. Sort your Risk Scoring tab by Risk Score descending to produce your ranked risk list. The highest score at the top is your top risk.
Common trap: undefined or inconsistent scale. If you score one risk's impact as 4 because "it's pretty bad" and another's as 3 because "it's also pretty bad," the register is not auditable. Every score must trace back to the declared scale definitions. If two risks seem equally impactful but you scored them differently, revisit the rationale column until the difference is explicit.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • A "Scoring Scale" tab with Likelihood 1-5 and Impact 1-5 definitions written out, plus the tier thresholds (Low/Medium/High/Critical)
  • A "Risk Scoring" tab with every threat/vulnerability pair scored, a rationale for both L and I, a calculated Risk Score, and a Risk Tier — sorted by Risk Score descending
✓
4
Build the Risk Register
The risk register is the single source of truth: it consolidates asset, threat, score, owner, and proposed treatment into one auditable document. Build it now by pulling from your previous tabs.
  1. Add a final tab named "Risk Register." This is the master document. Create these columns exactly:
    Risk ID | Asset | Threat Event | Vulnerability | L | I | Score | Tier | Risk Owner | Proposed Treatment | Treatment Type | Residual Risk Note | Status
  2. Populate one row per scored risk from Phase 3, pulling asset and threat data from Phase 1 and Phase 2. Every column must be filled — "TBD" or blank is not acceptable in a risk register.
  3. Assign a Risk Owner to each row. The owner is the person accountable for the treatment decision — not the IT department as a unit, but a named individual or role (e.g., "IT Manager," "CFO," "Dev Lead"). An asset without an owner means no one is responsible if the risk materializes.
    Owner vs. Custodian: The owner decides what to do about the risk (treatment authority). The custodian maintains the asset day-to-day. They can be the same person in small organizations, but the distinction matters when something goes wrong.
  4. Worked example rows for the top risks:
    R-001 | Customer Order DB | SQL injection exposes PII Vulnerability: No input validation on search endpoint L:4 I:5 Score:20 Tier:CRITICAL Owner: IT Manager Treatment: (leave blank -- assigned in Phase 5) Residual Risk: Medium (5) -- post-treatment L:1, I:5 (1x5=5) SQLi path closed; I unchanged (data still sensitive) Status: Open R-002 | Customer Order DB | Insider DB exfiltration Vulnerability: Over-privileged DB accounts, no monitoring L:2 I:5 Score:10 Tier:HIGH Owner: IT Manager Treatment: (leave blank -- assigned in Phase 5) Residual Risk: Medium (5) -- post-treatment L:1, I:5 (1x5=5) Monitoring deters; data remains sensitive Status: Open R-003 | Order App (A-002) | Ransomware encrypts server + backup Vulnerability: Backup drive permanently mounted, no offline copy L:3 I:5 Score:15 Tier:HIGH Owner: IT Manager Treatment: (leave blank -- assigned in Phase 5) Residual Risk: Low (4) -- post-treatment L:2, I:2 (2x2=4) Backup window still a brief exposure; recovery now feasible Status: Open
  5. Identify your top 3 risks by highlighting or bolding the top three rows (highest scores). These are the risks that will receive explicit treatment decisions in Phase 5.
Common trap: assets with no owner. If you write "IT Department" as the owner, you have assigned responsibility to no one. When the risk materializes, every member of the IT Department will say someone else was responsible. Name a specific person or title. If no one will accept ownership of a risk, that is itself a finding you document.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • A completed Risk Register tab with at least 8 scored entries — Risk ID, Asset, Threat Event, Vulnerability, L, I, Score, Tier, Risk Owner, and Status all populated; Treatment columns may be blank at this stage (treatments are assigned in Phase 5)
  • A named Risk Owner (person or specific role) in every row — not "IT Department"
  • The top 3 risks are clearly identifiable by Risk Score — visually highlighted or sorted to the top
  • The scope boundary from Phase 1 is referenced or reproduced at the top of the register tab
✓
5
Propose Treatments
For each of your top risks, select a treatment strategy from the four NIST options, write a concrete action, and document the residual risk that remains after the treatment is applied. A treatment without a residual risk note is incomplete.
  1. Understand the four treatment types. Add these definitions to your Scoring Scale tab or a new "Treatment Guide" tab:
    MITIGATE -- Implement a control that reduces the likelihood or impact of the risk. The risk still exists after mitigation but at a lower level. Example: add input validation to reduce SQLi likelihood from 4 to 1. TRANSFER -- Shift financial consequence to a third party, typically via cyber liability insurance or a vendor contract with indemnification. The threat and vulnerability remain; only the financial impact is transferred. ACCEPT -- Formally acknowledge that the risk exists and decide not to act on it. MUST include written justification: why the cost of mitigation exceeds the expected loss, and who authorized the decision. "We accept it" with no justification is not acceptance -- it is negligence. AVOID -- Eliminate the activity or asset that creates the risk. Example: stop collecting credit card data entirely and use a PCI-compliant payment processor, removing the data asset from scope.
  2. Open your Risk Register tab. For each of your top 3 risks, fill in the Treatment Type, Proposed Treatment (a concrete, actionable sentence), and Residual Risk Note columns.
    A concrete treatment names specific actions. "Improve security" is not a treatment. "Deploy a WAF rule set blocking UNION-based SQL injection payloads, and rewrite the order search query to use parameterized statements by 2026-07-30" is a treatment.
  3. Worked example treatment decisions for the top 3 risks:
    R-001 | CRITICAL | Score 20 | MITIGATE Action: Rewrite order search endpoint to use parameterized SQL queries (PDO/prepared statements). Deploy ModSecurity WAF on app server with OWASP Core Rule Set. Complete by end of sprint. Residual: After fix, SQLi L drops to 1; I stays 5. Residual Score = 5 (Medium). Acceptable; monitor for bypass. R-003 | HIGH | Score 15 | MITIGATE Action: Disconnect backup drive after each nightly backup job using a scheduled task. Implement Veeam free-tier with immutable repository on a second drive kept physically offline. Test restore quarterly. Residual: L drops from 3 to 2 (backup still reachable during backup window); I drops from 5 to 2 (recovery now feasible). Residual Score = 4 (Low). Acceptable. R-002 | HIGH | Score 10 | MITIGATE + TRANSFER Action (Mitigate): Revoke broad SELECT/INSERT grants; issue role-specific DB accounts with least-privilege. Enable MySQL general query log for the 2 privileged accounts; review weekly. Action (Transfer): Add cyber liability rider to company insurance policy covering first-party breach response costs. Residual: L drops to 1 (monitoring deters); I stays 5 (data still sensitive). Residual Score = 5 (Medium). Insurance backstops remaining exposure.
  4. Add an "Accept" example for one of your Low-scored risks to practice the acceptance decision:
    R-007 | LOW | Score 2 | ACCEPT Risk: Guest Wi-Fi disruption (L:1, I:2) Justification: Guest Wi-Fi is not connected to internal systems. Disruption affects only internet access for visitors, not operations. Cost of redundant ISP link ($300/month) exceeds expected annual impact (~$50 in lost productivity). IT Manager accepts on behalf of organization. Decision documented and reviewed annually. Residual: No change; risk remains at Score 2. Acceptable without action.
  5. Final review. Read back through your complete Risk Register. Verify: every row has an Owner, every top risk has a Treatment Type and an actionable Treatment sentence, every treatment has a Residual Risk note with a score. If any row fails these checks, complete it before calling Phase 5 done.
Common trap: accepting risk with no rationale. Writing "Accept" in the Treatment Type column without a written justification is a compliance violation in most frameworks (NIST CSF, ISO 27001, SOC 2). Acceptance requires: (1) who authorized it, (2) why the cost of treatment exceeds the expected loss, and (3) a review date. Without all three, auditors will treat it as an unmitigated unaddressed risk.
Common trap: treatments that are not actionable. "Improve access controls" is a direction, not a treatment. Every treatment must answer: what exactly will change, who will change it, and by when. If you cannot answer those three, the treatment will not be implemented.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • Every top-3 risk has a Treatment Type (Mitigate / Transfer / Accept / Avoid), a concrete named action, and a calculated Residual Risk score
  • At least one Accept decision is documented with authorization, cost justification, and review date
  • The complete Risk Register has at least 8 scored, treated entries; every row has a Risk Owner and a Status
  • You can state aloud why each top risk received its treatment type rather than one of the other three options
