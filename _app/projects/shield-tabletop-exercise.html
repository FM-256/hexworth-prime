Subject

Lead a Tabletop Exercise

Specialist 2 hr 2,000 XP
Mission Brief

A plan you have never exercised is a hope, not a capability. You will design and facilitate a tabletop exercise — a discussion-based incident simulation — and produce an after-action report that turns lessons into owned, dated improvements.

This is how a security leader proves readiness, finds the gaps before an attacker does, and earns the trust to lead in a real crisis.

By the end you will have run an exercise, captured what broke, and closed the loop by feeding fixes back into the plan.

Agent Requirements
  • "Write an Incident Response Plan" strongly recommended (you will exercise a plan)
  • A small group is ideal, but you can run it solo as a thought exercise -- see the solo note below
  • A document for notes and the after-action report
  • CISA tabletop exercise packages as reference
Running solo? Write your responses to each inject as you go -- answer the discussion questions in writing as if you were speaking for the room. Immediately after the last inject, do the hot wash as a written self-reflection: what was the biggest gap you saw, what worked, what should be tested next? The "separate Evaluator" rule applies to group runs, where the facilitator genuinely cannot manage the room and take notes at once. Solo, your written notes ARE the record -- just keep them honest and specific. The no-fault framing still matters: write what you would actually do, not what you wish you would do.

Design the Scenario & Injects
Before a single person sits down, you need a written scenario that tells a plausible story and a set of injects that force real decisions. An inject is a scripted event you hand to the room at a specific moment -- a new email, a vendor call, a news alert -- that escalates the situation and requires the team to act. CISA Exercise Design guidance and NIST SP 800-84 both describe this as the foundation: without pre-written injects, exercises collapse into unguided conversation with no testable moments.
  1. Write a one-paragraph scenario description. Give the organization a name, an industry, a size, and a concrete triggering event. Make it realistic enough that people can role-play it, not so fantastical that it feels like a movie. Paste the template below into your document and fill it in.
EXERCISE SCENARIO: [Exercise Name] Organization: [Name], [Industry], ~[Size] employees Date/Time of fictional incident: [Day, Time] SITUATION: At [TIME], [triggering event -- one specific thing that actually happened]. [SENTENCE 2: What the team first noticed -- an alert, a call, a user complaint.] [SENTENCE 3: Initial scope -- what systems or data appear to be affected.] [SENTENCE 4: What is still unknown -- set up the uncertainty the team must navigate.] This exercise will run for approximately [duration]. All events are fictional. No real systems, data, or customers are involved.
Worked example (ransomware scenario): "At 7:42 AM on a Tuesday, the help desk receives 11 simultaneous calls from staff in the Accounts Payable department reporting that their files have been replaced with files ending in .locked and a ransom note has appeared on their desktops. The IT team's endpoint monitoring tool shows the agent is offline on all 11 machines. It is unclear whether the infection is contained to AP or has spread to file servers. Backups were last verified six weeks ago."
  1. Write at least 4 injects. Each inject is a new piece of information delivered to the room at a scripted time. Injects must escalate -- each one should make the situation harder or open a new decision point. Use the template below for each inject, then fill in the example column.
Facilitator note -- Inject 1 timing: Inject 1 both introduces the scenario and launches the first decision questions. Before asking those questions, give the room about 5 minutes to read and absorb the scenario briefing. Announce: "Take a few minutes to read through the situation. We will begin the discussion shortly." Only after that pause do you move to the discussion questions. Rushing Inject 1 produces reflexive, shallow answers; the pause produces the deliberate thinking that surfaces real gaps.
INJECT LOG --------------------------------------------------------------- INJECT 1 -- T+0 (Exercise Start) Situation update: [Describe the initial triggering event in present tense.] [Allow ~5 minutes for participants to read and absorb the scenario before discussion.] Discussion questions: - Who owns the initial response decision? Who calls it? - What is your first action in the next 15 minutes? - Do you isolate affected systems now, or investigate first? What drives that call? INJECT 2 -- T+20 minutes Situation update: [Escalation -- scope expands, new system affected, or key resource unavailable.] Discussion questions: - Does this change your containment strategy? - Who needs to be notified now that wasn't on your initial call list? - What information do you need before you can make the next decision? INJECT 3 -- T+40 minutes Situation update: [Complication -- leadership demands status, media inquiry arrives, or a critical dependency fails.] Discussion questions: - What do you tell [executive / board / public] right now? Who delivers that message? - Has anyone contacted legal / compliance / cyber insurance? Who owns that call? - What is your current containment status -- can you confirm the spread has stopped? INJECT 4 -- T+55 minutes Situation update: [Recovery decision point -- you have options with trade-offs, e.g., restore from backup vs. pay ransom vs. rebuild from scratch.] Discussion questions: - What criteria does your IR plan specify for this decision? - Who has authority to authorize the chosen recovery path? - What is your estimated RTO and does that match the business's tolerance? ---------------------------------------------------------------
Worked example -- Inject 2: "T+20 min: The network team reports that three file servers in the finance segment have been encrypted. The CTO is asking for a briefing in 10 minutes. Your backup administrator is unreachable -- it is their day off and they are not answering their phone."
  1. Write your exercise objectives. Objectives state what IR capability you are testing -- not what you hope will happen, but what specific decisions and processes you will observe. Aim for 3 to 5 objectives. Paste the template below.
EXERCISE OBJECTIVES This exercise will test the organization's ability to: OBJ-1: [Action verb] [specific capability]. Example: Identify and activate the correct escalation chain within 30 minutes of initial detection. OBJ-2: [Action verb] [specific capability]. Example: Make and document a containment decision -- isolate vs. investigate -- with a named owner within 15 minutes. OBJ-3: [Action verb] [specific capability]. Example: Communicate incident status to executive leadership with accurate, timely information. OBJ-4: [Action verb] [specific capability]. Example: Identify which regulatory notification obligations apply and who owns the notification decision. OBJ-5: [Action verb] [specific capability]. Example: Select a recovery path and articulate the criteria and authority for that decision.
GRC trap -- Objectives that are too vague: "Test our IR plan" is not an objective. You cannot observe whether the plan was "tested" -- you can only observe whether specific decisions were made, by whom, in what time, using what information. Vague objectives produce vague findings. Write objectives that could appear on a scoresheet: either the team did it or they didn't.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • A scenario with at least 4 injects that escalate and force real decisions
  • Clear objectives stating what capability you are testing

Set Players, Objectives & Ground Rules
A tabletop with the wrong people in the room will expose zero real gaps -- because the people who actually own the decisions aren't there. Equally, without explicit no-fault ground rules, people will perform rather than think out loud, and you will learn nothing. CISA's Exercise Design handbook identifies participant selection and ground-rule establishment as mandatory pre-exercise steps. NIST SP 800-84 formalizes the Evaluator role (sometimes called a data collector or scribe) specifically so findings are not lost when the facilitator is busy running injects.
  1. Build your participant roster. Every role in your IR plan that owns a decision should have a seat. Roles are more important than names -- when someone is absent, their backup fills the role. Paste the roster template below and fill it in for your organization.
PARTICIPANT ROSTER --------------------------------------------------------------- Role | Name (or TBD) | Responsibility in this exercise -------------------------|------------------|----------------------------------------- Incident Commander | | Owns all response decisions IT Lead / SOC Lead | | Owns technical containment decisions Legal / Compliance | | Advises on notification obligations Communications / PR | | Owns external messaging and media response Executive Sponsor | | Provides business authority for resource decisions HR Representative | | Advises if insider threat element is introduced Vendor / MSP Contact | | Technical escalation point (optional) --------------------------------------------------------------- FACILITATOR: | | Delivers injects, keeps time, does NOT solve problems DATA COLLECTOR: | | Records decisions, questions, friction points -- silent role ---------------------------------------------------------------
The Evaluator (data collector) is not optional. NIST SP 800-84 and HSEEP call this role the Evaluator -- someone other than the facilitator whose sole job is to record decisions, questions raised, and friction points in real time. The facilitator is busy managing the room and cannot simultaneously write down every friction point, side conversation, and unanswered question. Without a dedicated Evaluator taking notes, findings are reconstructed from memory after the fact -- and memory is selective. Designate this role before the exercise starts.
  1. Define your success criteria for each objective. For each OBJ you wrote in Phase 1, add a measurable success indicator -- what observable behavior would tell you the team handled it well?
OBJECTIVE SUCCESS CRITERIA --------------------------------------------------------------- OBJ-1: Activate escalation chain within 30 minutes. SUCCESS: The correct people were contacted in the correct order with no lookup time. The escalation contact list was accessible and current. OBJ-2: Make a containment decision within 15 minutes with a named owner. SUCCESS: One person said "we are isolating / we are investigating, I own this." The decision was logged with a timestamp. OBJ-3: Communicate status to executive leadership. SUCCESS: An exec-level status message was drafted and delivered, accurate at time of delivery. The message did not contradict technical team's current assessment. OBJ-4: Identify regulatory notification obligations. SUCCESS: Legal/compliance named the applicable framework (e.g., HIPAA, state breach law). A notification timeline was stated. OBJ-5: Select and authorize a recovery path. SUCCESS: Recovery criteria from the IR plan were cited. An authority name was attached to the decision. ---------------------------------------------------------------
  1. Write and distribute the ground rules before the exercise starts. Every participant receives this in writing. Read it aloud at the start of the session. These rules are not bureaucratic -- they are the only reason people will say what they actually think rather than what sounds good.
EXERCISE GROUND RULES --------------------------------------------------------------- 1. NO-FAULT ENVIRONMENT. The purpose of this exercise is to find gaps, not to assign blame. What is said in this room stays in this room unless the participant chooses otherwise. There are no wrong answers -- only honest and dishonest ones. 2. SAY WHAT YOU WOULD ACTUALLY DO, not what the plan says you should do. If the plan says "notify the CISO within 1 hour" and you actually wouldn't know who to call, say that. That is a finding. That is why we are here. 3. THIS IS NOT A TEST OF INDIVIDUALS. This is a test of the plan, the tools, and the process. Gaps that surface here get fixed. Gaps that stay hidden here get discovered by an attacker. 4. THE FACILITATOR IS NOT YOUR ADVERSARY. The facilitator's job is to inject pressure and ask hard questions -- not to catch you out. 5. STAY IN THE SCENARIO. Resist the urge to say "in real life we would just..." The scenario IS real life for this exercise. Make the calls you would make. 6. NO LAPTOPS FOR WORK. This time is reserved for the exercise. If you need to reference a document, use the printed materials provided. ---------------------------------------------------------------
GRC trap -- Skipping the ground rules briefing: The most common failure mode in organizational tabletops is that people perform competence for leadership rather than exposing real gaps. If a VP is in the room and no one has explicitly stated this is a no-fault exercise, people will not say "I don't know who to call." They will guess and move on. The findings you collect will be useless because they will reflect the room performing, not the process functioning.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • A participant roster mapped to roles, plus defined objectives
  • Ground rules that make it safe for people to admit "we don't have that" or "we don't know"

Facilitate the Exercise
Facilitation is not presentation. Your job is to deliver injects, ask follow-up questions, and keep the room on track -- not to help the team solve the problem. The hardest discipline in facilitation is staying quiet when you see an obvious gap. Let the team find it. If they don't find it, that gap goes on the findings list.
  1. Open the session with a 5-minute setup. Use this script exactly -- or adapt it to your voice -- to orient the room before Inject 1 goes out.
FACILITATOR OPENING SCRIPT (5 minutes) --------------------------------------------------------------- "Welcome. Today's exercise is [Exercise Name]. This is a discussion-based simulation -- no systems are being touched, no real data is involved. Here is what we are testing: [read your 3-5 objectives aloud]. Ground rules: [read the ground rules from Phase 2 aloud]. Our data collector today is [Name]. [Name] will be taking notes but will not participate in the discussion. We will run through [N] injects over approximately [duration]. I will hand each inject to you as a printed card / read it aloud / share it on screen. When you receive it, your job is to discuss what you would actually do -- not what the plan says, what you would actually do. I am not here to help you. I am here to observe. If you get stuck, say you are stuck -- that is a finding. Any questions before we start? [Pause.] Let's begin. Inject 1." ---------------------------------------------------------------
  1. Deliver each inject and run the discussion. After you read or distribute an inject, let the room react for 30 seconds before asking your first follow-up question. Use the facilitation pattern below. Your data collector is capturing everything -- you focus on the room.
FACILITATOR INJECT PATTERN --------------------------------------------------------------- 1. DELIVER -- Read the inject aloud or hand it out. State the simulated time. "The time is now T+20. New information." 2. WAIT -- Let the room absorb it. Count 30 seconds silently. 3. OPEN -- Ask the broadest question first: "What does this change for you?" or "What is your first action right now?" 4. DRILL -- Follow up on anything vague: "You said you would notify the team -- which team, and how?" "You said you would contain it -- who makes that call and what is the authority?" "You said you'd check the backups -- who does that, and where are the backups?" 5. NOTE -- If a decision is made, confirm it: "So the decision is [X], owned by [Name]. Is that correct?" Your data collector logs this verbatim. 6. ADVANCE -- When the discussion stalls or hits 10-15 minutes, close the inject: "Good. Let's note that and move forward. Inject [N+1]." ---------------------------------------------------------------
Friction is the data, not the failure. When the team hesitates, argues, or says "I'm not sure" -- that is valuable. Your data collector should flag every moment the team could not reach a decision quickly. Those hesitations become your findings in Phase 4.
  1. Close the exercise with a 5-minute hot wash. Before people leave the room, ask these three questions while the experience is fresh. This is not the full debrief -- that is the AAR. This is a quick capture so nothing evaporates in the hallway.
HOT WASH (5 minutes, immediately after last inject) --------------------------------------------------------------- Q1: "In one sentence: what is the biggest gap you personally saw today?" [Go around the room. Data collector logs every answer.] Q2: "What did we get right -- what would have actually worked?" [Go around the room. These become your 'strengths' in the AAR.] Q3: "Is there anything we didn't test that you think we should have?" [Open floor. Log any items mentioned -- these feed future exercises.] ---------------------------------------------------------------
GRC trap -- Solving problems during the exercise: The most common facilitator error is jumping in to help when the team gets stuck. "Well, your IR plan says you should call the CISO..." kills the exercise. If the team doesn't know to call the CISO, that is a finding. Helping them in the moment erases it. Stay silent. Let them surface it or not -- that is the data.
GRC trap -- No Evaluator: If the facilitator tries to facilitate and take notes simultaneously, one of those jobs fails. NIST SP 800-84 defines the Evaluator (also called a data collector or scribe) as a distinct, non-participating role for exactly this reason. Every finding that is not written down in the moment is a finding that gets reconstructed from memory -- which means it will be softened, combined with other findings, or forgotten entirely. Findings reconstructed from memory are not actionable. Assign the Evaluator role before the exercise starts.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • Notes capturing each inject and the decisions taken
  • At least 3 friction points where the team hesitated, disagreed, or lacked the info/authority to act

Turn Friction into Findings
Raw notes are not findings. A finding has a specific structure: what happened (observation), why it happened (root cause), and what it would cost if it happened for real (impact). CISA's After-Action Reporting guide and NIST SP 800-84 both require findings to be grounded in root cause -- not restated observations -- because only a root cause can be fixed. "The team didn't know who to call" is an observation. "The escalation contact list is not embedded in the IR plan and is stored only in one person's email" is a root cause.
  1. Review your data collector's notes. Read through every logged moment. Highlight every hesitation, disagreement, unanswered question, missing resource, and decision that could not be made. Each highlighted item is a candidate finding.
  2. Write a structured finding for each friction point. Use the template below. Be specific about the root cause -- name the plan section, the tool, the role, or the process that is broken. "Communication gap" is not a root cause.
FINDINGS LOG --------------------------------------------------------------- FINDING F-01 Observation: During Inject 2, the team spent 8 minutes attempting to determine who had authority to isolate the affected file servers. No decision was made during that window. Root cause: The IR plan (Section 3.2) names the "IT Manager" as the isolation authority, but the person in that role has no documented backup. The actual IT Manager was not in the exercise room (simulating absence). No one else knew if they had authority to make the call unilaterally. Impact: In a real ransomware event, an 8-minute delay in isolation authority allows continued encryption. At observed encryption rates (approx. 10,000 files/minute for common ransomware families), this represents significant additional data loss. Objective tested: OBJ-2 (containment decision within 15 minutes). Result: NOT MET. --------------------------------------------------------------- FINDING F-02 Observation: When Inject 3 introduced a media inquiry, no one in the room knew the current external messaging protocol or who was authorized to speak to press. Root cause: The Communications / PR role was not included in the participant roster. The IR plan references a "Communications Lead" but no name or backup contact is documented. Impact: A real media inquiry in a breach event, handled by a non-authorized spokesperson, creates legal exposure and reputational risk. Objective tested: OBJ-3 (executive and external communication). Result: NOT MET. --------------------------------------------------------------- FINDING F-03 Observation: [Your observation from your exercise notes.] Root cause: [The specific plan section, role gap, tool gap, or process gap.] Impact: [What this costs in a real incident -- data loss, downtime, legal exposure, etc.] Objective tested: [OBJ-N] Result: [MET / PARTIALLY MET / NOT MET] ---------------------------------------------------------------
Strengths are findings too. If the team executed something well -- activated the escalation chain immediately, had a current contact list, made a clean containment decision -- log that as a strength. The AAR needs both. Strengths tell you what to protect; gaps tell you what to fix.
GRC trap -- Restating symptoms as root causes: "The team was slow to respond" is a symptom. "The IR plan does not specify who owns the first-hour response decision, so three people waited for someone else to act" is a root cause. Symptoms produce no actionable improvement. Root causes produce specific fixes: update Section X of the plan, add a backup contact for Role Y, procure Tool Z. If your finding cannot produce a specific action item, the root cause is not specific enough.
GRC trap -- Turning the debrief into a blame session: Findings name process and plan gaps, not individuals. "The IT Manager didn't know the procedure" is blame. "The procedure is not written down anywhere the IT Manager can find it" is a finding. Blame produces defensiveness and silence in future exercises. Named-process findings produce organizational improvement.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • A findings list where each item has a root cause tied to a specific plan/role/tool gap
  • Findings name causes, not restated symptoms

Write the After-Action Report
The After-Action Report (AAR) is the product that determines whether the exercise was worth doing. CISA's HSEEP (Homeland Security Exercise and Evaluation Program) framework and NIST SP 800-84 both require the AAR to include: exercise summary, objectives and results, strengths, areas for improvement, and an Improvement Plan with named owners and dates. Without named owners and due dates, an AAR is a list of findings that will never be addressed. Findings without owners are organizational decoration.
  1. Write the AAR. Use the full template below. Every section has a clear purpose -- do not skip any of them. The Improvement Plan section is where the exercise either closes the loop or doesn't.
AFTER-ACTION REPORT (AAR) Exercise Name: [Exercise Name] Date: [Date exercise was conducted] Location: [In-person location or "Virtual"] Duration: [Start time] to [End time] Participants: [Number] participants, [list roles] Facilitator: [Name] Data Collector: [Name] Prepared by: [Name] Distribution: [List who receives this document] --------------------------------------------------------------- SECTION 1: EXECUTIVE SUMMARY --------------------------------------------------------------- [2-4 sentences. State what was tested, what the overall result was, and the top 1-2 priorities for improvement. Written for a non-technical executive reader.] Example: "On [date], [Organization] conducted a [duration] discussion-based tabletop exercise testing our ransomware incident response capability. The exercise revealed that our escalation authority chain has a single point of failure -- one person with no documented backup -- and that our Communications Lead role is not staffed or documented in the IR plan. These two gaps are the highest priority improvements identified and are assigned to named owners with 30-day completion targets." --------------------------------------------------------------- SECTION 2: EXERCISE OBJECTIVES AND RESULTS --------------------------------------------------------------- OBJ-1: [Objective text] Result: [MET / PARTIALLY MET / NOT MET] OBJ-2: [Objective text] Result: [MET / PARTIALLY MET / NOT MET] OBJ-3: [Objective text] Result: [MET / PARTIALLY MET / NOT MET] OBJ-4: [Objective text] Result: [MET / PARTIALLY MET / NOT MET] OBJ-5: [Objective text] Result: [MET / PARTIALLY MET / NOT MET] --------------------------------------------------------------- SECTION 3: STRENGTHS --------------------------------------------------------------- [List what the team did well -- specific decisions, fast activations, correct procedures followed. Be specific. Strengths tell leadership what to protect and reinforce.] STR-1: [Specific strength with brief description of the observed evidence.] STR-2: [Specific strength with brief description of the observed evidence.] --------------------------------------------------------------- SECTION 4: AREAS FOR IMPROVEMENT (FINDINGS) --------------------------------------------------------------- [Paste your structured findings from Phase 4 here in full.] F-01: [Observation / Root Cause / Impact / Objective / Result] F-02: [Observation / Root Cause / Impact / Objective / Result] F-03: [Observation / Root Cause / Impact / Objective / Result] --------------------------------------------------------------- SECTION 5: IMPROVEMENT PLAN --------------------------------------------------------------- [This is the most important section. Every finding maps to at least one action. Every action has a named owner and a specific due date. No owner = no accountability. No date = no urgency. This section is reviewed at the re-test date to verify completion.] IP-01 (addresses F-01): Action: Update IR Plan Section 3.2 to name a primary and backup isolation authority. Verify both individuals have been briefed on their authority and acceptance. Owner: [Name, Title] Due date: [Specific date -- not "Q3" -- a calendar date] Status: Open IP-02 (addresses F-02): Action: Identify and document a Communications Lead for incident response events. Add their name, backup contact, and the media response protocol to IR Plan Section 5. Distribute updated plan to all participants. Owner: [Name, Title] Due date: [Specific date] Status: Open IP-03 (addresses F-0N): Action: [Specific action -- name the document section, tool, or process to change.] Owner: [Name, Title] Due date: [Specific date] Status: Open --------------------------------------------------------------- SECTION 6: RE-TEST DATE --------------------------------------------------------------- NOTE: This AAR and Improvement Plan should be completed and distributed within two weeks of the exercise. Do not wait for the re-test date to document findings. The highest-priority findings (F-01, F-02) will be re-tested in a follow-on exercise no later than: [DATE -- typically 6-12 months from this exercise]. Responsible for scheduling: [Name, Title] --------------------------------------------------------------- SECTION 7: DISTRIBUTION AND RETENTION --------------------------------------------------------------- This document is [INTERNAL / CONFIDENTIAL]. Distribution is limited to participants and their direct management chain. The AAR is retained for [X] years per [policy name or regulatory requirement]. Do not distribute externally without legal review. ---------------------------------------------------------------
The re-test date is not optional -- and the AAR cannot wait. HSEEP guidance calls for completing the AAR and Improvement Plan within weeks of the exercise, not months -- findings fade, participants leave, and context is lost if you wait. The 6-12 month re-test window refers to the next exercise, not to when you write the AAR. Write and distribute the AAR within two weeks of the exercise. Schedule the next exercise before the current AAR is distributed. An improvement plan without a re-test date is not an improvement plan -- it is a list of intentions.
GRC trap -- Findings with no owners or vague due dates: "IT will update the IR plan by end of Q2" has no owner and no accountability. "Jane Doe, IT Director, will update IR Plan Sections 3.2 and 5 by July 15, 2026" is actionable. If a finding has no name attached, it will not be completed. If it has no date, it will be deprioritized whenever something urgent appears -- which is always. Name every owner. Set every date.
GRC trap -- AAR with findings but no IR plan update: The exercise exists to improve the plan. If none of the improvement actions feed back into the IR plan itself, the exercise was a compliance checkbox, not a real improvement loop. At minimum, one action item must update, correct, or expand the IR plan. Verify this before distributing the AAR.
GRC trap -- Never closing the loop on action items: The most common post-exercise failure is that the AAR is written, distributed, praised -- and then nothing happens. The IP actions sit in someone's inbox. The re-test date comes and goes without a follow-up exercise. Schedule the re-test before the AAR leaves the room. Assign a named person to track IP status monthly. An unreviewed AAR is evidence that your organization views exercises as theater.
Checkpoint — the deliverable
  • An AAR with prioritized actions, each having an owner and a due date
  • At least one action updates the IR plan itself, closing the loop
