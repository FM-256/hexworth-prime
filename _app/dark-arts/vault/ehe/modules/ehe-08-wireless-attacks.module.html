Wireless Attacks and Countermeasures

EHE • Module 08 • Wireless Security

Wireless networks extend the attack surface beyond physical perimeters into the air. The radio frequency medium is inherently broadcast — every device within range can receive every frame. From WEP's catastrophically broken cryptography to modern KRACK attacks on WPA2 and Bluetooth vulnerabilities that enable zero-interaction device compromise, wireless security demands both rigorous protocol knowledge and disciplined configuration practice.

Section 01

📶 Wireless Standards

The IEEE 802.11 family of standards defines Wi-Fi communication protocols, with each generation introducing new frequency bands, modulation techniques, and throughput capabilities. From a security perspective, different standards have different cryptographic options and protocol design characteristics that affect their attack surface.

Standard Marketing Name Frequency Max Speed Security Notes
802.11b Wi-Fi 1 2.4 GHz 11 Mbps Legacy; WEP only in original deployments. 2.4 GHz congested and susceptible to interference.
802.11a Wi-Fi 2 5 GHz 54 Mbps 5 GHz less penetrating through walls — shorter effective range for attackers outside building perimeter.
802.11g Wi-Fi 3 2.4 GHz 54 Mbps WPA introduced as WEP replacement. Backward compatibility with 802.11b preserved legacy security weaknesses.
802.11n Wi-Fi 4 2.4/5 GHz 600 Mbps WPA2 mandatory. MIMO antennas. Dual-band operation. Still widely deployed and targeted.
802.11ac Wi-Fi 5 5 GHz 3.5 Gbps WPA2/WPA3. MU-MIMO, beamforming. 5 GHz only limits signal propagation and eavesdropping range.
802.11ax Wi-Fi 6/6E 2.4/5/6 GHz 9.6 Gbps WPA3 native. OFDMA for dense environments. 6 GHz band (6E) restricted to WPA3. Enhanced PMF mandatory.
802.16 (WiMAX) WiMAX 2-66 GHz 75 Mbps Metropolitan area network standard. Uses 802.16e for mobile. PKMv2 authentication, AES encryption in modern deployments.
802.15.1 (Bluetooth) Bluetooth 2.4 GHz 3 Mbps Short range (10-100m). Frequency hopping spread spectrum (FHSS). Separate attack taxonomy — Bluejacking, Bluesnarfing, Bluebugging.
Section 02

🔒 Wireless Encryption Protocols

Wireless encryption protects the confidentiality of data transmitted over radio frequency links. The evolution from WEP to WPA3 reflects a decades-long process of patching and re-engineering cryptographic protocols as each generation's weaknesses were discovered and exploited. Understanding each protocol's design enables precise identification of what attacks are feasible against each.

WEP
Broken / Deprecated
RC4 + 24-bit IV | 64/128-bit key
Wired Equivalent Privacy (1997). The 24-bit Initialization Vector (IV) space is too small — IVs repeat within minutes on busy networks. FMS, KoreK, and PTW attacks recover the WEP key from captured IV packets in seconds. A WEP network can be cracked in under 60 seconds using modern tools. Never deploy.
WPA
Weak / Avoid
TKIP + RC4 | 128-bit per-packet key
Wi-Fi Protected Access (2003) — emergency patch for WEP using TKIP (Temporal Key Integrity Protocol) that added per-packet key mixing and message integrity checking (MIC). TKIP was designed to run on WEP hardware — RC4's fundamental weaknesses remain. TKIP is deprecated and attackable via Beck-Tews and Ohigashi-Morii attacks.
WPA2
Acceptable (with caveats)
AES-CCMP | 128-bit block cipher
Wi-Fi Protected Access 2 (2004). Replaced TKIP with AES-CCMP (Counter Mode CBC-MAC Protocol) — a strong cipher. Vulnerable to: offline dictionary attacks against the 4-way handshake (PMKID capture), KRACK (Key Reinstallation Attack, 2017), PMKID attack (Steube, 2018). Personal mode (PSK) remains weak against brute-force with captured handshakes.
WPA3
Recommended
SAE + AES-GCMP-256 | ECDH key exchange
Wi-Fi Protected Access 3 (2018). SAE (Simultaneous Authentication of Equals, also called Dragonfly) replaces PSK for personal mode — eliminates offline dictionary attacks by requiring online brute force (rate-limitable). Forward secrecy: captured traffic cannot be decrypted even if the password is later compromised. Enhanced PMF (Protected Management Frames) mandatory — prevents deauthentication attacks.
ℹ
TKIP vs AES/CCMP
TKIP is a wrapper around RC4 that was designed to be backward-compatible with WEP hardware. AES-CCMP uses the Advanced Encryption Standard in Counter Mode (for confidentiality) combined with CBC-MAC (for integrity). AES was specifically designed with hardware acceleration in mind and provides substantially stronger security guarantees. WPA2 with TKIP mode is considered deprecated — always configure AES/CCMP when WPA2 is required.
Section 03

🔫 Wireless Attack Types

🔌
Rogue Access Point
An unauthorized AP connected to the corporate network — either planted by an attacker with physical access or established by an employee for convenience. Bypasses network access controls, providing a wireless entry point into a wired corporate network. Detected via WIDS or periodic wireless surveys.
Infrastructure
🗣
Evil Twin Attack
Creates a fake AP broadcasting the same SSID as a legitimate network with a stronger signal. Clients deauthenticated from the real AP associate with the attacker's AP. The attacker captures credentials (especially on WPA-Enterprise), serves fake login pages, or performs SSL stripping on client traffic.
Impersonation
💥
Deauthentication Attack
IEEE 802.11 management frames are unauthenticated and unencrypted (prior to WPA3 PMF). Attackers can forge deauthentication frames with the victim's MAC address, forcibly disconnecting them. Used to force reconnection (capturing the 4-way handshake) or as a denial-of-service. Enabled by: aireplay-ng -0.
DoS / Handshake
🔐
WEP Cracking
The FMS/KoreK/PTW attacks against WEP recover the RC4 key by collecting IVs from captured frames. With passive capture and traffic injection to accelerate IV collection, a 40-bit WEP key can be recovered from approximately 20,000-40,000 frames — achievable in under two minutes with traffic injection.
Key Recovery
🔐
WPA/WPA2 Cracking
Captures the 4-way handshake (client + AP authentication exchange) during association, then performs offline dictionary/brute-force attacks against the PMK (Pairwise Master Key). The PMKID attack (2018) enables handshake-free cracking from a single packet. GPU acceleration: hashcat processes billions of candidates per second.
Offline Brute-Force
⚡
KRACK Attack
Key Reinstallation Attack (Mathy Vanhoef, 2017). Exploits a flaw in the WPA2 4-way handshake by retransmitting handshake message 3. Forces nonce reuse, breaking AES-CCMP confidentiality and enabling decryption of encrypted Wi-Fi traffic. Patched via firmware updates; unpatched devices remain vulnerable.
Protocol Flaw
⚠
WPA2 4-Way Handshake Attack Flow
1) Put wireless adapter in monitor mode. 2) Identify target network BSSID and channel. 3) Capture traffic on target channel. 4) Send deauthentication frames to force client reconnection. 5) Capture 4-way handshake (EAPOL frames). 6) Run offline dictionary attack against captured handshake using aircrack-ng or hashcat. 7) If PSK is in the wordlist, it will be recovered. Countermeasure: strong, random passphrase (20+ characters) makes offline attack computationally infeasible.
Section 04

🛠 Wireless Attack Tools

The Aircrack-ng suite is the standard toolkit for wireless security assessments on Linux (included in Kali Linux). Each component handles a specific phase of the wireless attack workflow, from enabling monitor mode through to key recovery.

airmon-ng
Monitor Mode Management
Enables or disables monitor mode on wireless adapters. Monitor mode allows a NIC to capture all nearby 802.11 frames regardless of BSSID destination.
$ sudo airmon-ng start wlan0 # Creates interface wlan0mon $ sudo airmon-ng stop wlan0mon
airodump-ng
Traffic Capture & AP Discovery
Captures raw 802.11 frames. Displays nearby APs (BSSID, SSID, channel, encryption), associated clients, and signal strength. Writes captures to .cap/.pcap files.
$ airodump-ng wlan0mon $ airodump-ng -c 6 --bssid AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF \ -w capture wlan0mon
aireplay-ng
Frame Injection
Injects 802.11 frames to manipulate wireless traffic. Key attacks: deauthentication (forces reconnection for handshake capture), fake authentication, ARP replay (accelerates WEP IV collection), chopchop attack.
# Deauth attack (forces handshake) $ aireplay-ng -0 5 -a AP_MAC \ -c CLIENT_MAC wlan0mon
aircrack-ng
Key Recovery
Statistical WEP key recovery from captured IVs. Dictionary/brute-force attack against WPA/WPA2 handshakes. Computes PMKID-based attacks. Integrates with hashcat for GPU-accelerated cracking.
# WPA2 dictionary attack $ aircrack-ng -w wordlist.txt \ capture-01.cap
Kismet
Wireless IDS / Scanning
Passive wireless network detector, sniffer, and intrusion detection system. Identifies all nearby 802.11 networks without transmitting, making it highly stealthy. Detects rogue APs and unusual frames. Exports to PCAP.
$ kismet -c wlan0mon # Web UI: http://localhost:2501
Wifite
Automated Wireless Cracking
Fully automated wireless auditing tool. Enumerates nearby networks, selects targets, captures handshakes, and runs cracking attacks automatically. Designed for efficiency in penetration testing engagements.
$ sudo wifite --wpa --dict \ passwords.txt
⚠
Legal Boundaries
Wireless security assessment tools are dual-use instruments. Using these tools against any wireless network without explicit written authorization from the network owner is illegal under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), the UK Computer Misuse Act, and equivalent statutes globally. Many activities — capturing traffic, injecting deauthentication frames, attempting key recovery — are criminal offenses when performed without authorization. All practical exercises must be conducted on networks you own or have explicit permission to test.
Section 05

🔌 Bluetooth Threats

Bluetooth (IEEE 802.15.1) uses frequency hopping spread spectrum (FHSS) across 79 channels in the 2.4 GHz band, hopping 1,600 times per second — a design that complicates passive interception but does not eliminate it. Bluetooth attacks target both the protocol's authentication mechanisms and implementation vulnerabilities in device firmware.

Bluejacking
Sending unsolicited messages (vCards, calendar invitations, plain text) to nearby discoverable Bluetooth devices. No data theft or access occurs — a nuisance/social engineering vector. Victim must be within Bluetooth range (typically 10-100m).
Low Impact
Bluesnarfing
Unauthorized access to data on a Bluetooth-enabled device via the OBEX Push protocol. Attackers can retrieve contacts, calendar entries, emails, and messages without pairing. Exploits authentication bypass vulnerabilities in older Bluetooth implementations.
High Impact
Bluebugging
Gains full control of a Bluetooth device — making calls, sending SMS, listening via microphone, reading messages. Exploits AT command channel access vulnerabilities. More severe than bluesnarfing. First demonstrated by Martin Herfurt in 2004.
Critical
KNOB Attack
Key Negotiation of Bluetooth (2019). Forces Bluetooth BR/EDR to negotiate minimum entropy (1 byte) for encryption keys, enabling brute-force decryption of intercepted traffic in real time. Affects Bluetooth core specification; patched via firmware updates.
Critical
BlueBorne
Collection of zero-click RCE vulnerabilities (Armis, 2017) affecting Bluetooth implementations across Windows, Linux, Android, and iOS. Attacker within range gains code execution without any user interaction or pairing. Estimated 5.3 billion devices affected.
Critical / Zero-Click
Bluetooth MITM
Exploits legacy Bluetooth pairing modes (Just Works, passkey entry) that lack mutual authentication. Attacker impersonates one device to each party in a connection, relaying and potentially modifying traffic. Mitigated by Bluetooth 5.x Secure Connections mode.
High Impact
✓
Bluetooth Hardening
Disable Bluetooth when not in use. Configure devices as non-discoverable except during explicit pairing. Reject unexpected pairing requests. Keep device firmware updated — BlueBorne and KNOB were addressed via firmware patches. Use Bluetooth Secure Simple Pairing (SSP) with Numeric Comparison or Out-of-Band methods rather than Just Works. In enterprise environments, restrict Bluetooth profiles via MDM policy.
Section 06

🔋 Jamming Attacks

Jamming is a physical-layer denial of service attack that disrupts wireless communication by broadcasting radio frequency noise on the target's operating frequencies. Unlike protocol-level DoS attacks that exploit software vulnerabilities, jamming attacks are effective against any wireless standard regardless of its cryptographic or protocol security properties.

🔋
RF Jamming
Broadband or spot frequency interference
▼

RF jamming transmits high-power noise or interference signals on the target frequency band. Types include: Spot jamming (single frequency), Sweep jamming (sweeps across a frequency range), Barrage jamming (covers an entire band simultaneously). Effective against 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and Zigbee simultaneously given band overlap. Physical-layer attack — cannot be mitigated by encryption or authentication.

RF jamming is illegal in most jurisdictions without government authorization (FCC Part 15 in the US prohibits devices that cause harmful interference). Detection requires spectrum analyzers or WIDS with RF noise floor monitoring.

💥
Deauthentication Flooding
Protocol-level jamming via management frame injection
▼

Prior to WPA3's mandatory Protected Management Frames (PMF), deauthentication frames are unauthenticated. Continuous injection of deauth frames against all clients on a target network prevents associations from persisting — clients are disconnected as fast as they can reconnect, creating a practical denial of service at the protocol layer without requiring physical RF power. More targeted and legally ambiguous than RF jamming, but equally effective against WPA2 networks.

🎗
Frequency Hopping as Evasion
FHSS design and its security implications
▼

Frequency Hopping Spread Spectrum (FHSS), used by Bluetooth and originally by some Wi-Fi standards, hops between frequencies in a pseudo-random pattern known to both communicating parties. This makes passive eavesdropping and spot jamming more difficult because the signal is only on any given frequency for a brief interval. However, barrage jamming that covers the entire band simultaneously remains effective regardless of hopping. Modern military and emergency communications use adaptive FHSS with classified hopping sequences for additional security.

Section 07

🛡 Wireless Countermeasures

🔒
WPA3 Deployment
WPA3-Personal with SAE eliminates offline dictionary attacks. WPA3-Enterprise with 192-bit security mode (CNSA suite) for high-assurance environments. Mandatory PMF eliminates deauthentication-based attacks. Migrate all infrastructure to WPA3-capable hardware.
Primary Defense
📈
WIDS / WIPS
Wireless Intrusion Detection/Prevention Systems continuously monitor the RF environment for rogue APs, evil twins, deauthentication floods, and anomalous frame patterns. WIPS can actively contain rogue APs by sending deauth frames to clients associating with them.
Detection
👤
802.1X / EAP Authentication
Port-based network access control. WPA2/3 Enterprise uses 802.1X with EAP (PEAP, EAP-TLS). Authenticates users/devices to a RADIUS server before granting network access. Eliminates shared PSK vulnerabilities — each user/device has unique credentials or certificate.
Enterprise Auth
🚫
MAC Filtering Limitations
MAC address filtering maintains an allowlist of authorized device MACs. It provides no meaningful security — MAC addresses are transmitted in cleartext and trivially spoofed (macchanger, ip link). Acceptable as a minor inconvenience layer but not a security control.
Weak Control
🔎
SSID Hiding Limitations
Disabling SSID broadcast (cloaking) prevents the AP from advertising its name in beacons. However, the SSID appears in probe requests from connecting clients and can be captured passively. SSID hiding provides security theater — it hides the network only from casual observers.
Security Theater
🌐
Network Segmentation
Guest, corporate, and IoT networks on separate VLANs with firewall rules between segments. Even if a wireless network is compromised, segmentation limits lateral movement. Captive portal for guest networks. Client isolation prevents peer-to-peer attacks on shared wireless networks.
Architecture
Section 08

⚡ Key Takeaways

📡
The Air Is a Shared Medium
Every wireless transmission is broadcast to all nearby devices. Encryption is not optional — it is the only barrier between transmissions and adjacent listeners. Treat wireless as inherently untrusted.
🔐
WEP Is Not Encryption
WEP provides a false sense of security. Any WEP-protected network should be treated as an open network. There is no valid use case for WEP in 2026. If hardware requires WEP, replace the hardware.
🔒
WPA3 + 802.1X Is the Target State
WPA3-Enterprise with 802.1X eliminates the shared secret vulnerability class, adds forward secrecy, and mandates PMF. This combination addresses most known wireless attack vectors.
🔌
Bluetooth Is Always in Range
BlueBorne required zero interaction to achieve RCE across billions of devices. Bluetooth attack surface exists whenever the radio is on. Disable when not needed, maintain firmware currency, and apply MDM policies in enterprise environments.

What does this tell us about the relationship between protocol security and implementation quality, and how should security engineers evaluate WPA3 deployment risk today?
  • 02 Evil twin attacks are particularly effective against employees connecting to corporate Wi-Fi from remote locations (hotels, coffee shops). Design a technical countermeasure architecture for a 500-person company that ensures employees cannot be silently MITM'd via evil twin, even when they believe they are on a trusted network.
  • 03 BlueBorne affected an estimated 5.3 billion devices across Android, iOS, Windows, and Linux with zero-click RCE. How does this vulnerability class illustrate the challenge of patching distributed embedded systems, and what systemic changes to the IoT/embedded device ecosystem would reduce the impact of future similar disclosures?
  • 04 MAC address filtering and hidden SSIDs are commonly cited in vendor documentation as "additional security measures" despite being trivially defeated. What professional and ethical obligations does a security consultant have when a client insists on deploying these controls as part of their wireless security strategy?
  • 05 The Aircrack-ng suite tools are freely available in Kali Linux and require no special hardware beyond a consumer wireless adapter. Given that a WPA2 network with a dictionary word passphrase can be cracked in minutes, what organizational policy and technical controls would you implement to protect wireless networks in a healthcare environment with HIPAA compliance obligations?
