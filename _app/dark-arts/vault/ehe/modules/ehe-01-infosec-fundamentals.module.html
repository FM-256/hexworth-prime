Module 01 • Information Security Fundamentals

The Architecture of Security

Before you can break security, you must understand what security is. This module establishes the conceptual framework used by every security professional: the CIA Triad, attack classifications, regulatory obligations, and the industry frameworks that structure organizational security programs.

Duration: 60 min
Sections: 7
Exam Weight: High
Level: Foundational
Section 01

▲ The CIA Triad

The CIA Triad is the foundational model of information security. Every security control, policy, and risk assessment maps back to one or more of its three components: Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability. Understanding the Triad is not an academic exercise — it is the lens through which every security decision is evaluated.

Attacks are most clearly understood as violations of one or more CIA properties. A data breach violates confidentiality. A man-in-the-middle attack that alters traffic violates integrity. A DDoS attack violates availability. Mapping attacks to Triad components sharpens both threat analysis and defensive control selection.

C
Confidentiality
Ensuring that information is accessible only to those authorized to access it. Data must be protected from unauthorized disclosure whether at rest, in transit, or in use.
Example: Encrypting a database of patient records so that unauthorized users cannot read the plaintext data even if they gain physical access to the server.
Eavesdropping Data Breach MITM Password Theft
I
Integrity
Ensuring that information is accurate, complete, and has not been altered by unauthorized parties. Data must be trustworthy from creation through deletion.
Example: Using cryptographic hash functions to verify that a software update package has not been tampered with between the vendor's server and the client.
MITM SQL Injection File Tampering Malware
A
Availability
Ensuring that information and systems are accessible to authorized users when needed. Downtime, whether caused by attack or failure, represents a security failure.
Example: Implementing redundant servers and DDoS mitigation services to ensure a banking platform remains accessible during volumetric attack campaigns.
DDoS Ransomware Resource Exhaustion
Beyond the Triad: The Parkerian Hexad
Some security models extend the CIA Triad with three additional properties: Possession/Control (ownership of data even without access), Authenticity (verifying origin), and Utility (data being in a usable form). The EHE exam focuses on the CIA Triad, but awareness of extended models demonstrates deeper professional understanding.
Section 02

🔐 Additional Security Concepts

The CIA Triad defines the goals of information security, but a complete professional vocabulary includes several additional concepts that govern access control, accountability, and trust. These properties underpin identity and access management (IAM) systems and are frequently tested on certification exams.

🔓
Authentication
Verifying the identity of a user, system, or entity. Authentication factors: something you know (password), something you have (token), something you are (biometric). Multi-factor authentication combines two or more categories.
Identity
📑
Authorization
Determining what an authenticated entity is permitted to do. Implemented via access control lists (ACL), role-based access control (RBAC), and mandatory access control (MAC) models. Distinct from authentication.
Access Control
🖌
Non-Repudiation
Ensuring that a party cannot deny performing an action or sending a message. Achieved through digital signatures, audit logs, and certificate-based authentication. Critical in legal and financial contexts.
Accountability
📋
Accountability
Tracing actions back to a responsible entity through audit trails and logs. Requires systems to record who did what, when, and from where. Forms the evidentiary foundation for incident response and forensic investigation.
Audit
🔎
Identification
The process of claiming an identity before authentication. A username is an identifier; the password that proves ownership of that username is the authenticator. Identification precedes authentication in every login flow.
IAM
🖧
Privacy
The right of individuals to control the collection, use, and disclosure of their personal information. Distinct from confidentiality: confidentiality protects data from unauthorized access; privacy governs how data may be used even by authorized parties.
Governance
Section 03

⚡ Information Security Attack Vectors

An attack vector is the path or means by which an attacker gains access to a target system in order to deliver a payload or exploit a vulnerability. Modern threat landscapes are characterized by the diversity of available attack vectors. Understanding each category informs the design of layered defenses.

☁
Cloud Threats
Misconfigurations in cloud storage (public S3 buckets), insecure APIs, identity and access management failures, account hijacking, and shared tenancy vulnerabilities in multi-tenant environments.
Infrastructure
🤖
Botnets
Networks of compromised machines (bots) controlled by a command-and-control server. Used to launch DDoS attacks, distribute spam, conduct credential stuffing, and mine cryptocurrency at scale.
Network
🎯
Advanced Persistent Threats
Long-duration, targeted intrusion campaigns typically attributed to nation-state actors. Characterized by low-and-slow movement, custom tooling, and deep network persistence lasting months or years.
Nation-State
🐛
Malware
Malicious software encompassing viruses, worms, trojans, spyware, adware, rootkits, and fileless malware. Delivered through phishing, drive-by downloads, removable media, and supply chain compromise.
Endpoint
🔒
Ransomware
Malware that encrypts victim data and demands payment for decryption keys. Modern ransomware operations combine data encryption with exfiltration (double extortion) to maximize leverage against victims.
Financial
💡
IoT Threats
Connected devices with poor security hygiene: default credentials, unpatched firmware, unencrypted communications, and no update mechanisms. IoT devices are prime botnet recruitment targets.
Hardware
👤
Insider Threats
Threats originating from employees, contractors, or partners with legitimate access. Can be malicious (deliberate theft/sabotage) or negligent (accidental data exposure). Often the most damaging category.
Human
📧
Phishing
Social engineering delivered via email, SMS (smishing), or voice (vishing). Designed to trick recipients into revealing credentials, clicking malicious links, or transferring funds. Spear phishing targets specific individuals.
Social Engineering
Section 04

📈 Classification of Attacks

Attacks are classified along multiple axes. The most common classification schema used in the EHE curriculum organizes attacks by their operational characteristics and the position of the attacker relative to the target. These classifications directly inform the selection of defensive countermeasures.

Category Type Characteristics Examples
Operational Mode Passive Attacker observes/intercepts without altering data. Difficult to detect because no system state changes. Network sniffing, traffic analysis, shoulder surfing, open-source reconnaissance
Operational Mode Active Attacker modifies data, disrupts services, or interacts with the target. Leaves artifacts and logs. DoS/DDoS, MITM, SQL injection, exploitation, malware installation
Attacker Position Close-In Attacker must be physically proximate to the target. Requires physical access or wireless range. Evil twin AP, USB drop attacks, RFID skimming, physical lock bypass, tailgating
Attacker Position Insider Attacker has legitimate credentials or physical access. Hardest to detect due to authorized baseline behavior. Privilege abuse, data exfiltration, sabotage, credential sharing, policy violations
Delivery Method Distribution Attack is embedded in hardware or software during the supply chain before delivery to the victim. Trojanized firmware, compromised software updates, hardware implants, counterfeit hardware
Exam Focus
The EHE exam frequently presents scenarios requiring you to classify an attack type. Key distinctions: passive attacks do not alter data (making them hard to detect); active attacks do alter data or disrupt services (making them detectable but often more damaging immediately). Close-in attacks require physical proximity; distribution attacks occur before delivery.
Section 05

⚖️ Information Security Laws and Regulations

Security professionals do not operate in a legal vacuum. A growing body of legislation and regulation imposes specific obligations on organizations that collect, process, or store personal and sensitive data. Violation of these frameworks carries substantial civil and criminal penalties.

HIPAA
Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act
Governs the protection of Protected Health Information (PHI) in the United States. Requires covered entities and business associates to implement administrative, physical, and technical safeguards.
US Healthcare
PCI-DSS
Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard
Industry-driven standard governing organizations that process payment card data. Mandates encryption, access controls, network segmentation, and regular vulnerability scanning.
Global Financial
SOX
Sarbanes-Oxley Act
US law requiring publicly traded companies to maintain accurate financial records and implement IT controls that ensure data integrity and auditability of financial systems.
US Public Companies
FISMA
Federal Information Security Management Act
Requires US federal agencies to develop, implement, and maintain information security programs. Agencies must follow NIST guidelines and conduct annual security reviews.
US Federal
DMCA
Digital Millennium Copyright Act
Prohibits circumvention of technological protection measures on copyrighted works. Security researchers must navigate DMCA exemptions when conducting legitimate vulnerability research.
US Copyright
GDPR
General Data Protection Regulation
EU regulation governing the processing of personal data of EU residents. Applies to any organization handling EU citizen data regardless of location. Fines up to 4% of global annual revenue.
EU / Global
Compliance vs. Security
A critical distinction: compliance is not security. An organization that meets all HIPAA requirements may still be successfully breached. Compliance frameworks define minimum requirements; genuine security requires continuous risk management that often exceeds compliance mandates. Ethical hackers frequently uncover vulnerabilities in organizations that claim to be compliant.
Section 06

🏛 Cybersecurity Frameworks

Cybersecurity frameworks provide structured guidance for building and managing security programs. Unlike regulatory requirements, which mandate specific actions, frameworks are voluntary guidelines that organizations adapt to their risk profile and business context. Three frameworks dominate the enterprise security landscape.

NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)

Developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology, the NIST CSF provides a risk-based approach to managing cybersecurity. It is organized around five core functions that represent a complete security lifecycle. Version 2.0 (released 2024) added a sixth function: Govern.

Govern
Identify
Protect
Detect
Respond
Recover

Each function is broken into categories and subcategories that map to specific security controls and outcomes. The framework is sector-agnostic and widely adopted by both government agencies and private sector organizations. It does not mandate specific technologies or configurations.

ISO/IEC 27001

The international standard for Information Security Management Systems (ISMS). ISO 27001 defines requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continuously improving a formal security management program. Organizations can pursue third-party certification through accredited registrars.

Context
Leadership
Planning
Support
Operation
Evaluation
Improvement

Annex A of ISO 27001 contains 93 controls organized into four themes: Organizational, People, Physical, and Technological. ISO 27001 certification is globally recognized and often required in enterprise vendor assessments and government contracts.

CIS Controls v8

The Center for Internet Security (CIS) Controls are 18 prioritized, actionable security controls developed from real-world attack data. Unlike ISO 27001 (management system) or NIST CSF (lifecycle framework), CIS Controls are prescriptive technical actions ranked by their effectiveness at stopping known attacks.

Inventory Assets
Patch Software
Data Protection
Secure Config
Access Control
Audit Logging

CIS Controls are organized into three Implementation Groups (IG1, IG2, IG3) that match organizational size and security maturity. IG1 represents a minimum standard of baseline cyber hygiene. Research indicates that implementing the top 5 CIS Controls alone prevents approximately 85% of common attacks.

Section 07

💡 Key Takeaways

Information security fundamentals are the vocabulary of the profession. Every conversation with a client, every finding in a penetration test report, and every defensive recommendation will reference these concepts. Fluency in this foundational layer accelerates understanding of every more advanced topic in this course.

01 The CIA Triad is universal. Every security decision — which control to implement, how to prioritize remediation — maps back to protecting confidentiality, integrity, or availability.
02 Authentication and authorization are distinct. Authentication proves identity; authorization determines what that identity may do. Conflating them is a common source of access control vulnerabilities.
03 Passive attacks are silent. Network sniffing leaves no system logs. Detecting passive attacks requires network-level monitoring such as IDS/IPS, anomaly detection, and encrypted traffic analysis.
04 Compliance does not equal security. Regulatory frameworks establish floors, not ceilings. Ethical hackers routinely find vulnerabilities in organizationally compliant environments.
05 GDPR has global reach. Any organization handling EU resident data falls under GDPR jurisdiction regardless of where the organization is headquartered. This makes GDPR the most broadly applicable privacy law.
06 Frameworks provide structure; controls provide implementation. NIST CSF defines what to achieve; CIS Controls define specific actions to take. Mature organizations use frameworks to govern and controls to execute.
💬 Discussion Prompts

Prompt 1: A ransomware attack encrypts all files on a hospital's file servers and deletes backups. Using the CIA Triad, identify which property (or properties) are violated, explain the impact of each violation, and describe what controls could have prevented each violation.

Prompt 2: An employee at a financial firm copies confidential client data to a personal USB drive before resigning, then takes a job at a competitor. Classify this attack using the attack classification framework from Section 04. Which CIA properties are violated? Which regulations may have been breached?

Prompt 3: An organization is trying to decide between adopting NIST CSF, ISO 27001, or CIS Controls as their security framework. They are a mid-sized healthcare company with 500 employees. What factors would you consider in making a recommendation, and which framework would you suggest first?
