Bug Bounty Lifecycle

Bug Hunting • Bounty Process • Start to Finish

Bug bounty programs are structured agreements between organizations and security researchers. The organization defines what can be tested, the researcher finds and reports vulnerabilities, and the organization pays for valid findings. This module covers the entire lifecycle — from reading a program scope to receiving payment — and the legal, ethical, and practical frameworks that make it all work.

Section 01

What is Bug Bounty?

A bug bounty program is a deal: an organization invites security researchers to find vulnerabilities in their systems, and in return, offers monetary rewards for valid findings. It is crowdsourced security testing at scale — thousands of researchers with diverse skill sets probing the same targets, often finding what internal security teams miss.

A Brief History

The concept predates the modern platforms. Netscape launched the first formal bug bounty in 1995, paying researchers to find flaws in Navigator 2.0. Mozilla continued the tradition. But the industry transformed in the early 2010s when platforms emerged to broker the relationship between companies and researchers.

Year Milestone Significance
1995 Netscape Bug Bounty First corporate bug bounty program; paid cash for Navigator browser vulnerabilities
2004 Mozilla Bug Bounty Formalized the open-source bug bounty model; $500 per critical vulnerability
2010 Google VRP Launch First major tech company to run a continuous, well-funded bounty program
2012 HackerOne Founded Platform model: companies post programs, researchers submit through a unified interface
2012 Bugcrowd Founded Managed bounty model with curated researcher pools and triage services
2016 DOD "Hack the Pentagon" First US government bug bounty; legitimized the practice for public sector
2020+ Industry Maturation $100M+ paid annually across platforms; full-time bug bounty hunting becomes viable

Legal Framework

Bug bounty programs exist within a specific legal context. Without authorization, security testing is a crime under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) in the US and similar legislation globally. Bug bounty programs provide that authorization through their policy documents.

✓ Authorized Testing

  • ▶ Testing within a published bug bounty scope
  • ▶ Following the program's rules of engagement
  • ▶ Reporting findings through official channels
  • ▶ Safe harbor clause protects the researcher
  • ▶ Coordinated disclosure with the vendor

✗ Unauthorized Testing

  • ▶ Testing systems without explicit permission
  • ▶ Exceeding scope boundaries defined in the program
  • ▶ Accessing or exfiltrating real user data
  • ▶ Public disclosure before the vendor patches
  • ▶ Exploiting findings for personal gain beyond the bounty
Legal Boundaries Are Non-Negotiable
"I was just testing" is not a legal defense. Always verify the program scope before testing. If a target does not have a bug bounty program or vulnerability disclosure policy, do not test it. The line between security research and criminal hacking is defined entirely by authorization.

Stage 01: Scope Review
Read the program policy thoroughly before touching anything. Identify in-scope domains, IP ranges, and application types. Note exclusions — certain vulnerability classes (DoS, social engineering) are almost always out of scope. Check for special instructions like required headers, VPN access, or test accounts. Many researchers skip this step and waste hours testing out-of-scope assets or reporting excluded vulnerability types.
ℹ
Time Allocation
Professional researchers typically spend 40-60% of their time on recon and documentation, not active exploitation. The hunting phase is often the shortest part of the cycle — the preparation and reporting around it determine whether a finding gets accepted and paid.
Section 03

Scope Analysis

The scope document is the contract. It defines what you can test, how you can test it, and what counts as a valid finding. Misreading scope is the most common reason reports get rejected. Hover over each line of the sample scope document below to see annotations.

Program: Acme Corporation Bug Bounty
The program name identifies the organization. Check if they have multiple programs (main product, API, mobile app) with different scopes.
Safe Harbor: Yes — we will not pursue legal action against researchers who follow this policy.
Safe harbor is your legal protection. Programs without safe harbor carry risk. Prefer programs that explicitly state they will not pursue legal action against researchers who comply with the policy.

In Scope:
Everything listed here is fair game for testing. If it is not listed, it is out of scope by default.
  *.acme.com  — All subdomains
Wildcard scope is the most valuable. This means any subdomain you discover through recon is a valid target. Forgotten dev servers, staging environments, and legacy apps are all fair game.
  api.acme.com/v2/*  — REST API v2
Only API v2 is in scope. Testing v1 or v3 endpoints would be out of scope. Pay attention to version-specific restrictions.
  iOS / Android apps  — Latest versions only
Mobile apps are in scope, but only current versions. Vulnerabilities in deprecated versions will likely be rejected as known issues.

Out of Scope:
Testing these assets or reporting these vulnerability types will result in report rejection and possible program ban.
  blog.acme.com  — Third-party hosted
Hosted on a third-party platform (e.g., WordPress.com, Medium). Vulnerabilities here are the platform vendor's responsibility, not Acme's.
  DoS / DDoS / Rate limiting
Nearly every program excludes denial of service. Testing availability impacts real users and infrastructure. This is a universal exclusion.
  Social engineering / Phishing
Targeting employees with phishing or social engineering is always off-limits in bug bounty. This tests people, not systems.
  Self-XSS, logout CSRF, missing headers without impact
Low-impact or theoretical findings. Self-XSS requires the victim to paste code into their own console. Logout CSRF has negligible impact. Missing security headers without demonstrated exploitation are noise.

Asset Types

Web Applications
The primary target for most programs. Includes main domains, subdomains, admin panels, customer portals, and single-page applications. Testing covers authentication, authorization, injection, business logic, and more.
Most Common
APIs
REST, GraphQL, gRPC, and SOAP endpoints. API testing often yields higher-severity findings because APIs lack the input validation layers that web frontends enforce. Documentation (Swagger, OpenAPI) is your starting point.
High Value
Mobile Applications
iOS and Android apps. Testing includes API traffic interception, local storage analysis, binary reverse engineering, deep link handling, and certificate pinning bypass. Fewer researchers test mobile, so competition is lower.
Less Competition
Infrastructure
IP ranges, cloud services, VPN endpoints, mail servers. Infrastructure scope is rarer but can yield critical findings — exposed databases, misconfigured cloud storage, and unpatched services.
Rare but Critical
Section 04

The Hunt

Methodology separates consistent earners from researchers who stumble into occasional findings. The best hunters follow structured frameworks while leaving room for creative intuition — the instinct that comes from testing thousands of applications.

Methodology Frameworks

Framework Focus Bug Bounty Relevance
OWASP Testing Guide Web application security testing The de facto checklist. Covers 90+ test cases organized by category. Start here for every web target.
PTES Penetration testing execution Broader scope (pre-engagement through reporting). Useful for structuring the overall approach.
OWASP ASVS Application Security Verification Defines security requirements by level. Helps identify what should be tested at each assurance level.
Bug Hunter's Methodology Bug bounty specific Community-driven methodology (jhaddix). Practical recon-to-report workflow built for bounty hunting.

Systematic vs. Creative Approaches

Systematic Testing

  • ▶ Follow a checklist methodically across every endpoint
  • ▶ Test every parameter for injection, every endpoint for auth bypass
  • ▶ High coverage, catches common vulnerabilities
  • ▶ Risk: duplicates, since other researchers follow the same checklists

Creative Hunting

  • ▶ Focus on business logic, race conditions, and chain attacks
  • ▶ Look for unique application behavior, not textbook vulnerabilities
  • ▶ Lower coverage, but finds that others miss
  • ▶ Advantage: fewer duplicates, higher severity potential

Time Management

Bug bounty hunting has no guaranteed hourly rate. Time management is the difference between a hobby that occasionally pays and a viable income source. Professional hunters track their time per program, calculate effective hourly rates, and drop programs where the math does not work. If you spend 20 hours and find nothing, reassess the target, not your skills.

✓
The 80/20 Rule
Most successful researchers focus on 3-5 programs rather than spreading across dozens. Deep familiarity with a target's architecture, naming conventions, and development patterns lets you spot anomalies faster. When a program ships new features, you are first to test them because you already understand the baseline.
Section 05

Documentation & Evidence

A vulnerability without documentation is a vulnerability that does not exist. The triage analyst reviewing your report was not looking over your shoulder when you found the bug. They need to reproduce it from your written instructions alone. Every piece of evidence you collect during testing directly supports your report.

What to Capture

  1. HTTP Requests and Responses — Raw request/response pairs from Burp Suite or browser DevTools. Include headers, cookies, and body content. These are the definitive proof of the vulnerability.
  2. Screenshots — Annotated screenshots showing the vulnerability in action. Highlight the relevant portion. Use sequential screenshots for multi-step exploits.
  3. Video Recording — For complex exploitation chains, a screen recording eliminates ambiguity. Keep it under 3 minutes. No narration needed if the steps are clear.
  4. Reproduction Steps — Numbered, unambiguous steps that a developer can follow. Assume zero context. Specify exact URLs, parameters, and payloads.
  5. Impact Evidence — Demonstrate what an attacker gains. Access to other users' data? Admin panel access? Server-side code execution? Show the impact, not just the technique.
Never Access Real User Data
When demonstrating impact, use your own test accounts. If you discover an IDOR that could access other users' data, demonstrate it by accessing your own secondary test account — not a real user. Accessing real user data, even to prove a vulnerability, crosses ethical and often legal boundaries.
Section 06

Triage & Communication

After submission, your report enters triage — the process where the security team (or platform triagers) validate, reproduce, and assess your finding. Understanding this process from the other side helps you write reports that move through it faster.

The Triage Pipeline

  1. Initial Review — A triager reads the report title and summary. Obvious spam, duplicates, and out-of-scope reports are closed here. Clear titles and summaries survive this filter.
  2. Reproduction — The triager follows your steps to reproduce. If they cannot reproduce, the report goes back to you for clarification. This is where detailed steps pay off.
  3. Severity Assessment — Valid findings are assigned a severity rating using CVSS or the program's own rubric. Your suggested severity may be adjusted up or down.
  4. Internal Routing — Validated reports are sent to the development team that owns the affected component. Bug fix priority depends on severity.
  5. Resolution — The development team patches the vulnerability. Some programs pay on triage (acceptance), others on resolution (fix deployed).

CVSS Severity Ratings

Rating CVSS Score Examples
Critical 9.0 – 10.0 Remote code execution, authentication bypass on admin, SQL injection with full DB access
High 7.0 – 8.9 Stored XSS on main domain, IDOR leaking PII, SSRF with internal network access
Medium 4.0 – 6.9 Reflected XSS requiring user interaction, information disclosure of internal paths
Low 0.1 – 3.9 Missing security headers with limited impact, verbose error messages, clickjacking on non-sensitive pages
Informational 0.0 Best practice recommendations, theoretical risks without demonstrated impact

Communication Best Practices

  • Be professional — triagers handle hundreds of reports. Clear, respectful communication gets faster responses.
  • Respond promptly — when triagers ask for clarification, respond within 24-48 hours. Stale reports get deprioritized.
  • Do not argue severity publicly — if you disagree with the assigned severity, provide additional impact evidence privately. Escalate through platform mediation if needed.
  • Handle duplicates gracefully — duplicates happen. If your report is marked duplicate, the earlier reporter gets the bounty. It is not personal. Move on to the next finding.

ℹ
Beyond the Score
CVSS provides a baseline, but many programs consider business impact separately. A medium-severity vulnerability in a payment processing flow may be rewarded higher than a high-severity finding in a marketing page. Always frame your report's impact in business terms, not just technical severity.
Discussion Prompts
  • 01 A company's bug bounty program excludes denial-of-service testing, but you discover an endpoint that crashes the entire server with a single malformed request. How do you handle this? What are the ethical considerations of even discovering this if you were not testing for DoS?
  • 02 You submit a critical vulnerability report and it is marked as a duplicate — someone reported it 2 hours before you. How does this affect your strategy going forward? Should you focus on speed, depth, or novel attack surfaces?
  • 03 Compare the economics of specializing in one vulnerability class (e.g., SSRF) across many programs versus deeply understanding one program's entire attack surface. What are the trade-offs of each approach?
