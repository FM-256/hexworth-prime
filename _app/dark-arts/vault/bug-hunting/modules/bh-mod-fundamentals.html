Bug Bounty Fundamentals

Bug Hunting • Module 01 • Foundations

Bug bounty programs have fundamentally changed how organizations approach security testing. Instead of relying solely on internal teams or periodic penetration tests, companies now invite thousands of independent researchers to continuously probe their assets for vulnerabilities. This module covers the ecosystem from the ground up: what bug bounties are, the platforms that host them, the legal boundaries you must respect, and the tools and mindset you need to find your first bug.

Section 01

🎯 What is Bug Bounty?

A bug bounty program is a deal that organizations offer to external security researchers: find a vulnerability in our systems, report it responsibly, and we will reward you. The reward can be monetary (ranging from $50 for a low-severity issue to $1,000,000+ for critical vulnerabilities in major platforms), or it can be recognition-based through hall-of-fame listings and public acknowledgment.

The concept is built on a simple economic insight: no internal security team, regardless of size, can match the collective skill and coverage of thousands of independent researchers working in parallel. A Fortune 500 company might employ 20 security engineers. A bug bounty program gives them access to 500,000+ researchers worldwide, each bringing different specializations, tools, and perspectives to the same attack surface.

A Brief History

The idea of paying for vulnerabilities is older than most people realize. Netscape launched the first formal bug bounty program in 1995, offering cash rewards to anyone who could find security flaws in Navigator 2.0. The program was the brainchild of engineer Jarrett Ridlinghafer, who recognized that the beta testing community was already finding bugs for free — formalizing the process with financial incentives would attract more skilled testers and produce higher-quality reports.

For the next decade, the idea lay mostly dormant. Mozilla revived it in 2004 with a $500 bounty for critical vulnerabilities in Firefox. Google launched its Vulnerability Reward Program (VRP) in 2010, and by 2013 the payout had grown so large that the company was spending millions per year on researcher rewards. Facebook, Microsoft, and Apple followed with their own programs. The founding of HackerOne in 2012 and Bugcrowd in 2012 created dedicated platforms that made bug bounties accessible to any organization, not just tech giants with the infrastructure to manage thousands of reports internally.

Today, the bug bounty industry is worth over $100 million annually in payouts. The U.S. Department of Defense runs "Hack the Pentagon" through HackerOne. The European Commission has funded bounty programs for open-source software. Every major tech company, most financial institutions, and an increasing number of government agencies operate some form of vulnerability disclosure or reward program.

Why Bug Bounties vs. Traditional Pentesting

▶ Traditional Penetration Testing

  • • Fixed scope, fixed timeline (1-4 weeks typical)
  • • 1-5 testers assigned to the engagement
  • • Quarterly or annual cadence
  • • Deliverable: formal report at engagement end
  • • Cost: $15,000-$100,000+ per engagement
  • • Testers move to next client after delivery

▶ Bug Bounty Program

  • • Always-on, continuous testing
  • • Hundreds to thousands of active researchers
  • • 24/7/365 coverage
  • • Pay only for valid, unique findings
  • • Cost scales with results, not time
  • • Researchers return to test new features

The two approaches are complementary, not competing. Penetration tests provide structured, methodology-driven assessments that satisfy compliance requirements (PCI-DSS, SOC 2, etc.). Bug bounties provide continuous coverage between assessments, catching vulnerabilities introduced by new deployments, configuration changes, and evolving attack techniques. The strongest security programs use both.

ℹ
Continuous Testing vs. Quarterly Assessments
Modern CI/CD pipelines deploy code multiple times per day. A quarterly penetration test evaluates a snapshot that may be hundreds of deployments out of date by the time the report is delivered. Bug bounty programs close this gap by maintaining constant researcher attention on production assets. When a new feature ships on Tuesday, researchers are testing it by Wednesday.
Section 02

🌐 Bug Bounty Platforms

While some organizations run self-hosted bug bounty programs (Google, Apple, Microsoft), most companies use a third-party platform to manage the process. These platforms handle researcher onboarding, report submission and triage, payment processing, and communication between researchers and security teams. Choosing the right platform matters because each has different program inventories, triage speeds, and community dynamics.

Major Platforms

🛡
HackerOne
The largest bug bounty platform by volume. Over 2,000 active programs including the U.S. Department of Defense, Goldman Sachs, Uber, Twitter, and Shopify. Features both public programs (anyone can participate) and private programs (invitation-only, based on researcher reputation). HackerOne's reputation system is the industry benchmark: Signal, Impact, and Reputation scores determine access to private programs.
Largest Platform
🐚
Bugcrowd
Known for its Vulnerability Rating Taxonomy (VRT), which standardizes severity classification across all programs. Bugcrowd offers managed programs where their internal triage team validates reports before forwarding to clients, reducing noise for organizations. Strong presence in financial services and healthcare verticals. Their "Crowd" researcher community tends to be smaller but more curated.
Managed Triage
🇪🇺
Intigriti
European-headquartered platform with a strong focus on GDPR-compliant programs. Known for high triage quality and fast response times. Hosts programs for European banks, telecom companies, and government agencies. Smaller program count than HackerOne but researchers report better communication and less duplicate friction.
European Focus
🔒
Synack
Invite-only platform with a rigorous vetting process for researchers (the Synack Red Team, or SRT). Applicants must pass technical assessments and background checks. In return, SRT members access high-value programs with significantly higher payouts. Synack provides its own testing platform with built-in reconnaissance tools and traffic logging. Used heavily by government and defense contractors.
Invite Only
🌎
YesWeHack
French-founded platform gaining momentum across Europe and Asia. Partners with the European Commission and French government agencies. Offers a training platform (DOJO) for researchers to practice on intentionally vulnerable applications. Growing program inventory with competitive payouts and fast triage.
EU/Asia Growing
🔓
Open Bug Bounty
Non-commercial platform focused on responsible disclosure. Researchers report vulnerabilities found in any website, and Open Bug Bounty coordinates with site owners. No monetary rewards by default, but site owners can choose to pay. Primarily covers XSS and other web vulnerabilities. Lower barrier to entry for beginners.

Section 03

📜 Program Types & Scope

Not all bug bounty programs are created equal. The type of program, its scope, and its rules of engagement determine what you can test, how you can test it, and what happens when you find something. Misunderstanding scope is the fastest way to get banned from a platform or, worse, face legal consequences.

Program Types

▶ VDP (Vulnerability Disclosure Program)

  • • No monetary reward — recognition only
  • • Legal safe harbor for good-faith testing
  • • Often run by government agencies
  • • Good for building a public track record
  • • Lower competition than paid programs

▶ Paid Bug Bounty Program

  • • Cash rewards based on severity
  • • Defined payout ranges per vulnerability class
  • • Higher competition from skilled researchers
  • • Tighter scope and stricter rules
  • • Managed or self-hosted on platforms

Programs are further divided by access level. Public programs are open to any registered researcher on the platform. Private programs require an invitation, typically extended based on your reputation score, past performance, and specialization. Private programs have significantly less competition — where a public program might have 10,000 active researchers, a private program may only invite 50-200 — which means less duplicate reports and higher acceptance rates.

Reading the Scope Document

Every bug bounty program publishes a scope document (also called a policy page). This is the single most important document you will read before testing. It defines exactly what is in-scope (you are authorized to test it) and out-of-scope (you are not authorized and may face consequences for testing it).

Common asset types in scope:

  • Web applications — the primary target for most programs (e.g., app.example.com)
  • Mobile applications — iOS and Android apps, usually the latest version from official stores
  • APIs — REST, GraphQL, and SOAP endpoints (e.g., api.example.com/v2/*)
  • Hardware / IoT — physical devices, firmware, embedded systems (rare, high payout)
  • Source code — open-source repositories on GitHub, usually for code-level vulnerabilities

Wildcard scopes like *.example.com mean that any subdomain of example.com is in scope. This is significant because it opens the door to subdomain enumeration as a reconnaissance technique — finding forgotten or development subdomains that the organization may not be actively monitoring. Wildcard scopes are where many researchers find their best bugs.

⚠
Always Read the Policy
Before you send a single packet to a target, read the entire scope document. Not the summary. Not the first paragraph. The entire document. Programs specify prohibited testing methods (no DoS, no social engineering against employees, no automated scanning without rate limits), data handling requirements (do not exfiltrate real user data), and reporting format expectations. Violating any of these can result in permanent bans, report rejection, and in extreme cases, legal action. The scope document is your authorization contract.

A typical scope document for a SaaS company might include:

In Scope:

  • *.example.com (all subdomains)
  • api.example.com/v2/*

  • Missing rate limiting without demonstrated impact
  • Clickjacking on non-sensitive pages
  • CSRF on logout functionality
  • Missing security headers without proof of exploitability
Section 04

👤 Setting Up Your Profile

Your profile on a bug bounty platform is your professional identity in this space. It determines which programs you can access, how triagers perceive your reports, and whether you get invited to lucrative private programs. Building reputation is a deliberate process that rewards consistency and quality over volume.

The HackerOne Reputation System

HackerOne uses three metrics to evaluate researchers. Reputation is a numerical score that increases when reports are resolved (triaged, bounty awarded) and decreases when reports are marked as Not Applicable (N/A), Informative, or Spam. Signal measures the percentage of your reports that are meaningful — valid, unique, and within scope. A high signal means you are not submitting noise. Impact tracks the average severity of your accepted reports — higher impact means you are finding critical bugs, not just informational issues.

Private program invitations start flowing consistently around 300-500 reputation points. The top researchers on the platform carry reputation scores in the tens of thousands. Your first 10 accepted reports establish your trajectory — make them count by prioritizing quality over quantity.

Profile Optimization

  • Use your real name or a consistent alias. Triagers see your username on every report. A professional-sounding handle builds trust.
  • Write a bio that signals competence. Mention your technical focus areas: web application security, API testing, mobile security. No need for embellishment — let your report history speak.
  • Link relevant credentials. OSCP, OSWE, CEH, or university coursework adds credibility but is not required. Many top researchers have no formal certifications.
  • Set your tax information early. Bounty payments are taxable income. Complete W-9 (US) or W-8BEN (international) forms when prompted to avoid payment delays.

Your First Targets

Not all programs are equally beginner-friendly. Large, well-funded programs attract the most skilled researchers, meaning competition is fierce and the obvious bugs have already been found. As a new researcher, prioritize these categories:

🏛
Government VDPs
The U.S. Department of Defense, NASA, and numerous federal agencies run VDPs on HackerOne. No monetary reward, but massive attack surfaces, less competition from profit-motivated researchers, and the accepted reports boost your reputation score and signal metrics.
Start Here
📚
Wide-Scope Programs
Programs with wildcard scope (*.example.com) give you room to explore. The main application may be hardened, but forgotten staging servers, documentation portals, and internal tools on subdomains often have weaker security postures. Subdomain enumeration becomes your primary advantage.
Opportunity
🚀
Newer Programs
Programs that launched within the last 30-60 days have not been picked over by experienced researchers yet. Sort by "newest" on HackerOne's directory to find fresh targets. The first wave of researchers on a new program typically finds the most and highest-severity vulnerabilities.
Low Competition
🛠
Open Source Projects
Projects like curl, Node.js, and Ruby on Rails run bounty programs and welcome code-level vulnerability reports. If you are comfortable reading source code, these programs offer a different (and less competitive) hunting ground than black-box web application testing.
Code Review
Section 05

⚖ Legal & Ethical Boundaries

Bug bounty hunting exists in a legal gray area that becomes black-and-white the moment you step outside the scope of a program's authorization. Understanding the legal framework is not optional — it is the difference between a career in security research and a federal indictment.

The Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA)

The CFAA (18 U.S.C. § 1030) is the primary federal law governing unauthorized computer access in the United States. Enacted in 1986 and amended multiple times since, it criminalizes accessing a computer "without authorization" or "exceeding authorized access." Violations can carry penalties of 5-20 years in prison and fines up to $250,000 depending on the severity and intent.

The key phrase is "without authorization." A bug bounty program's scope document is what grants you authorization. If the scope says *.example.com is in scope, you are authorized to test subdomains of example.com using the permitted methods described in the policy. The moment you test an out-of-scope asset, pivot to a system not covered by the program, or use a prohibited technique, you may be operating without authorization under the CFAA.

Safe Harbor Provisions

Most reputable bug bounty programs include a safe harbor clause in their policy. This is a written commitment that the organization will not pursue legal action against researchers who act in good faith, follow the program rules, and report vulnerabilities through proper channels. HackerOne's Gold Standard Safe Harbor language explicitly states that authorized testing under the program is "conduct authorized by the organization" and is not a violation of the CFAA.

However, safe harbor has limits. It protects you only for activities within the defined scope using permitted testing methods. It does not protect you if you:

  • Test assets explicitly marked as out of scope
  • Access, download, or store user data beyond what is necessary to demonstrate the vulnerability
  • Disclose vulnerability details publicly before the organization has had time to remediate
  • Use the vulnerability for personal gain beyond the bounty reward
  • Perform denial-of-service attacks unless the program explicitly permits it
  • Engage in social engineering or physical intrusion attempts

Data Handling

During testing, you may encounter real user data — email addresses, names, financial information, medical records. Your obligation is to minimize exposure and report responsibly:

  1. Stop exploiting the vulnerability as soon as you have enough evidence to write a clear report. Do not dump entire databases.
  2. Do not store real user data on your local machine. If you must screenshot evidence, redact all PII before including it in the report.
  3. Never share user data with anyone other than the program's security team through the official reporting channel.
  4. If you accidentally access sensitive data (medical, financial, credentials), immediately note this in your report and delete any local copies.
  5. Some programs require you to destroy all test data after the report is resolved. Comply with these requirements.

Responsible Disclosure Timelines

The industry standard for responsible disclosure is 90 days. After reporting a vulnerability, the organization has 90 days to develop and deploy a fix before the researcher is considered free to disclose publicly. Google's Project Zero formalized this timeline, and most bug bounty platforms enforce similar windows. Some programs specify their own timelines (30, 60, or 120 days) in their policy documents.

If the organization has not fixed the issue within the agreed timeline, the researcher may disclose — but this should be a last resort. In practice, most researchers work collaboratively with security teams, granting extensions when the fix is genuinely complex and the team is actively working on it.

⚠
Legal Consequences Are Real
Researchers have been prosecuted under the CFAA for testing without authorization, even when their intent was to help. In 2021, a Missouri reporter was investigated for viewing publicly accessible HTML source code on a state website. Authorization is not determined by intent — it is determined by what the program policy says you can do. When in doubt, do not test. Ask the program for clarification first. No bug bounty payout is worth a criminal record.
Section 06

🛠 Essential Toolkit

Bug bounty hunting does not require expensive equipment or obscure proprietary tools. The core toolkit is mostly open-source or has free tiers sufficient for effective research. What matters more than the specific tools is understanding what each category of tool does and when to use it in your workflow.

Browser & Developer Tools

Your browser is your primary testing instrument. Both Firefox and Chrome ship with developer tools (F12) that provide a network inspector, JavaScript console, DOM inspector, and storage viewer. For bug bounty work, the Network tab is where you spend the most time: watching HTTP requests and responses, inspecting cookies and headers, and identifying API endpoints that the frontend communicates with.

Firefox is generally preferred for security research because it integrates cleanly with proxy tools (no system-level proxy configuration needed with FoxyProxy), has better built-in cookie editing, and its developer tools are open-source and extensible.

Intercepting Proxies

Burp Suite
Industry Standard Proxy
The most widely used tool in bug bounty. Community Edition is free and sufficient for most testing. Professional Edition ($449/year) adds the Scanner, Intruder with no rate limits, and advanced features. Sits between your browser and the target, intercepting, modifying, and replaying HTTP/S requests.
Caido
Modern Alternative
A newer, Rust-based proxy that aims to be a faster, more modern alternative to Burp Suite. Built for performance with features like HTTPQL for querying traffic history. Growing adoption in the researcher community. Free tier available.
OWASP ZAP
Free & Open Source
Fully open-source proxy maintained by the OWASP foundation. Includes an automated scanner, fuzzer, and spider. Heavier than Burp but completely free. Good for automated baseline scanning before manual testing.

Reconnaissance Tools

Amass
Subdomain Enumeration
OWASP's subdomain discovery tool. Performs DNS enumeration, certificate transparency log scraping, web scraping, and API integrations with dozens of data sources. The go-to tool for mapping wildcard scope targets.
subfinder
Fast Subdomain Discovery
ProjectDiscovery's passive subdomain enumeration tool. Faster than Amass for pure passive discovery because it does not perform active DNS resolution by default. Uses certificate transparency, search engines, and API sources.
httpx
HTTP Probing
Takes a list of domains or IPs and probes for live HTTP/S servers. Reports status codes, titles, content length, technologies, and more. Essential for filtering subdomain lists down to live, interesting targets.

Content Discovery & Scanning

ffuf
Web Fuzzer
Fast web fuzzer written in Go. Used for directory brute-forcing, parameter discovery, virtual host enumeration, and general fuzzing. Extremely fast and configurable with filters, matchers, and output formats.
Nuclei
Template-Based Scanner
ProjectDiscovery's vulnerability scanner driven by YAML templates. The community maintains thousands of templates for known CVEs, misconfigurations, and exposed panels. Run it against a list of live hosts for quick wins.
feroxbuster
Recursive Content Discovery
Rust-based content discovery tool that recursively brute-forces directories and files. Faster than dirsearch with built-in recursion, auto-filtering, and resume capability for long scans.

Note-Taking & Organization

Effective bug bounty research generates significant amounts of data: subdomains, endpoints, parameters, screenshots, request/response pairs, and notes about application behavior. Without a system, you will forget where you found something, re-test the same endpoint twice, or fail to connect related observations.

  • Obsidian — Markdown-based local notes with bidirectional linking. Excellent for building a knowledge graph of targets, techniques, and findings.
  • Notion — Database-style organization with Kanban boards for tracking which targets you are actively working, which reports are pending, and which bounties have been paid.
  • XMind — Mind mapping for visual attack surface exploration. Map out an application's functionality, then branch into potential vulnerability classes for each feature.

Section 07

🔍 Your First Bug

Finding your first bug is the most significant milestone in a bug bounty career. It proves the model works, builds your confidence, and gives you a tangible data point on the platform's leaderboard. The good news: your first bug does not need to be a critical remote code execution chain. Most researchers start with medium-severity web vulnerabilities — and that is exactly the right place to begin.

Mindset: Think Like an Attacker

The difference between a developer and a security researcher is perspective. A developer asks "how do I make this work?" A researcher asks "how can I make this break?" Every input field, every API parameter, every file upload, every state change is a potential attack vector. Your job is to ask: what happens if I provide unexpected input? What happens if I skip a step in the workflow? What happens if I access this resource as a different user?

The 15-Minute Rule

Before you start testing, spend at least 15 minutes using the application as a normal user. Create an account, explore every feature, read the documentation, understand the business logic. This is not wasted time — it is reconnaissance. You cannot find logic flaws in a system you do not understand. The researchers who skip this step end up submitting reports for "vulnerabilities" that are actually intended behavior, tanking their signal score.

Low-Hanging Fruit

Start with vulnerability classes that do not require deep application knowledge and are commonly found in production web applications:

🔍
Information Disclosure
Exposed .git directories, debug pages, stack traces with internal paths, verbose error messages revealing database structure, robots.txt and sitemap.xml revealing hidden endpoints. These are often missed because automated scanners do not always check for them.
Recon Finds
🔒
Misconfigurations
Default credentials on admin panels, open S3 buckets referenced in JavaScript source, CORS misconfigurations allowing arbitrary origins, missing security headers on sensitive endpoints, exposed Swagger/OpenAPI documentation with undocumented admin endpoints.
Config Errors
🔗
IDOR (Insecure Direct Object Reference)
Change a numeric ID in a URL or API parameter and see if you can access another user's data. This is one of the most common and impactful bug classes in web applications. Create two accounts and test whether account A can access account B's resources by manipulating identifiers.
High Impact
📄
Exposed Endpoints
APIs that do not require authentication, admin panels accessible without login, internal tools exposed to the internet, staging environments with test credentials. Content discovery tools like ffuf are built for finding these at scale.
Access Control

The Workflow

  1. Browse normally. Use the application as intended for 15+ minutes. Understand the features, user roles, and business logic. Note interesting functionality.
  2. Map the attack surface. Review Burp's site map and HTTP history. Identify all endpoints, parameters, and user-controllable inputs. Note which requests handle sensitive operations.
  3. Probe. For each interesting endpoint, test for common vulnerability classes: injection (SQL, XSS, command), broken access control (IDOR, privilege escalation), authentication flaws (password reset, session handling).
  4. Exploit. When you find something that behaves unexpectedly, develop a proof of concept. Can you demonstrate actual impact? Unauthorized data access, code execution, account takeover?
  5. Report. Write a clear, reproducible report. Include: vulnerability description, affected endpoint, step-by-step reproduction, proof of concept, impact assessment, and suggested remediation.
✓
Every Expert Was Once a Beginner
The top researchers on HackerOne's leaderboard all submitted their first report at some point. Many of them started with information disclosure findings or misconfigurations before progressing to critical authentication bypasses and remote code execution chains. Your first accepted report — regardless of severity — is the proof that you belong in this space. Focus on learning the process, not on maximizing payout. The payouts scale naturally as your skills develop.
📝
Writing a Good Report
The structure that gets reports triaged quickly
▼

A well-structured report dramatically increases the chance of acceptance and reduces triage time. Follow this template:

Title: Clear, specific, one-line summary. "IDOR in /api/v2/invoices allows any authenticated user to download other users' invoices" — not "Bug found in API."

Severity: Use CVSS or the platform's severity guide. Do not overstate — triagers will downgrade and it hurts your Signal score.

Description: Explain what the vulnerability is and why it matters. Reference the CWE (Common Weakness Enumeration) if applicable.

Steps to Reproduce: Numbered, precise steps that a triager can follow to reproduce the issue. Include exact URLs, HTTP requests (from Burp), and expected vs. actual results.

Impact: What can an attacker do with this vulnerability? Quantify if possible: "allows access to any user's PII including email, phone number, and billing address."

Proof of Concept: Screenshots, HTTP request/response pairs, or a video demonstrating the exploitation. Redact any real user data.

Suggested Fix: Optional but appreciated. "Implement server-side authorization check on the invoice ID parameter to verify the requesting user owns the resource."

💬 Discussion Prompts
  • 01 What motivates companies to pay strangers to hack them? Consider the economics: a company pays $10,000 for a critical vulnerability report. The same vulnerability, if discovered by a malicious actor, could cost millions in breach response, regulatory fines, and reputational damage. How does this cost-benefit analysis change for small startups versus Fortune 500 companies?
  • 02 How do you balance persistence with knowing when to move to a different target? Some researchers spend weeks on a single program and find nothing. Others jump between programs every few hours and submit low-quality reports. What signals tell you that a target is worth continued investment, and what signals tell you to move on?
  • 03 What ethical considerations should guide your testing decisions? You discover a SQL injection that gives you access to a production database with 50 million user records. The bug bounty program pays $5,000 for critical findings. What do you do next? How much data do you access to prove the vulnerability? How do you handle the knowledge that the data is currently exposed?
  • 04 The CFAA was written in 1986, before the web existed. How well does a law designed to prevent unauthorized mainframe access translate to modern bug bounty research? What legal reforms would make security research safer without enabling malicious hacking? How do safe harbor provisions address the gap between the law and current practice?
