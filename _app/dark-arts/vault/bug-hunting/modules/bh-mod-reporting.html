Reporting & Professional Methodology

Bug Hunting • Module 04 • Reporting

Finding a vulnerability is only half the work. The report you write determines whether that finding earns a bounty, gets closed as informative, or disappears into a queue of duplicates. This module covers the complete lifecycle from discovery to payout: CVSS scoring, report structure, proof-of-concept standards, disclosure ethics, and the professional methodology that separates consistent earners from one-time finders. Every section is built around the practices that triagers and program managers actually reward.

Section 01

Why Reports Matter More Than Bugs

There is a persistent misconception in the bug bounty community that the vulnerability is the product. It is not. The report is the product. A critical remote code execution flaw described in three vague sentences will earn less than a well-documented medium-severity IDOR with annotated screenshots, clear reproduction steps, and a measured impact statement. This is not a hypothetical scenario. It happens on every major platform, every week.

The Triager Perspective

Triagers at platforms like HackerOne, Bugcrowd, and Intigriti process hundreds of reports per day. The median time a triager spends on an initial review is between 90 seconds and three minutes. In that window, they need to determine whether the report is valid, whether it is a duplicate, and what severity bucket it falls into. Your report is competing for attention against every other submission in the queue.

A triager who cannot reproduce your finding in five minutes will typically set it aside, request more information, or close it as "needs more info." Every round trip of clarification adds days or weeks to the resolution cycle. Reports that require zero follow-up questions get triaged fastest, escalated fastest, and paid fastest.

Why Great Bugs Get Closed as N/A

The following are real reasons that valid vulnerabilities get closed without a bounty. None of them have anything to do with the technical severity of the finding:

  • The report did not include reproduction steps, forcing the triager to guess the attack path
  • The title was generic ("XSS found on website") and the triager assumed it was automated noise
  • The reporter described theoretical impact instead of demonstrated impact
  • The proof of concept only worked in a specific browser version that was not mentioned
  • The report contained raw scanner output with no human analysis
  • The vulnerability was out of scope but the reporter did not check the policy
  • The impact statement was exaggerated to the point of losing credibility ("full account takeover" for a reflected XSS on a static marketing page)
  • The reporter submitted the same finding to multiple programs and confused the targets in the description

The Difference Between $500 and $5,000

Consider two researchers who find the same IDOR vulnerability on the same program on the same day. Researcher A submits a report with a one-line description: "User can access other users' data by changing the ID parameter." Researcher B submits a structured report with a descriptive title, sequential reproduction steps starting from account creation, screenshots showing the request and response with sensitive fields highlighted, a CVSS vector string with justification for each metric, an impact analysis that quantifies the number of affected endpoints, and a suggested remediation approach.

Researcher A receives the base payout for a medium-severity finding. Researcher B receives an enhanced payout because the triager can immediately escalate, the development team can begin remediation without a follow-up meeting, and the program manager sees a professional report that reflects well on the platform. The bug is identical. The payout is not.

Industry Reality
Multiple platform managers have stated publicly that fewer than 20% of submitted reports require zero clarification. Researchers who consistently submit clean reports build reputation scores that grant them access to private programs with higher payouts and less competition. Report quality compounds over time in ways that raw technical skill does not.

What Programs Actually Pay For

Clarity
Can the triager understand the vulnerability, its location, and its impact within 60 seconds of opening the report? If yes, you have cleared the first and most important hurdle.
Triage Speed
Reproducibility
Can a developer who has never seen the report before follow the steps and confirm the vulnerability independently? Every assumption you leave unstated is a potential failure point.
Developer Trust
Measured Impact
Does the report quantify real-world impact rather than hand-waving about worst-case scenarios? A statement like "affects all 2.3 million user profiles" is more persuasive than "could potentially compromise user data."
Payout Factor

Does the report give the engineering team enough context to begin fixing the issue immediately? Reports that include suggested remediation demonstrate expertise and accelerate the fix cycle.

Section 02

CVSS v3.1 Scoring Deep Dive

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS) is the industry standard for communicating the severity of security vulnerabilities. Version 3.1, published by FIRST (the Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams), remains the most widely used scoring framework on bug bounty platforms. Understanding how to calculate and justify a CVSS score is not optional for professional hunters. Programs use it to determine payout tiers, and an incorrect score in your report signals either inexperience or attempted inflation.

Base Score Metrics

The Base Score captures the intrinsic characteristics of a vulnerability that remain constant over time and across environments. It is composed of two sub-groups: Exploitability metrics (how the vulnerability is exploited) and Impact metrics (what happens when it is exploited).

High (H) 0.44 		Whether specialized conditions beyond the attacker's control must exist. Low means the attack works reliably every time. High means it requires winning a race condition, specific configuration states, or gathering target-specific information not publicly available.

Scope

Scope (S) is the most misunderstood metric in CVSS and the one most frequently scored incorrectly. It answers one question: does the vulnerability in one component impact resources beyond that component's security authority?

  • Unchanged (U): The exploited vulnerability only affects resources managed by the same security authority. An SQL injection that reads data from the same database the application owns is Scope Unchanged.
  • Changed (C): The exploited vulnerability affects resources beyond the security scope of the vulnerable component. A sandbox escape, a VM breakout, or an XSS in a subdomain that can steal cookies from the parent domain are all Scope Changed.

The practical test: identify the security authority of the vulnerable component, then ask whether the impact crosses into a different security authority. If the web application has a vulnerability that only affects the web application's own data, the scope is unchanged. If a vulnerability in the web application can read files from the underlying operating system, the scope is changed because the OS is a separate security authority.

Impact Metrics

Metric Values Description
Confidentiality (C) High (H) 0.56
Low (L) 0.22

Temporal and Environmental Scores

The Temporal Score adjusts the Base Score based on factors that change over time:

  • Exploit Code Maturity (E): Is there a working exploit publicly available? Ranges from Unproven (U) through Proof-of-Concept (P) and Functional (F) to High (H). A vulnerability with a Metasploit module scores higher than one that is only theoretical.
  • Remediation Level (RL): Is there an official fix? Ranges from Official Fix (O), which lowers the score, through Temporary Fix (T) and Workaround (W) to Unavailable (U), which keeps the score at maximum.
  • Report Confidence (RC): How confident are we that the vulnerability exists? Ranges from Unknown (U) through Reasonable (R) to Confirmed (C). A bug with CVE assignment and vendor confirmation is Confirmed.

Environmental Scores allow organizations to customize the score based on their own infrastructure. Most bug bounty reporters do not need to calculate Environmental Scores because the program's security team handles that internally. However, understanding that it exists helps you anticipate why a program might rate your finding differently than your own CVSS calculation suggests.

CVSS Score Ranges

Score Range Severity Typical Bounty Tier Example
0.0 None No payout Informational finding, no security impact
0.1 - 3.9 Low $50 - $300 Information disclosure of non-sensitive data, verbose error messages
4.0 - 6.9 Medium $300 - $2,000 Stored XSS on non-critical page, CSRF on secondary function
7.0 - 8.9 High $2,000 - $10,000 SQL injection with data access, privilege escalation, IDOR on PII
9.0 - 10.0 Critical $10,000 - $100,000+ Pre-auth RCE, authentication bypass, full database dump

Common Scoring Mistakes

  • Inflating Attack Vector: Claiming Network when the attack actually requires local access or specific network positioning. If the attacker must be on the same Wi-Fi network, it is Adjacent, not Network.
  • Misusing Scope Changed: Marking Scope as Changed because the impact is severe. Scope is about crossing security boundaries, not about severity. A devastating SQL injection that stays within the application's own database is still Scope Unchanged.
  • Maxing CIA without justification: Setting Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability all to High without explaining how the vulnerability achieves total compromise of each. An XSS that steals session cookies has High Confidentiality impact but typically None for Availability.
  • Ignoring Privileges Required: Rating as None when the attacker actually needs a valid account. Free registration does not mean no privileges are required. If you must create an account first, the value is Low, not None.
  • Confusing Attack Complexity with exploit difficulty: Attack Complexity is about conditions outside the attacker's control, not about how hard the exploit was to develop. A complex exploit that works reliably is still Low complexity.
Scoring Credibility
Consistently inflating CVSS scores in your reports erodes trust with triagers and program managers. Experienced triagers can identify score inflation within seconds. It is far better to submit an accurate score and let the program upgrade it than to submit an inflated score and have it downgraded. The researcher who scores conservatively and accurately builds a reputation that opens doors to private programs.
✔
Vector String Format
Always include the full CVSS vector string in your reports. The format is: CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H (this example scores 9.8). The vector string lets anyone verify your score instantly using the FIRST calculator. Providing only a numeric score without the vector string forces the triager to either trust your number or recalculate from scratch.
Section 03

CVSS v4.0 Changes

CVSS version 4.0 was officially released by FIRST in November 2023. It represents the largest structural change to the scoring system since v2 to v3. While adoption on bug bounty platforms has been gradual, understanding v4.0 is essential for professional hunters because enterprise programs and CVE databases are increasingly requiring it. The transition period means you may need to provide both v3.1 and v4.0 scores for the next several years.

Key Structural Changes

CVSS v4.0 replaces the three-group model (Base, Temporal, Environmental) with a more granular nomenclature. The score is now described using specific combinations of metric groups:

  • CVSS-B: Base metrics only, equivalent to the v3.1 Base Score
  • CVSS-BT: Base plus Threat metrics (replaces Temporal)
  • CVSS-BE: Base plus Environmental metrics
  • CVSS-BTE: All three metric groups combined, the most complete score

New and Revised Metrics

Metric Group What Changed
Attack Requirements (AT) Base Replaces Attack Complexity. Focuses specifically on prerequisites the attacker must satisfy in the vulnerable system or its environment (e.g., race condition window, specific configuration state). Separates the concept of "attack difficulty" from "deployment conditions."
Vulnerable System CIA Base Impact metrics are now split into Vulnerable System (VC/VI/VA) and Subsequent System (SC/SI/SA). This replaces the binary Scope metric with a more precise model. You score the impact on the vulnerable component and any downstream systems separately.
Subsequent System CIA Base Captures the impact on systems beyond the vulnerable component. A sandbox escape would have High Subsequent System impact but possibly low Vulnerable System impact. This granularity eliminates many of the Scope-related scoring disagreements from v3.1.
Exploit Maturity (E) Threat The only remaining Threat metric. Simplified from the three Temporal metrics in v3.1 to a single value: Not Defined, Attacked, POC, or Unreported. Remediation Level and Report Confidence were removed.

Supplemental Metric Group

CVSS v4.0 introduces a Supplemental metric group that provides additional context without affecting the numeric score. These metrics are informational and help organizations make risk-based decisions beyond the score itself:

Safety (S)
Whether exploitation could result in physical safety consequences. Values: Negligible or Present. Relevant for IoT, medical devices, and industrial control systems where a vulnerability could endanger human life.
Supplemental
♦
Value Density (V)
Whether the vulnerable system contains concentrated or diffuse value. Diffuse means each instance holds limited resources (e.g., a user endpoint). Concentrated means a single instance holds significant aggregate value (e.g., a database server with millions of records).
Supplemental
Vulnerability Response Effort (RE)
How much effort is required to respond to and remediate the vulnerability. Low means a simple patch or configuration change. Moderate means a standard update cycle. High means significant architectural changes are needed. This helps organizations prioritize remediation resources.
Supplemental
⏲
Provider Urgency (U)
The vendor's own assessment of urgency. Allows the software provider to communicate their contextual understanding of the risk. Values range from Not Defined through Low, Amber (medium), to Red (critical). This metric bridges the gap between standardized scoring and vendor knowledge.
Supplemental

When to Use v4.0

The decision of which CVSS version to use depends on the program you are reporting to:

  • If the program's policy specifies CVSS v3.1, use v3.1. Do not unilaterally switch to v4.0.
  • If the program requests v4.0 or does not specify a version, use v4.0 with the CVSS-BT nomenclature.
  • For CVE submissions through MITRE or NVD, v4.0 is increasingly preferred. Check the current submission guidelines.
  • When in doubt, provide both. A v3.1 vector string and a v4.0 vector string takes an extra 30 seconds and demonstrates thoroughness.
  • Enterprise-focused programs (especially those in regulated industries like healthcare and finance) tend to adopt new CVSS versions faster than startup programs.
Practical Impact of v4.0 on Scoring
The elimination of the binary Scope metric and its replacement with Vulnerable System and Subsequent System impact metrics means that some vulnerabilities will score differently between v3.1 and v4.0. In particular, XSS vulnerabilities and sandbox escapes may see score adjustments. The split impact model tends to produce more accurate scores for vulnerabilities that cross trust boundaries, which was one of the primary motivations for the v4.0 revision.
Section 04

Writing the Perfect Report

Title Formulas That Work

The title is the first thing a triager reads and often the only thing a program manager sees in the queue overview. A good title follows a predictable formula that communicates the vulnerability type, the affected component, and the impact in a single line:

// Effective title formulas: [Vulnerability Type] in [Component/Endpoint] allows [Impact] // Examples: Stored XSS in comment field on /blog/post allows session hijacking IDOR on /api/v2/users/{id}/profile exposes PII of all users SQL Injection in search parameter on /products enables database extraction Authentication bypass via password reset token reuse on /auth/reset SSRF in image proxy at /fetch?url= allows internal network scanning // Avoid these patterns: "XSS found" // Too vague "Critical vulnerability!!!" // Unprofessional "Bug on your website" // Useless "Multiple vulnerabilities discovered" // Submit separate reports

Description Structure

Every report should contain four mandatory sections and one recommended section. This structure is not arbitrary; it mirrors what triagers are trained to look for and maps directly to the fields in most platform submission forms.

  • Summary -- Two to four sentences describing what the vulnerability is, where it exists, and what an attacker can achieve by exploiting it. Write this for someone who will not read the rest of the report. The summary should stand alone as a complete description of the finding.
  • Steps to Reproduce -- Sequential, numbered instructions that start from a clean state (logged out, new browser session, freshly created test account). Each step should be a single action. Include exact URLs, parameter names, header values, and HTTP methods. Never assume the reader knows which page you are on or what button you clicked.
  • Impact -- What can an attacker actually do with this vulnerability? Quantify where possible: number of affected users, types of data exposed, business functions disrupted. Distinguish between what you demonstrated and what is theoretically possible, clearly labeling each.
  • CVSS Score -- The full vector string with a brief justification for any non-obvious metric choices. If Scope is Changed, explain why. If Attack Complexity is High, explain what conditions must be met.
  • Remediation Suggestion -- Not required but strongly recommended. A one-paragraph suggestion of how to fix the issue demonstrates expertise and helps the engineering team. Keep it technical but concise: "Implement parameterized queries for the search endpoint" is better than a five-paragraph essay on SQL injection prevention.

The Five-Minute Reproduction Test

Before submitting any report, apply the five-minute reproduction test. Give your reproduction steps to someone who has never seen the vulnerability. Set a timer for five minutes. If they cannot reproduce the issue in that time using only your written instructions, the report needs revision. If you do not have a colleague to test with, read your steps aloud as if you were dictating them to someone over the phone. Every gap in the instructions will become obvious.

Writing for the Developer, Not the Security Team

The person who fixes your vulnerability is usually a developer, not a security engineer. They may not know what IDOR stands for. They may not understand CVSS metrics. Write your Steps to Reproduce section so that a backend developer who is unfamiliar with security terminology can follow them. Use the application's own terminology (the specific endpoint names, parameter names, and feature labels) rather than abstract security jargon.

This does not mean dumbing down the report. It means being precise. "Change the user_id parameter from 12345 to 12346 in the GET request to /api/v2/users/12345/profile" is more useful than "exploit the IDOR vulnerability in the user profile endpoint" even though they describe the same action.

Good Report vs. Bad Report

✗ Weak Report

  • • Title: "XSS on website"
  • • "I found an XSS vulnerability on your website. When I put a script tag in the search box it pops an alert. This is critical because an attacker could steal cookies."
  • • No URL provided
  • • No steps to reproduce
  • • No screenshot or PoC
  • • CVSS: "9.8 Critical"
  • • No vector string
  • • Impact: "attacker can do anything"

✓ Strong Report

  • • Title: "Stored XSS in /support/tickets comment field allows session hijacking of support agents"
  • • Summary: 3 sentences identifying vuln class, location, and proven impact
  • • 7 numbered reproduction steps from login to payload execution
  • • Annotated screenshots of each step
  • • CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:L/UI:R/S:C/C:L/I:L/A:N (5.4 Medium)
  • • Justification: "Scope Changed because the XSS executes in the context of the support agent's session, crossing from user to agent trust boundary"
  • • Impact: quantified affected user count, demonstrated cookie theft PoC
  • • Remediation: "Sanitize user input with DOMPurify before rendering in the agent dashboard view"
✔
Template Discipline
Create a report template in a text file and use it for every submission. The template should include all section headers and placeholder text reminding you what each section requires. Using a consistent template ensures you never forget a section under the pressure of wanting to submit before someone else finds the same bug. Speed without structure produces rejected reports.
Section 05

📸 Proof of Concept Best Practices

A proof of concept (PoC) transforms a claim into evidence. Without a PoC, your report is an assertion. With one, it is a demonstration. The quality of your PoC directly influences how quickly your report is triaged, how seriously the impact is assessed, and whether the finding survives internal review.

Screenshots: The Foundation

Screenshots remain the most common form of PoC evidence. They are quick to capture, universally viewable, and can be annotated to draw attention to specific elements. However, most researchers undermine their screenshots by violating basic practices:

  • Annotate meaningfully. Use arrows, boxes, and numbered callouts to highlight the relevant request parameters, response data, or UI elements. A raw screenshot of a browser window is not evidence; it is a picture.
  • Capture sequentially. Number your screenshots to match your reproduction steps. Step 1 corresponds to Screenshot 1. This one-to-one mapping eliminates ambiguity about which visual evidence supports which claim.
  • Include the URL bar. Every browser screenshot should show the full URL. This proves which endpoint you are demonstrating and prevents the triager from needing to ask "where was this?"
  • Show the request and response. For API-level findings, capture both the request (with method, headers, and body) and the response. Browser DevTools or Burp Suite's request/response panels serve this purpose.
  • Use consistent dimensions. Screenshots at different zoom levels and window sizes look unprofessional and make the report harder to follow. Pick a consistent window size and stick with it.

Video Recordings

Video recordings are appropriate when the vulnerability involves a multi-step interaction that is difficult to convey through static screenshots. Race conditions, timing-dependent exploits, and complex user interaction flows benefit from video. However, video should supplement screenshots, not replace them. Triagers often need to reference specific moments in the attack chain, and scrubbing through a video to find one frame is slower than glancing at a labeled screenshot.

  • Keep recordings under two minutes. If your PoC takes longer, the reproduction steps need simplification.
  • Record at a resolution where text is legible. 1080p minimum, with the browser window filling most of the frame.
  • Narration is optional but helpful for complex flows. If you narrate, speak clearly and describe each action as you perform it.
  • Host on a stable platform. Upload to the bug bounty platform's attachment system directly. Do not link to URLs that may expire.

Minimal PoC Scripts

For technical findings like SSRF, SQL injection, or authentication bypasses, a minimal PoC script demonstrates the vulnerability more precisely than any screenshot. The key word is minimal. Your script should do exactly one thing: prove the vulnerability exists.

# Good: Minimal PoC demonstrating IDOR curl -s -H "Authorization: Bearer USER_A_TOKEN" \ "https://api.example.com/v2/users/99999/profile" | jq '.email, .phone' # Output demonstrates access to User B's PII using User A's token "userB@example.com" "+1-555-0199"
# Good: Minimal PoC demonstrating SQL injection (boolean-based blind) # True condition (normal response): curl -s "https://example.com/products?id=1 AND 1=1" | wc -c 4892 # False condition (empty/different response): curl -s "https://example.com/products?id=1 AND 1=2" | wc -c 0

PoC scripts should use common tools that any developer or security engineer has available: curl, Python requests, or JavaScript fetch. Do not submit PoCs that require installing niche frameworks or compiling custom tooling. The easier it is to run your PoC, the faster it gets validated.

Data Handling and PII Masking

When your PoC reveals real user data, you have both an ethical and a legal obligation to handle it carefully:

  • Mask PII in screenshots. Redact email addresses, phone numbers, physical addresses, and any other personally identifiable information. Show enough to prove the data is real (e.g., the first and last characters of an email) but not enough to identify the individual.
  • Do not exfiltrate more than necessary. If one record proves the IDOR exists, do not dump the entire table. Accessing one unauthorized record is a PoC. Accessing ten thousand is a breach.
  • Delete any data you downloaded. After your report is submitted and the screenshots are captured, delete any local copies of user data. Some programs explicitly require this in their policy.
  • Use your own test accounts. Whenever possible, demonstrate the vulnerability using accounts you control. Create User A and User B yourself. This eliminates the ethical concern entirely and is stronger evidence because you can show both sides of the access control failure.

What NOT to Include

  • Automated scanner output without analysis (Burp Suite scan results, Nikto dumps, Nessus reports). Triagers view raw scanner output as noise, not evidence.
  • PoCs that cause actual damage: dropping tables, deleting user data, sending emails from compromised accounts, modifying production content. Demonstrate the capability without exercising it destructively.
  • Weaponized exploits with evasion capabilities. Your PoC should prove the vulnerability exists, not demonstrate how to build a production-grade attack tool.
  • Personal commentary about the target's security posture. "I can't believe you haven't fixed this" or "your security team should be fired" has no place in a professional report.
  • Findings from other researchers or other programs. Each report must contain only your own original work on the specific program you are reporting to.
Impact Without Damage
The golden rule of proof-of-concept development: demonstrate impact without causing harm. For XSS, use alert(), console.log(), or document.domain instead of stealing actual session cookies. For SQL injection, extract the database version string instead of dumping user tables. For RCE, run id, whoami, or cat /etc/hostname instead of installing backdoors. Every program's policy draws a line between testing and abuse. Stay well inside that line.
Section 06

Responsible Disclosure & Communication

Disclosure is the process of communicating a vulnerability finding to the affected party so they can fix it. How you handle disclosure defines your professional reputation more than any technical finding. The security community has a long memory, and a single instance of irresponsible disclosure can permanently close doors to private programs, conference invitations, and industry employment.

The 90-Day Disclosure Timeline

The industry standard, established by Google's Project Zero and widely adopted, gives vendors 90 days from the initial report to release a fix before the researcher may publicly disclose the vulnerability. This timeline balances two competing interests: the vendor's need for time to develop, test, and deploy a patch, and the public's right to know about risks that affect them.

  • Day 0: You submit the report to the vendor or platform.
  • Days 1-14: The vendor acknowledges receipt and begins triage. If you receive no acknowledgment within 14 days, send a follow-up. Document the follow-up.
  • Days 15-60: The vendor develops and tests a fix. You should be available to answer technical questions and verify proposed patches if asked.
  • Days 61-80: If no fix has been released and no communication has been received, send a formal notice reminding the vendor that public disclosure will occur at 90 days.
  • Day 90: If the vendor has not released a fix, you may publicly disclose the vulnerability. Whether you should is a separate question from whether you may.
  • Extensions: Reasonable extensions (14-30 days) are commonly granted when the vendor demonstrates active progress toward a fix. Granting extensions for vendors who are clearly working on a patch is professional. Granting indefinite extensions for vendors who have gone silent is not.

Communicating with Triagers

Platform triagers are your primary point of contact for bug bounty submissions. They are not adversaries. They are professionals doing a high-volume, high-pressure job. Effective communication with triagers follows predictable patterns:

  • Be concise. Triagers process hundreds of reports. Every sentence in your follow-up messages should contain information, not filler. "Can you provide an update on the status of this report?" is better than a three-paragraph message about how long you have been waiting.
  • Respond promptly. When a triager asks for clarification, reply within 24-48 hours. Reports that go unanswered for weeks get deprioritized or closed.
  • Be factual, not emotional. If you disagree with a severity rating or a closure decision, respond with technical evidence, not frustration. "The impact warrants a higher severity because [specific technical reason]" is productive. "This is clearly critical and you don't understand security" is not.
  • Provide context proactively. If a related CVE is published, if the vendor releases a partial fix, or if you discover additional impact, add that information to the report as a comment. Triagers value researchers who keep their reports current.

Handling Disputes

Disagreements happen. Programs sometimes rate findings lower than you believe is justified. Findings sometimes get closed as "informative" when you believe they warrant a bounty. The way you handle these situations determines your long-term reputation:

Severity Disagreement
When your CVSS score differs from the program's assessment
▼

Start by re-reading the program's severity guidelines. Many programs publish their own severity definitions that may differ from standard CVSS. A program might classify all XSS as medium regardless of the CVSS score, or they might rate IDOR higher than CVSS suggests because of their specific data sensitivity.

If you still believe the rating is incorrect after reviewing the program's guidelines, write a focused rebuttal that addresses the specific CVSS metrics in dispute. For example: "The report was rated as Medium (CVSS 5.3) but I believe High (CVSS 7.5) is more accurate because the Scope should be Changed, not Unchanged. The vulnerable component is the user profile API, but the impact crosses into the admin dashboard context because the injected content renders in the admin review queue."

Accept the outcome gracefully. If the program maintains their rating after your rebuttal, the professional response is to accept it and move on. Prolonged arguments over one severity level do not serve your interests.

N/A or Informative Closure
When a valid finding is closed without a bounty
▼

First, verify that the finding is actually in scope. Re-read the program policy carefully. Many closures are legitimate: the endpoint is excluded, the vulnerability class is listed as out-of-scope, or the program considers the risk accepted.

If the finding is in scope and you believe the closure is incorrect, provide additional evidence. Sometimes a finding is closed because the triager could not reproduce it. In that case, provide a more detailed PoC, a video recording, or offer to schedule a live demonstration.

Platform mediation is available on most major platforms. HackerOne has a mediation process, Bugcrowd has a researcher advocacy team. Use these channels when direct communication fails. They exist specifically for this purpose.

Coordinated Disclosure vs. Full Disclosure

Coordinated Disclosure

  • Work with the vendor to agree on a disclosure date
  • Vendor releases the patch before or simultaneously with public disclosure
  • Researcher and vendor may publish a joint advisory
  • Preferred by enterprise vendors, government agencies, and most bounty platforms
  • Builds long-term relationships and access to private programs
  • The professional standard in the industry today

Full Disclosure

  • Researcher publishes vulnerability details without vendor coordination
  • Historically used when vendors ignore or suppress reports
  • Puts pressure on unresponsive vendors to act
  • Carries significant reputational risk if used prematurely
  • May expose users to active exploitation before a patch is available
  • Should be a last resort after all other channels are exhausted

Bounty Negotiation

Negotiation is a normal part of professional bug bounty hunting. It is not confrontational when done correctly. The following principles guide effective negotiation:

  • Negotiate on impact, not effort. "I spent 40 hours finding this" is not a valid argument for a higher payout. "This vulnerability exposes the payment processing API and could result in financial loss to 50,000 active subscribers" is.
  • Reference the program's own payout table. Most programs publish severity-to-payout ranges. If your finding falls within the High range and you received a Medium payout, point to the specific row in their table and explain why High is more appropriate.
  • Provide additional impact evidence. If you discover during the negotiation that the vulnerability has a wider scope than originally reported (more affected endpoints, more data types exposed), add that evidence to strengthen your case.
  • Know when to accept. If the program has reviewed your evidence, maintained their assessment, and the payout falls within their published range for that severity, continued negotiation becomes counterproductive. Take the payout, maintain the relationship, and invest that energy into finding the next bug.
When to Go Public
Public disclosure should only be considered when: (1) the vendor has been unresponsive for 90+ days despite documented follow-up attempts, (2) the vulnerability poses an active, ongoing risk to users, and (3) you have exhausted all private channels including platform mediation. Disclosing a vulnerability publicly to pressure a vendor into paying a higher bounty is considered extortion in many jurisdictions and will result in permanent platform bans. The line between responsible disclosure and coercion is clear: you disclose to protect users, never to leverage payment.
Section 07

Handling Duplicates & Edge Cases

Duplicates are the single largest source of frustration in bug bounty hunting. On popular programs, the duplicate rate for common vulnerability classes can exceed 80%. Understanding why duplicates happen, how platforms handle them, and what you can do to reduce your duplicate rate is essential for making bug bounty hunting sustainable rather than demoralizing.

Why Duplicates Happen

  • Shared tooling produces shared results. If you run the same automated scanner that 200 other researchers are running against the same target, you will find the same things they find. Tool-generated findings have the highest duplicate rates in the industry.
  • Public disclosures trigger pile-ons. When a new CVE is published for a popular framework, every researcher who uses that framework's version detection tool submits reports to every program running an affected version. Programs receive dozens of identical reports within hours.
  • Low-hanging fruit is low-hanging for everyone. The missing security headers, the exposed .git directory, the verbose error page -- these findings are discovered by the first ten researchers who look at any new program.
  • Programs with wide scope attract volume. A program that lists *.example.com as in scope receives far more duplicates than a program that targets a specific application endpoint, because more researchers are looking at more surface area.

How to Reduce Duplicate Risk

🕑
Avoid the Crowd
New programs receive the most submissions in their first 72 hours. Either be among the very first to test (within the first hour of launch) or wait two weeks until the initial rush subsides and begin looking at areas the crowd missed.
Timing
Go Deeper
The automated scanner findings are all duplicates. The manual, logic-based findings are not. Business logic flaws, multi-step authorization bypasses, and race conditions require human reasoning that tools cannot replicate. This is where duplicate rates drop below 20%.
Depth
Target Neglected Assets
Mobile APIs, legacy subdomains, partner integrations, and development/staging environments receive far less attention than the primary web application. Finding the same vulnerability class on an overlooked asset has a lower duplicate rate than finding it on the main product.
Targeting
Specialize
Researchers who specialize in a specific vulnerability class (e.g., OAuth implementation flaws, GraphQL security, WebSocket vulnerabilities) develop testing instincts that generalists lack. Specialization naturally leads to findings that fewer people are equipped to discover.
Expertise

Informative vs. N/A

When a report is closed, the resolution status matters for your reputation score on the platform:

Status Meaning Reputation Impact
Resolved Valid finding, bounty awarded, fix deployed Positive
Duplicate Valid finding, but another researcher reported it first Neutral
Informative Valid observation but not considered a security vulnerability by the program Neutral
Not Applicable Not a valid finding (out of scope, not reproducible, not a vulnerability) Negative
Spam Automated or irrelevant submission Severely Negative

The difference between Informative and N/A is significant. An Informative closure means the program acknowledged your work but chose not to pay for it. This might happen because the finding is a known accepted risk, because it falls below their severity threshold, or because it is technically valid but not exploitable in their specific configuration. An N/A closure means the program determined the submission was invalid. Too many N/A closures will reduce your reputation score and can result in restricted access to programs.

Partial Duplicates and Report Grouping

Partial duplicates occur when your report overlaps with a previous submission but includes additional scope. For example, the original report identified an IDOR on /api/users/{id}/profile, and your report identifies the same IDOR pattern on /api/users/{id}/billing, /api/users/{id}/orders, and /api/users/{id}/preferences.

How platforms handle this varies. Some programs treat each affected endpoint as a separate finding. Others consider the root cause (the missing authorization check) as a single finding regardless of how many endpoints it affects. Read the program's policy on report grouping before submitting. If the policy is unclear, submit a single report that documents all affected endpoints rather than multiple reports for each one. A comprehensive report that maps the full scope of a vulnerability is more valuable to the program and more likely to receive enhanced compensation.

Retesting After Fixes

Many programs request that researchers verify fixes before closing a report. This is an opportunity, not a chore. Retesting gives you:

  • Confirmation that your finding was taken seriously and addressed
  • The chance to identify incomplete fixes (a fix that addresses the reported endpoint but not similar endpoints is a new finding)
  • A stronger relationship with the program team, which can lead to invitations to private programs
  • Additional context about the application's architecture that informs future testing

When retesting, use the exact same reproduction steps from your original report. If the fix is effective, confirm it explicitly: "Retested on [date]. The fix is effective. The request to /api/users/{id}/profile now returns a 403 Forbidden when accessed with a different user's token." If the fix is incomplete, document precisely what still works and submit a new report referencing the original.

Dealing with Program Changes

Programs change their policies, scope, and payout tables. Assets are added and removed. Vulnerability classes move in and out of scope. These changes can affect pending reports:

  • If an asset moves out of scope while your report is pending, the report should still be honored under the policy that was active when you submitted. If it is not, escalate through the platform's mediation process.
  • If a program reduces its payout table, pending reports should be paid at the rate that was active at the time of submission. Document the payout table (screenshot it) when you submit.
  • If a program expands scope, previously out-of-scope findings do not retroactively become valid. You would need to retest and submit a new report under the new policy.
✔
The Duplicate Mindset
Professional hunters accept that duplicates are a cost of doing business. A healthy duplicate rate is 20-30%. If your duplicate rate is 0%, you are likely not submitting enough. If it is above 50%, you are not differentiating your methodology from the crowd. Track your duplicate rate monthly and use it as a signal to adjust your target selection and testing depth.
Section 08

Building Your Professional Methodology

The researchers who earn consistently are not the ones with the most technical knowledge. They are the ones with a repeatable, documented methodology that they execute with discipline. This section covers the end-to-end workflow from target selection to payout, and the operational habits that separate a professional practice from a hobby.

End-to-End Workflow

A complete hunting session follows a predictable lifecycle. Deviating from this structure is where most researchers lose time and efficiency:

  • Target Selection -- Choose a program based on your specialization, the program's payout range, response time history, and current scope. Check the program's activity: how recently were bounties awarded? A program that has not paid in six months may be inactive or have exhausted its low-hanging fruit.
  • Scope Review -- Read the entire program policy before touching a single endpoint. Document what is in scope, what is excluded, which vulnerability classes are rewarded, and which are explicitly out of scope. Screenshot the payout table for your records.
  • Reconnaissance -- Map the attack surface systematically. Subdomain enumeration, technology stack fingerprinting, content discovery, API documentation review. Spend 30-40% of your total session time on reconnaissance. The quality of your recon directly determines the quality of your findings.
  • Targeted Testing -- Based on your recon, prioritize the most promising attack surfaces. Test for vulnerability classes that match the technology stack you identified. A Django application warrants different testing than a Node.js application.
  • Documentation -- Document as you test, not after. Capture screenshots and requests in real-time. If you find a vulnerability, write the report immediately while the context is fresh. Reports written from memory hours later are always lower quality.
  • Report Submission -- Apply the five-minute reproduction test. Verify your CVSS score against the FIRST calculator. Check that all screenshots have PII masked. Submit.
  • Follow-Up -- Monitor the report for triager questions. Respond within 24 hours. If retesting is requested, do it promptly. Close the loop on every report you submit.
  • Post-Mortem -- After a report is resolved (paid or closed), review what worked and what did not. How long did triage take? Was there any follow-up? Did your CVSS score match the program's assessment? Record these metrics.

Time Management

Bug bounty hunting without time boundaries leads to burnout. Professional hunters treat it like a job with defined working hours and structured sessions:

  • Set session limits. A focused 3-hour session is more productive than a distracted 10-hour marathon. Diminishing returns set in after 3-4 hours on the same target.
  • Use the Pomodoro technique for recon. 25 minutes of focused reconnaissance followed by a 5-minute break. Recon is detail-oriented work that degrades with fatigue.
  • Timebox dead ends. If you have spent 45 minutes investigating a potential vulnerability and cannot confirm exploitability, move on. Document your notes in case you want to return later, but do not let sunk cost keep you invested in a dead end.
  • Schedule report writing. Block dedicated time for writing reports rather than trying to hunt and write simultaneously. Context switching between testing and writing degrades both activities.

Target Rotation

Testing the same program for weeks produces diminishing returns. Effective target rotation prevents staleness and increases your exposure to different technology stacks and vulnerability classes:

Primary Targets (60% of time)

  • 2-3 programs you know deeply
  • Return to these every 2-3 weeks
  • Track their changelog and deployments
  • Test new features as they launch
  • These produce your highest-severity findings

Rotation Targets (40% of time)

  • New programs in their first week
  • Programs you have never tested before
  • Different technology stacks from your primaries
  • Keeps your skills broad and adaptable
  • These produce your most surprising findings

Specialization vs. Generalization

The optimal strategy evolves over time. New hunters should start as generalists, learning every vulnerability class broadly. After six months to a year, patterns emerge: you find more of one vulnerability class than others, certain technology stacks feel more natural, some testing methodologies produce results more consistently.

Follow those signals. The researcher who deeply understands OAuth implementation patterns will find authorization flaws that ten generalists will miss. The researcher who has spent hundreds of hours testing GraphQL APIs sees query structure anomalies that others overlook. Specialization does not mean ignoring other vulnerability classes. It means having one or two areas where your testing depth exceeds the industry average.

The most effective professional researchers are T-shaped: broad knowledge across all vulnerability classes with deep expertise in one or two. The broad knowledge helps you recognize opportunities. The deep expertise helps you exploit them.

Tracking Your Metrics

You cannot improve what you do not measure. Professional hunters track their performance metrics with the same rigor that a business tracks revenue and expenses. At minimum, track:

Metric What It Tells You Target Range
Reports Submitted / Month Volume and consistency of output 8-20 reports/month for part-time
Acceptance Rate Quality of your findings Above 60% is professional grade
Duplicate Rate Whether your methodology differentiates from the crowd Below 30% indicates good targeting
Average Severity Depth of your testing Medium or higher for experienced hunters
Time to Triage Quality of your reports (faster triage = clearer reports) Under 48 hours for well-written reports
Revenue Per Hour Overall efficiency of your practice Track trend, not absolute number
N/A Rate Whether you are submitting invalid findings Below 10% -- high N/A rates damage reputation

The Hunter's Journal

Maintain a dedicated document (plain text, markdown, or a private wiki) where you record every testing session. This journal serves as your institutional memory and compounds in value over time. Each entry should include:

  • Date and target: Which program, which assets tested
  • Time spent: How many hours, broken down by recon, testing, and reporting
  • Findings: What you found, including dead ends and near-misses
  • Techniques used: Which tools and manual tests you ran
  • Notes for next session: Specific areas to revisit, hypotheses to test, features to watch for updates
  • Lessons learned: What worked, what did not, what you would do differently

After six months of journal entries, you will have a personal knowledge base that no course or certification can replicate. You will see patterns in your own productivity: which times of day you are most effective, which vulnerability classes you find most naturally, which programs reward your specific testing style. This self-knowledge is the foundation of a sustainable practice.

The Long Game
Bug bounty hunting rewards patience and consistency over intensity and luck. The researchers who earn six figures annually did not start there. They built their methodology over years, refined their specialization through thousands of hours of practice, and invested in relationships with programs and platforms. Treat every report as a brick in a career, not a lottery ticket. The compounding effects of reputation, skill, and access to private programs create an exponential growth curve that only reveals itself over time.
