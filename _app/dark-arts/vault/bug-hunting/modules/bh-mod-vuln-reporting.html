Section 01

Why Reporting Matters

Triagers at major platforms process hundreds of reports per week. Your report competes for attention with every other submission in the queue. The first 30 seconds determine whether it gets a thorough review or a quick rejection. Clear, professional reports signal competence and earn trust — which translates directly into faster triage times and better payouts.

The Report Quality Spectrum

✗ Bad Reports

  • ▶ Vague descriptions ("I found XSS on your site")
  • ▶ No reproduction steps or steps that skip key details
  • ▶ Screenshots without context or annotation
  • ▶ No impact assessment beyond the vulnerability name
  • ▶ Demanding tone or unreasonable severity claims

✓ Good Reports

  • ▶ Precise title that names the vulnerability and location
  • ▶ Numbered steps anyone can follow to reproduce
  • ▶ HTTP requests/responses with key parts highlighted
  • ▶ Business impact tied to real-world consequences
  • ▶ Professional, concise, and evidence-backed
A bug report is not a diary entry. It is a technical document that must convince a stranger that a vulnerability exists, that they can reproduce it, and that it matters. Every unnecessary sentence is friction between your finding and the bounty.

Section 03

Writing Steps to Reproduce

Steps to reproduce are the most important section of your report. If the triager cannot reproduce the vulnerability, the report dies. Write as if the reader has never seen the application before. Include every click, every URL, every parameter.

Rules for Reproduction Steps

  1. Start from scratch — begin with a clean browser session. Specify whether to use incognito mode, clear cookies, or log out first.
  2. Include exact URLs — never say "go to the profile page." Say "navigate to https://target.com/settings/profile."
  3. Specify exact payloads — provide the literal string to type or paste. Wrap payloads in code blocks so there is no ambiguity about whitespace or special characters.
  4. Number every step — even trivial actions like "Click the Submit button." Missing a single step can prevent reproduction.
  5. Describe expected vs. actual behavior — after the final step, state what should happen (expected) and what actually happens (the vulnerability).
  6. Include prerequisites — if the vulnerability requires two accounts, a specific role, or a particular browser, state that upfront before step 1.

Example: Well-Written Reproduction Steps

Prerequisites: - Two registered accounts: attacker@test.com (User A) and victim@test.com (User B) - Both accounts have profile photos uploaded - Browser: Chrome 120+ with DevTools open (Network tab) Steps: 1. Log in as User A at https://target.com/login 2. Navigate to https://target.com/api/v2/users/profile 3. In the browser URL bar, change the path to: https://target.com/api/v2/users/profile?user_id=USER_B_ID (Replace USER_B_ID with User B's numeric ID, e.g., 48291) 4. Observe the response in the Network tab Expected: 403 Forbidden or redirect to own profile Actual: 200 OK with User B's full profile data including: - email, phone number, home address - payment method (last 4 digits + expiry) - linked social media accounts
ℹ
The Reproduction Test
Before submitting, hand your report to someone unfamiliar with the target and ask them to reproduce it using only your written steps. If they cannot, your steps need more detail. This exercise alone will improve your acceptance rate.
Section 04

Assessing Impact

Impact assessment bridges the gap between a technical finding and a business decision. The development team needs to prioritize your bug against hundreds of other tasks. Your impact section determines whether it gets fixed this sprint or sits in the backlog for months.

Technical Severity vs. Business Impact

Technical Severity

  • ▶ CVSS vector and base score
  • ▶ Attack vector (network, adjacent, local)
  • ▶ Attack complexity (low, high)
  • ▶ Privileges required and user interaction
  • ▶ Confidentiality, integrity, availability impact

Business Impact

  • ▶ Number of affected users
  • ▶ Type of data exposed (PII, financial, medical)
  • ▶ Regulatory implications (GDPR, HIPAA, PCI DSS)
  • ▶ Reputational damage potential
  • ▶ Financial loss (fraud, breach notification costs)

Vulnerability Chaining

Individual findings often appear low or medium severity in isolation, but chaining them together creates high or critical impact. Always consider whether your finding can be combined with other issues to escalate the attack.

IDOR + Info Disclosure
An information disclosure leak reveals user IDs. An IDOR vulnerability on the profile API allows enumeration using those IDs. Separately: medium. Together: high — unauthenticated mass data extraction.
Self-XSS + CSRF
A self-XSS alone has no impact (victim must paste the payload themselves). But a CSRF vulnerability on the same form can inject the XSS payload via a crafted link. The chain bypasses the self-XSS limitation entirely.
SSRF + Cloud Metadata
An SSRF that can only reach internal IPs seems limited. But reaching the cloud metadata endpoint (169.254.169.254) leaks IAM credentials, turning a medium SSRF into a critical cloud account compromise.
✓
Always Mention Chain Potential
Even if you have not found the second link in the chain, mention the potential. "This IDOR could be chained with an information disclosure vulnerability to enable mass data extraction" frames your finding in a broader attack context and may influence the severity assessment upward.
Section 05

Proof of Concept

A proof of concept (PoC) demonstrates the vulnerability beyond the written steps. For simple bugs, annotated screenshots suffice. For complex chains, you may need video recordings, HTTP request captures, or even a minimal exploitation script.

PoC Evidence Types

Evidence Type Best For Tips
Screenshots XSS popups, exposed data, error messages, config files Annotate with arrows and highlights. Include the URL bar. Redact PII.
HTTP Request/Response API vulnerabilities, IDOR, injection, auth bypass Copy raw from Burp Suite. Highlight the vulnerable parameter and the interesting response data.
Video Recording Multi-step chains, race conditions, timing-based attacks Keep under 3 minutes. No narration needed if steps are clear. Use a timestamp overlay.
cURL Commands API bugs that are fully reproducible via command line Include all headers and cookies. Test the command yourself before submitting.
Exploitation Script Complex or automated exploitation Write minimal, readable code. Comment each step. Include a safe mode that limits impact.

HTTP Request Capture Example

--- REQUEST --- GET /api/v2/users/48291/profile HTTP/2 Host: target.com Authorization: Bearer eyJhbGciOiJIUzI1NiJ9.eyJ1c2VyX2lkIjoxMjM0NX0.abc123 Cookie: session=a1b2c3d4e5f6 Accept: application/json --- RESPONSE --- HTTP/2 200 OK Content-Type: application/json { "id": 48291, "email": "victim@example.com", // Not the authenticated user's data "phone": "+1-555-0142", "address": "123 Main St, Anytown, US", "payment_method": { "last_four": "4242", "expiry": "12/2027" } }
Redact Sensitive Data
If your PoC captures real user data (even from your own accounts), redact sensitive fields before including them in the report. Replace real emails, addresses, and payment details with obvious placeholders. This protects privacy and demonstrates professionalism.
Section 06

Common Mistakes

These are the top reasons vulnerability reports get rejected, downgraded, or ignored. Avoiding these mistakes puts you ahead of most researchers in the queue.

# Mistake Why It Kills Your Report
01 Submitting duplicates without checking The earlier reporter gets the bounty. Search disclosed reports on the platform before submitting.
02 Testing out-of-scope assets Instant rejection. Repeated violations can get you banned from the program.
03 Reporting theoretical-only risks "This could potentially be exploited if..." is not a finding. Demonstrate actual exploitation or do not submit.
04 Inflating severity Claiming critical for a low-impact finding erodes trust. Let the evidence speak for itself.
05 Missing reproduction steps If the triager cannot reproduce it, the report is effectively worthless regardless of the actual severity.
06 Scanner output as a report Pasting raw Nuclei or Burp Scanner output shows zero effort. Validate the finding manually, then write a human-readable report.
07 Reporting known issues (CVEs without context) "Your server runs Apache 2.4.49 which has CVE-2021-41773" without confirming exploitability is just a version check.
08 Submitting during program changes When programs update their scope, bugs in newly excluded areas may be rejected retroactively.
09 Threatening public disclosure This violates every platform's terms of service. It may also be illegal depending on jurisdiction.
10 Poor communication during triage Not responding to clarification requests within 48 hours, arguing publicly, or being rude to triagers.

Discussion Prompts
  • 01 You find a reflected XSS that only triggers in Internet Explorer 11. The program scope does not specify browser requirements. How do you assess the severity, and how do you frame the report to maximize acceptance?
  • 02 A triager downgrades your critical IDOR to medium severity because "the exposed data is not sensitive." You disagree. Walk through the steps you would take to advocate for a higher severity without damaging the relationship.
  • 03 You discover three separate low-severity findings that, when chained together, create a critical account takeover. Should you submit them as one report or three? What are the strategic trade-offs?
