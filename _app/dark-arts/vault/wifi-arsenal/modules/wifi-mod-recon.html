Section 01

The Reconnaissance Mindset

Wireless reconnaissance is the practice of systematically gathering intelligence about the 802.11 radio frequency environment before conducting any active engagement. In a well-executed assessment, recon is not a single step — it is an ongoing discipline that informs every subsequent decision. A target network discovered during passive monitoring tells you the encryption type, approximate signal strength, client device count, and channel occupancy. That intelligence shapes everything from wordlist selection to physical positioning.

The most critical distinction in wireless recon is the boundary between passive and active techniques. Passive reconnaissance involves listening without transmitting — your adapter receives frames but never sends any. Active reconnaissance involves transmitting probe requests, deauthentication frames, or other traffic to elicit responses. This distinction has both technical and legal consequences.

👁 Passive Reconnaissance

  • ▸No frames transmitted — RF-silent
  • ▸Captures beacons, probe responses, data frames
  • ▸Undetectable by WIDS systems in many configurations
  • ▸Legal grey area: capturing broadcast traffic
  • ▸Cannot discover hidden SSIDs without client traffic
  • ▸Tools: airodump-ng (no injection), Kismet, tcpdump

Active Reconnaissance

  • ▸Transmits probe requests, deauth frames, beacons
  • ▸Can reveal hidden SSIDs via forced probe responses
  • ▸Detectable by WIDS — leaves evidence in RF environment
  • ▸Requires explicit authorization to perform legally
  • ▸Faster intelligence gathering but higher risk profile
  • ▸Tools: aireplay-ng, bettercap (active modules)
👁
OPSEC During Recon
Operational security begins before the first packet is captured. Use a dedicated assessment adapter with MAC address randomization. Remove or disable adapters not in use. In sensitive engagements, conduct initial passive recon from a vehicle at perimeter distance to avoid physical detection.
Discipline
Scope Discipline
A wireless scan in a dense urban environment will reveal dozens of networks beyond your authorized scope. Rigorous scope documentation — including specific BSSIDs and SSID patterns — prevents accidental engagement of out-of-scope systems and protects both the assessor and the client legally.
Methodology
Legal Framework
Even passive interception of encrypted wireless frames may violate the Electronic Communications Privacy Act (ECPA) in the US, the Computer Misuse Act in the UK, or equivalent statutes. Obtain explicit written authorization that specifies the techniques authorized, not just the target systems.
Authorization
Hardware Selection
Not all wireless adapters support monitor mode or packet injection. Assessment work requires an adapter with a chipset explicitly supported by the Linux mac80211 subsystem — Atheros, Ralink, and Realtek chipsets are commonly used. The Alfa AWUS036ACH is a field-standard choice for its dual-band capability and robust injection support.
Equipment
The Authorization Problem
Passive wireless reconnaissance (listening only) captures data about every device in range — including devices, networks, and individuals with no relationship to your authorized target. In a shared office building, a single airodump-ng session may capture client MAC addresses, device fingerprints, and traffic patterns from dozens of third-party organizations. Document this risk in your engagement scope and rules of engagement before proceeding.
Section 02

Monitor Mode Fundamentals

Wireless network interfaces operate in different modes that control which frames the kernel passes to userspace. Understanding these modes is not optional background knowledge — it directly determines what data you can capture and what attacks you can execute.

Managed Mode
The default operating mode for client devices. The NIC associates with an access point (or works as a standalone client). The kernel 802.11 stack filters out all frames not addressed to the adapter's own MAC address. You cannot capture other devices' traffic in this mode.
Default
📡
Monitor Mode
The NIC captures all 802.11 frames in range regardless of BSSID, destination MAC, or association state. The kernel passes raw 802.11 frames (including management, control, and data frames) directly to the capture interface. This is the mode required for all wireless assessment operations.
Assessment Required
Promiscuous Mode
Often confused with monitor mode. Promiscuous mode applies to wired (Ethernet) interfaces and allows capture of all frames on the connected segment — not filtered by destination MAC. On wireless adapters, promiscuous mode does not provide monitor mode capability. The distinction matters when reviewing tcpdump flags.
Ethernet Concept
Master Mode (AP)
Configures the adapter to act as an access point, broadcasting beacons and managing client associations. Used for evil twin attacks, rogue AP deployment, and captive portal scenarios. Requires hostapd in practice for full AP functionality, or bettercap's ap module for rapid setup.
AP Emulation

Enabling Monitor Mode with airmon-ng

The airmon-ng utility manages monitor mode on wireless interfaces. On modern Kali Linux systems, airmon-ng wraps the underlying iw and ip commands, handling interface creation, naming, and interference process management automatically.

Step 1 — Identify Your Wireless Interface
Enumerate adapters and verify chipset support
▼

Before enabling monitor mode, identify the interface name and confirm it supports injection. Interface names vary: wlan0, wlan1, wlp2s0, wlx... depending on the adapter and system configuration.

# List all wireless interfaces with driver and chipset info $ airmon-ng PHY Interface Driver Chipset phy0 wlan0 rt2800usb Ralink Technology, Corp. RT2870/RT3070 phy1 wlan1 ath9k_htc Qualcomm Atheros Communications AR9271 # Also check with iw for supported modes $ iw list | grep -A 8 "Supported interface modes" Supported interface modes: * IBSS * managed * AP * monitor # Must be present * P2P-client * P2P-GO
Step 2 — Kill Interfering Processes
NetworkManager, wpa_supplicant, and dhclient can disrupt monitor mode
▼

System services that manage wireless connectivity — particularly NetworkManager and wpa_supplicant — actively manage the adapter and will interfere with monitor mode by periodically reasserting managed mode or sending probe requests. airmon-ng detects these processes and can terminate them automatically.

# Check for interfering processes $ sudo airmon-ng check Found 3 processes that could cause trouble. PID Name 718 NetworkManager 892 wpa_supplicant 1204 dhclient # Kill all interfering processes automatically $ sudo airmon-ng check kill Killing these processes: PID Name 718 NetworkManager 892 wpa_supplicant 1204 dhclient

Note that killing NetworkManager will drop any active wired or wireless connections on the system. On a remote assessment box accessed via SSH over a separate wired interface, this is acceptable. Killing NetworkManager on a system with only wireless connectivity will terminate your session.

Step 3 — Start Monitor Mode
Create the monitor interface (wlan0mon)
▼
# Enable monitor mode — creates wlan0mon virtual interface $ sudo airmon-ng start wlan0 PHY Interface Driver Chipset phy0 wlan0 rt2800usb Ralink Technology Corp. RT2870 (mac80211 monitor mode vif enabled for [phy0]wlan0 on [phy0]wlan0mon) (mac80211 station mode vif disabled for [phy0]wlan0) # Start on a specific channel (reduces channel-hop overhead) $ sudo airmon-ng start wlan0 6

On some systems and drivers, airmon-ng may rename the interface differently. Always confirm the resulting interface name from the command output before proceeding.

Step 4 — Verify Monitor Mode with iwconfig
Confirm the interface is in the correct mode
▼
# Verify mode with iwconfig (legacy but reliable) $ iwconfig wlan0mon wlan0mon IEEE 802.11 Mode:Monitor Frequency:2.412 GHz Tx-Power=20 dBm Retry short limit:7 RTS thr:off Fragment thr:off Power Management:off # Or use the modern iw command $ iw dev wlan0mon info Interface wlan0mon ifindex 5 wdev 0x100000001 addr 00:c0:ca:97:d2:4a type monitor wiphy 0 channel 6 (2437 MHz), width: 20 MHz, center1: 2437 MHz

Confirm the Mode field reads "Monitor". If it reads "Managed", the monitor interface was not created correctly — re-run airmon-ng or attempt the manual method via iw set type monitor.

Stopping Monitor Mode
Restore the interface to managed mode cleanly
▼
# Stop monitor mode and restore the managed interface $ sudo airmon-ng stop wlan0mon PHY Interface Driver Chipset phy0 wlan0mon rt2800usb Ralink Technology Corp. RT2870 (mac80211 monitor mode vif disabled for [phy0]wlan0mon) (mac80211 station mode vif enabled for [phy0]wlan0) # Restart NetworkManager if you killed it earlier $ sudo systemctl start NetworkManager
✓
Manual Method with iw
If airmon-ng fails (common with newer kernels or unusual driver configurations), you can enable monitor mode manually: bring the interface down with ip link set wlan0 down, set the type with iw dev wlan0 set type monitor, then bring it back up with ip link set wlan0 up. This approach does not rename the interface and preserves the original name.
Section 03

Network Discovery with airodump-ng

airodump-ng is the wireless packet capture component of the Aircrack-ng suite. In its default configuration it performs channel hopping across all 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz channels, displaying discovered access points and associated client devices in real time. Its output is dense with information that requires careful interpretation.

Basic Scan — Channel Hopping Mode

# Start a broad scan across all channels $ sudo airodump-ng wlan0mon # Limit to 2.4 GHz band only (faster channel cycling) $ sudo airodump-ng --band bg wlan0mon # Include 5 GHz channels (requires dual-band adapter) $ sudo airodump-ng --band abg wlan0mon

Reading the Output — AP Table Columns Explained

The airodump-ng display is split into two sections: the upper table lists access points, the lower table lists stations (client devices). Each column carries specific intelligence value.

Column Example Value What It Tells You
BSSID AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF The MAC address of the access point radio. The OUI (first 3 octets) identifies the hardware vendor — useful for fingerprinting AP manufacturer and model families.
PWR -62 Received signal strength in dBm. Higher (less negative) is stronger. -30 to -50: excellent, close range. -60 to -75: good. -80 to -90: marginal. Below -90: unreliable. Used to estimate physical distance and select optimal attack position.
Beacons 1842 Number of beacon frames captured from this AP. APs broadcast beacons approximately 10 times per second. A high beacon count confirms sustained proximity and reception quality. Low counts may indicate intermittent reception or a distant AP.
#Data 247 Count of data frames captured. Higher values indicate active clients generating traffic — both more opportunity to capture handshakes and more IV data if WEP is in use. A value of 0 with associated clients suggests they are idle.
#/s 18 Data frames captured per second — real-time traffic rate. Useful for identifying active sessions and estimating when WEP IVs will accumulate to crackable counts.
CH 6 The 802.11 channel the AP is operating on. 2.4 GHz non-overlapping channels: 1, 6, 11. 5 GHz channels: 36, 40, 44, 48, 52... (varies by region). Channel congestion analysis reveals overlapping networks that may cause interference during assessment.
MB 54e Maximum link speed supported by the AP. Indicates 802.11 standard generation. The 'e' suffix indicates short preamble support. Dot (.) indicates WPS enabled. Used to infer approximate 802.11 standard (11n, 11ac, 11ax) and attack surface.
ENC WPA2 Encryption protocol: OPN (open), WEP, WPA, WPA2, WPA3, or WPA2/WPA3 (transition mode). Immediately identifies attack feasibility — OPN requires no credential attacks; WEP is trivially crackable; WPA2-PSK is handshake-attackable; WPA3 requires different techniques.
CIPHER CCMP The specific cipher in use: CCMP (AES), TKIP, or WRAP. TKIP networks running WPA2 are running in compatibility mode and are weaker than AES/CCMP. GCMP indicates WPA3.
AUTH PSK Authentication method: PSK (pre-shared key / personal mode) or MGT (802.1X enterprise). PSK networks are the primary target for handshake-based cracking. MGT requires different attack vectors (evil twin, RADIUS capture).
ESSID CorpNet_5G The human-readable network name. A blank or <length: 0> entry indicates a hidden SSID network (SSID is not broadcast in beacons). ESSID naming patterns often reveal organizational structure, site names, or IT naming conventions useful for OSINT correlation.

Targeted Capture — Locking to a Single Network

After identifying a target from the broad scan, lock airodump-ng to that network's specific channel and BSSID. This eliminates channel-hop lag and captures all frames — including the 4-way handshake — more reliably. Always write captures to disk for offline analysis.

# Lock to target BSSID on channel 6, write to capture files $ sudo airodump-ng \ --bssid AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF \ --channel 6 \ --write target_capture \ wlan0mon # Output files created: # target_capture-01.cap — raw 802.11 frames (pcap format) # target_capture-01.csv — AP and client summary data # target_capture-01.kismet.csv — Kismet-compatible format # target_capture-01.kismet.netxml — Kismet XML format # Capture only EAPOL/handshake frames (reduces file size) $ sudo airodump-ng \ --bssid AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF \ -c 6 \ --output-format pcap \ -w handshake_only \ wlan0mon

Client Association Tracking

The lower section of the airodump-ng display shows client stations. This data is equally valuable to the AP table — it reveals which devices are associated with which network, and which devices are probing for networks they previously connected to.

# Station table format STATION PWR Rate Lost Frames Notes Probes B4:F0:AB:12:34:56 -71 54 -54 0 892 CorpNet_5G C8:A7:2F:98:76:54 -83 1 - 1 0 14 Starbucks_WiFi, home_network ↑ Probe requests reveal previously connected SSIDs # Unassociated clients (STATION with BSSID = "(not associated)") # These devices are actively sending probe requests — fingerprint them
ℹ
Probe Requests as Intelligence
Client devices send probe requests containing the SSIDs of previously connected networks when searching for known networks. This creates a passive intelligence opportunity: an unassociated client probing for "CompanyVPN_Guest" or "HomeOffice_Net" reveals previous locations and network memberships without any active scanning on your part. This is the mechanism that karma/MANA attacks exploit for evil twin association.

Alternative Recon: bettercap

bettercap is a modern, modular network attack framework that has largely superseded older tools like ettercap for active network manipulation. Its WiFi module provides a clean, interactive interface for wireless reconnaissance that is architecturally superior to the Aircrack-ng suite for some assessment workflows — particularly when you need real-time filtering, scripting, or integration with other bettercap attack modules in a single session.

bettercap Advantages

  • ▸Interactive console with live filtering
  • ▸Module system integrates WiFi + ARP + HTTP in one session
  • ▸Built-in scripting with caplets (.cap files)
  • ▸REST API for programmatic control
  • ▸Web UI for visual monitoring
  • ▸Active development and maintenance (2024+)

airodump-ng Advantages

  • ▸Lower overhead — single-purpose, fast
  • ▸Better passive capture fidelity in some drivers
  • ▸Handshake capture tightly integrated with aircrack-ng
  • ▸Simpler to script in bash pipelines
  • ▸More granular channel control options
  • ▸Industry standard — universally recognized output format

Channel Filtering and Targeting

bettercap Caplets — Scripted Reconnaissance

Caplets are bettercap's scripting format. A recon caplet can automate the full initial discovery phase, reducing manual interaction and standardizing the reconnaissance workflow across assessments.

# wifi-recon.cap — standardized recon caplet set $ {restapi.username} admin set $ {restapi.password} password # Enable the REST API for external tooling integration api.rest on # Start WiFi recon in dual-band mode set wifi.recon.channel 0 wifi.recon on # Sort by signal strength, refresh every 5 seconds set wifi.show.sort rssi asc set ticker.commands "clear; wifi.show" set ticker.period 5 ticker on # Run the caplet: sudo bettercap -iface wlan0mon -caplet wifi-recon.cap
ℹ
When to Choose bettercap over airodump-ng
Choose bettercap when you need to chain wireless recon with other attack phases in a single session — for example, conducting WiFi recon while simultaneously running ARP spoofing or DNS spoofing on a network you have access to. Choose airodump-ng when you need maximum capture fidelity for handshake collection, are working with a wordlist cracking workflow, or need to produce output files in a format expected by downstream tools like aircrack-ng or hashcat.
Section 05

Hidden SSID Detection

Hidden SSIDs — also called cloaked or non-broadcasting networks — are access points configured to omit the ESSID field from beacon frames. The AP still transmits beacons (it must, to maintain timing synchronization), but the ESSID field is filled with null bytes or has a reported length of zero. Network administrators deploy this configuration believing it provides additional security through obscurity.

It does not. The SSID is revealed in multiple other frame types that cannot be suppressed without breaking 802.11 association entirely. Understanding why SSID hiding fails requires understanding the full 802.11 association flow.

SSID Hiding Is Security Theater
A hidden SSID network is visible to any attacker within radio range within seconds of a legitimate client connecting. Probe request frames, probe response frames, and association request frames all contain the plaintext SSID in cleartext — these frames are never encrypted regardless of the network's encryption configuration. SSID hiding adds zero meaningful security against a wireless-capable attacker and may actually create privacy problems for clients (forced to broadcast probe requests for the hidden SSID everywhere they go).

Why the SSID Cannot Stay Hidden

📡
Beacon Frames (Blank ESSID)
The AP continues to transmit beacons roughly 10 times per second. The ESSID information element is present but contains null bytes or zero length. airodump-ng shows this as blank ESSID or <length: N> where N is the real SSID length — giving you the password length as a bonus data point.
Visible Immediately
Probe Request Frames
Client devices that have previously connected to a hidden SSID network must transmit directed probe requests — broadcast frames containing the plaintext SSID — to locate the hidden AP. Every device connected to the network leaks the SSID passively whenever it searches for the network.
Client Leaks SSID
📢
Probe Response Frames
When an AP receives a directed probe request for its SSID, it responds with a probe response frame that includes the plaintext SSID. An attacker monitoring the channel sees this exchange and recovers the SSID without any active attack — purely from passive monitoring of legitimate client activity.
AP Responds in Clear
Association Request Frames
During the 802.11 association process, the client sends an association request frame that contains the SSID in plaintext. This occurs every time a device connects to the network — on startup, after sleep, after roaming. These frames are unencrypted even on WPA3 networks.
Every Association Leaks

Passive Detection — Wait for Client Activity

# Run airodump-ng and watch for probe requests from clients $ sudo airodump-ng wlan0mon # In the AP table, the hidden network appears as: BSSID PWR Beacons #Data CH ENC ESSID AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:03 -74 892 12 11 WPA2 <length: 9> ↑ SSID is 9 characters — captured even without the SSID itself # In the station table, watch for directed probe requests STATION PWR Rate Lost Frames Probes C8:A7:2F:98:76:54 -78 11e-11e 0 44 HiddenNet ↑ Client revealed the SSID in its probe request

Active Detection — Forced Deauthentication

If no clients are currently connected or probing, you can force SSID revelation by sending a deauthentication frame to a client associated with the hidden AP. The client will disconnect and immediately attempt to reconnect — sending probe request and association request frames that expose the hidden SSID in plaintext.

Active Technique — Requires Authorization
Sending deauthentication frames is an active attack. It disrupts the target client's connectivity, is detectable by WIDS systems, and requires explicit authorization in your scope of work. Do not perform this against unconfirmed in-scope networks — confirming the target BSSID belongs to your authorized scope is mandatory before any active frame injection.
# Step 1: Lock airodump-ng to the hidden AP's channel # and record the BSSID of an associated client from the station table $ sudo airodump-ng --bssid AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:03 -c 11 wlan0mon # Step 2: In a second terminal, send targeted deauth to the associated client # -0 = deauth attack, 3 = send 3 deauth frames, -a = AP BSSID, -c = client MAC $ sudo aireplay-ng \ -0 3 \ -a AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:03 \ -c C8:A7:2F:98:76:54 \ wlan0mon # Step 3: Watch the airodump-ng output — the SSID will appear # in the Probes column as the client reconnects BSSID PWR Beacons ESSID AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:03 -74 896 HiddenNet <-- SSID revealed after client reconnect
Using bettercap for Hidden SSID Discovery
Integrated passive and active detection workflow
▼

bettercap's WiFi module displays hidden networks and can automate the deauth-based SSID revelation process with fewer manual steps than the airodump-ng + aireplay-ng workflow.

Probe Request Analysis with Wireshark
Manual frame inspection for SSID extraction from pcap
▼

When reviewing airodump-ng capture files offline, Wireshark's 802.11 dissector can filter probe requests and association frames to extract SSIDs that may not have appeared in the live airodump-ng display.

# Wireshark display filter — all probe request frames wlan.fc.type_subtype == 0x0004 # Filter for probe requests containing SSID information elements wlan.ssid and wlan.fc.type_subtype == 0x0004 # From tshark (CLI Wireshark) — extract SSIDs from probe requests in a pcap $ tshark -r target_capture-01.cap \ -Y "wlan.fc.type_subtype == 0x0004" \ -T fields \ -e wlan.sa \ -e wlan.ssid | sort -u
Section 06

Signal Analysis & Target Selection

Raw reconnaissance data requires interpretation before it becomes actionable intelligence. Signal strength values, channel assignments, and client counts all inform the prioritization and logistics of a wireless assessment. Understanding how to read this data prevents wasted effort pursuing targets that are physically inaccessible or technically unsuitable.

Interpreting PWR (Signal Strength) Values

The PWR column in airodump-ng shows received signal strength in dBm (decibels relative to one milliwatt). These values are negative — closer to zero means stronger signal. The scale is logarithmic: a 3 dBm difference represents approximately double the signal power.

-30 dBm
Exceptional
You are within meters of the AP. All attacks are feasible. Capture quality is excellent.
-50 dBm
Strong
Excellent working range. Reliable capture. Well within injection distance for all techniques.
-65 dBm
Good
Reliable capture with minor packet loss. Injection should work. Typical outdoor assessment range.
-75 dBm
Marginal
Intermittent capture. Injection success rate drops significantly. Move closer before attacking.
-85 dBm
Weak
Poor capture quality. Injection likely to fail. Target may be out of effective attack range.
-1 dBm
Unknown
A value of -1 in airodump-ng means the driver did not report signal strength for this frame. Not a real measurement.

Channel Congestion Analysis

In the 2.4 GHz band, only three channels are truly non-overlapping: 1, 6, and 11. All other channels overlap with at least two neighbors, causing co-channel and adjacent-channel interference. A heavily congested channel reduces capture quality and injection reliability.

# Count networks per channel from airodump-ng CSV output $ awk -F, 'NR>2 && $4!="" {print $4}' airodump-01.csv | \ sort | uniq -c | sort -rn 14 6 # Channel 6 is extremely congested — 14 competing networks 8 1 # Channel 1 has 8 networks 5 11 # Channel 11 is least congested 3 36 # 5 GHz channels are much less congested # If your target is on a congested channel, consider: # 1. Increasing adapter TX power (within legal limits) # 2. Moving physically closer to the target # 3. Using a directional antenna to reduce interference from competing networks

Target Prioritization Framework

High-Value Target Indicators
What makes a network worth prioritizing in an assessment
▼
  • WPA2-PSK with active clients — Deauth and capture the 4-way handshake; high-value if the PSK is weak
  • WEP encryption — Immediate critical finding; recoverable in minutes regardless of key complexity
  • Open (OPN) network — All traffic in cleartext; immediate credential and session interception possible
  • WPA2-PSK with signal above -65 dBm — Reliable attack range; injection and handshake capture highly feasible
  • Multiple clients associated — More opportunities for handshake capture; also more traffic for passive intelligence
  • Organizational SSID naming patterns — "CorpNet", "Internal", "Mgmt" suggest access to internal network segments rather than guest VLANs
  • Hidden SSID with known clients — Suggests a network the organization is actively trying to conceal; may be a management or OOB network
🚫
Lower-Priority or Out-of-Scope Indicators
Targets to document but deprioritize or avoid
▼
  • Signal below -80 dBm — Likely out of practical attack range; move closer or treat as out-of-scope
  • WPA3 (non-transition mode) with no clients — Dragonfly handshake attack requires online brute-force; high complexity, low ROI without active clients
  • ISP/carrier hotspot SSIDs — "xfinitywifi", "NETGEAR-Guest" — typically third-party networks; confirm scope before touching
  • Zero data frames, zero clients, very high beacons — AP may be a honeypot or WIDS sensor; unusual traffic patterns warrant investigation before engagement
  • EAP/MGT auth without captive portal — Requires RADIUS credential capture via evil twin; more complex than PSK attacks; separate planning needed
Directional Antennas for Extended Range
Yagi and panel antennas for perimeter assessment
▼

Omnidirectional antennas (standard on most adapters) radiate and receive in all directions. Directional antennas — Yagi, panel, parabolic dish — concentrate RF energy in a narrow beam. For perimeter assessments from a vehicle or adjacent building, directional antennas can extend effective range from 100 meters to 500 meters or more.

  • Yagi antenna (8-18 dBi) — High gain, narrow beam. Ideal for point-to-point targeting from long range.
  • Panel antenna (8-14 dBi) — Moderately directional with wider beam than Yagi. Balances coverage with gain.
  • Parabolic dish (24+ dBi) — Maximum gain for kilometer-range assessments. Used in specialist long-range scenarios.
  • Omnidirectional stick (5-9 dBi) — All-directions reception with modest gain improvement. Best for dense urban environments.

A directional antenna paired with an assessment adapter can achieve effective signal improvement equivalent to moving significantly closer to the target. A 9 dBi gain antenna effectively triples your perceived signal strength — turning a -85 dBm marginal reading into a -76 dBm workable signal.

✓
Reconnaissance Documentation Discipline
Every wireless assessment should begin with a structured recon capture saved to disk. Write airodump-ng output with the --write flag from the start of recon, not after target selection. Early captures may contain transient clients, handshakes, or probe requests that disappear from the live display. The CSV output serves as your assessment log and deliverable evidence of network conditions at time of assessment.
Section 07

Key Takeaways

👁
Passive First, Always
Complete passive reconnaissance before any active technique. Passive recon is safer, undetectable in most WIDS configurations, and sufficient to identify most targets.
Monitor Mode is the Foundation
Every wireless assessment technique requires monitor mode. Verify it correctly before proceeding. A misconfigured adapter produces useless data and wastes engagement time.
Read the Full airodump-ng Output
The station table is as valuable as the AP table. Probe requests, client counts, and data rates reveal intelligence that BSSID listings alone cannot provide.
Hidden SSIDs Are Not Hidden
Any network with active clients is discoverable within minutes of passive monitoring. SSID hiding is a documentation-worthy finding: the client believes they have additional security controls but they do not.
Signal Strength Drives Logistics
PWR values determine physical positioning requirements. Do not attempt injection attacks against targets below -80 dBm — move closer or use directional antenna before investing time in an attack that will fail due to signal margin.
Write Everything to Disk
Always run airodump-ng with --write from the start of recon. Transient events — spontaneous handshakes, probe requests from passing devices — cannot be recaptured if missed in the live display.
