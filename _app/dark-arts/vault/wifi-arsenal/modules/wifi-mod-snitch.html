Section 01

📡 What Is AirSnitch?

AirSnitch is an open-source wireless intrusion detection system designed to run on low-cost embedded hardware — principally the Raspberry Pi — and monitor the 802.11 RF environment passively for indicators of attack. Unlike an access point or a penetration testing tool, AirSnitch never transmits; it only listens. It places a compatible wireless adapter into monitor mode, captures raw 802.11 frames, and applies a set of detection rules to identify anomalous activity across the entire spectrum it can observe.

The project emerged from a gap in the defensive tooling landscape: commercial wireless intrusion prevention systems (WIPS) such as Cisco Adaptive Wireless IPS and Aruba RFProtect cost tens of thousands of dollars to deploy at scale. AirSnitch gives defenders — from home lab operators to under-resourced security teams — a credible passive wireless sensor for the cost of a Raspberry Pi and a monitor-mode-capable USB adapter.

👁
Passive-Only Monitoring
AirSnitch operates exclusively in receive mode. It never injects frames, never transmits deauthentication packets, and never associates with any AP. This passive posture is legally important: active WIPS that inject deauth frames to block rogue APs can violate the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act and FCC regulations. AirSnitch detects and alerts; it does not act.
Passive / Legal
Frame-Level Visibility
By operating in monitor mode, AirSnitch captures management frames — Beacon, Probe Request, Probe Response, Authentication, Association, and Deauthentication — that a normally associated client never sees. The detection logic operates on the full 802.11 frame header, giving visibility into attacks that are invisible to wired network sensors.
802.11 Frames
Open-Source and Extensible
AirSnitch's detection rules are written in Python and structured as independent detector classes. Adding a custom detection rule requires only implementing a class with a process_frame() method and registering it in the detector pipeline. The community has contributed detectors for Karma attacks, PMKID harvesting attempts, and beacon stuffing.
Open Source
Continuous Channel Hopping
Wi-Fi operates across 14 channels in 2.4 GHz and up to 29 non-overlapping channels in 5 GHz. A single sensor cannot monitor all channels simultaneously. AirSnitch implements configurable channel hopping, dwelling on each channel for a configurable duration before rotating. Multi-sensor deployments with each sensor locked to a subset of channels provide continuous coverage.
Channel Hopping
ℹ
WIDS vs WIPS: Detection vs Prevention
A Wireless Intrusion Detection System (WIDS) observes and alerts. A Wireless Intrusion Prevention System (WIPS) also takes automated countermeasures, such as injecting deauthentication frames to disconnect rogue clients or block connections to rogue APs. AirSnitch is a WIDS. WIPS functionality in the United States requires specific regulatory and legal authorization because frame injection can disrupt other users of the RF spectrum. For most organizations, a WIDS integrated into a SOC workflow provides the necessary visibility without the legal exposure of automated RF countermeasures.
Project History and Ecosystem Context
How AirSnitch fits among Kismet, Wireshark, and commercial WIPS
▼

AirSnitch occupies a distinct niche from established wireless tools. Kismet is a comprehensive wireless network detector and packet sniffer that excels at discovery and logging but is not purpose-built for real-time IDS alerting. Wireshark is a protocol analyzer rather than a detection engine. Commercial WIPS platforms such as Cisco Catalyst Center Wireless, Aruba AirWave, and Extreme Networks AirDefense provide enterprise-grade detection but require significant investment and typically integrate only with the vendor's own AP hardware.

AirSnitch's design goal is a deployable sensor in under 30 minutes on commodity hardware, with alerting pipelines compatible with syslog, SMTP, and HTTP webhooks. It is best understood as a lightweight IDS head that can forward structured alert data to a full SIEM for correlation and retention, rather than a standalone security console.

  • First public release: 2021 as a proof-of-concept deauth detector built on Scapy
  • Subsequent versions added modular detector architecture and multi-adapter support
  • Integration modules for Splunk HEC, Elasticsearch, and syslog-ng added in 2022
  • Community-contributed detectors for PMKID capture attempts and Karma-style responses
Section 02

Hardware & Setup

AirSnitch requires three things: a Raspberry Pi (any model from Pi 3B onward), a monitor-mode-capable wireless adapter, and a stable power supply. The built-in WiFi adapter on the Pi cannot be placed in monitor mode reliably across all platforms; a dedicated USB adapter using a chipset with proven Linux driver support is required for the IDS interface. The built-in adapter can still handle internet connectivity for management and alert forwarding if needed.

Recommended Hardware

Raspberry Pi 4B / 5
Primary Sensor Platform
Pi 4B (4GB RAM) or Pi 5 is preferred for multi-adapter deployments or when running local log storage and forwarding. Pi 3B+ is sufficient for single-adapter passive monitoring with remote syslog. The Pi Zero 2W is usable for ultra-compact single-channel deployments.
Alfa AWUS036ACHM
Monitor Mode Adapter — 2.4/5 GHz
MT7610U chipset. Dual-band, highly reliable monitor mode and injection (injection capability unused by AirSnitch but indicates driver maturity). Maintained driver support in mainstream Linux kernel. Recommended for coverage in both bands simultaneously with dual-adapter setups.
Alfa AWUS036ACM
Monitor Mode Adapter — AC1200
MT7612U chipset. 802.11ac capable, reliable monitor mode, broad channel hopping range including 5 GHz DFS channels. Good choice where 5 GHz coverage is the priority. Works out-of-the-box on Raspberry Pi OS.
TP-Link TL-WN722N v1
Budget 2.4 GHz Adapter
AR9271 chipset (v1 only — v2 and v3 use Realtek and are not compatible). Inexpensive, widely available, reliable monitor mode for 2.4 GHz deployments. Verify the version before purchasing; chip revision identification is covered in Module 02: Hardware & Setup.
Chipset Verification Is Non-Negotiable
Many popular USB WiFi adapters have undergone silent hardware revisions that replaced a fully supported chipset with one that lacks monitor mode capability. The TP-Link TL-WN722N is the most notorious example. Always verify the exact chipset with lsusb -v before purchasing at scale. The Alfa AWUS036ACM and AWUS036ACHM have maintained consistent chipsets across revisions and are the safest choices for bulk procurement.

Installation

AirSnitch installs via pip into a Python virtual environment. The following sequence covers a clean Raspberry Pi OS Lite installation:

# Update system and install dependencies sudo apt update && sudo apt install -y python3-pip python3-venv aircrack-ng tcpdump # Install AirSnitch into a virtual environment python3 -m venv /opt/airsnitch/venv source /opt/airsnitch/venv/bin/activate pip install airsnitch scapy # Place the wireless adapter into monitor mode sudo ip link set wlan1 down sudo iw dev wlan1 set type monitor sudo ip link set wlan1 up # Verify monitor mode is active iw dev wlan1 info

Configuration File

AirSnitch reads its primary configuration from /etc/airsnitch/airsnitch.conf. The following is a production-ready base configuration for a dual-band single-sensor deployment:

# /etc/airsnitch/airsnitch.conf # AirSnitch Wireless IDS — Primary Configuration [interface] monitor_iface = wlan1mon # adapter in monitor mode management_iface = wlan0 # uplink for syslog/webhooks [channel_hopping] enabled = true bands = 2.4,5 # monitor both bands dwell_ms = 300 # milliseconds per channel skip_dfs = false # include 5 GHz DFS channels [detection] deauth_threshold = 10 # deauth frames/sec to trigger alert rogue_ap_check = true evil_twin_check = true new_device_alert = true ssid_spoof_check = true pmkid_detect = true [whitelist] known_ssids_file = /etc/airsnitch/known_ssids.txt known_bssids_file = /etc/airsnitch/known_bssids.txt [alerts] syslog_enabled = true syslog_host = 192.168.10.50 syslog_port = 514 syslog_protocol = udp webhook_enabled = false email_enabled = false
Systemd Service Configuration
Run AirSnitch as a persistent background service on boot
▼

For production deployments, AirSnitch should run as a systemd service that starts automatically on boot and restarts on failure. Create the unit file at /etc/systemd/system/airsnitch.service:

[Unit] Description=AirSnitch Wireless IDS After=network.target [Service] Type=simple User=root ExecStartPre=/sbin/ip link set wlan1 down ExecStartPre=/sbin/iw dev wlan1 set type monitor ExecStartPre=/sbin/ip link set wlan1 up ExecStart=/opt/airsnitch/venv/bin/airsnitch \ --interface wlan1mon \ --config /etc/airsnitch/airsnitch.conf Restart=on-failure RestartSec=10s [Install] WantedBy=multi-user.target
  • Enable with sudo systemctl enable --now airsnitch
  • Check status with sudo systemctl status airsnitch
  • Follow live logs with sudo journalctl -u airsnitch -f
Section 03

Detection Capabilities

AirSnitch implements a modular detection pipeline where each threat category is handled by a dedicated detector class. Frames captured from the monitor interface are passed sequentially through the enabled detector chain. Each detector maintains its own state (frame counters, BSSID registries, timing windows) and emits a structured alert object when its detection threshold is crossed.

🚫
Deauthentication Flood Detection
Identifying management frame flooding used for DoS and handshake capture
▼

Deauthentication frames (subtype 0x0C in the 802.11 management frame class) are used legitimately by clients and APs to end an association cleanly. In an attack context, they are sent at high volume — typically hundreds per second — to force clients to disassociate, either for denial-of-service or to capture the WPA2 4-way handshake that follows re-association.

AirSnitch's deauth detector counts deauthentication and disassociation frames per source BSSID per second using a sliding time window. When the count exceeds the configured threshold (default: 10 frames/second), an alert is generated with the source BSSID, target (broadcast or specific MAC), current count, and the channel on which frames were observed.

# Launch AirSnitch with explicit deauth detection and channel hopping sudo airsnitch \ --interface wlan1mon \ --config /etc/airsnitch/airsnitch.conf \ --scan \ --channel-hop \ --alert-deauth
  • Detects both broadcast deauth floods (targeting all clients) and unicast floods (targeting specific devices)
  • Correlates deauth source with known BSSID whitelist to distinguish legitimate AP management from attack
  • Alert includes reason code from the deauth frame; attackers commonly use reason 7 (Class 3 frame from non-associated STA)
  • WPA3 networks with PMF Required are immune to deauth flooding; the detector notes PMF status parsed from beacon frames
🔱
Evil Twin and Rogue AP Detection
Detecting unauthorized APs advertising known SSIDs
▼

An evil twin attack creates a fraudulent AP that broadcasts the same SSID as a legitimate network. Clients configured for automatic reconnection may associate with the attacker's AP, exposing credentials, traffic, and session data. AirSnitch's rogue AP detector maintains a registry of authorized BSSIDs mapped to their advertised SSIDs, loaded from known_bssids.txt.

When a beacon frame is observed advertising a known SSID from a BSSID that is not in the whitelist, an alert is generated with the unauthorized BSSID, the spoofed SSID, the signal strength (RSSI), and the channel. For enterprise deployments, the authorized BSSID list is populated from the wireless controller's AP inventory via API.

  • Detects same-SSID impersonation: exact SSID match with an unauthorized BSSID
  • Detects SSID spoofing variants including added or removed spaces, Unicode lookalikes, and capitalization changes
  • RSSI value helps estimate the attacker's physical proximity — high RSSI suggests a sensor near the rogue AP
  • Works for both 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz; dual-band evil twins generate alerts on both sensors independently
New Device and First-Seen Alerts
Detecting previously unseen MACs in the wireless environment
▼

AirSnitch maintains a persistent device registry (stored as a SQLite database at /var/lib/airsnitch/devices.db) of every MAC address observed probing or associating within sensor range. When a MAC is observed for the first time, a "new device" informational alert is generated. In a strictly controlled environment such as a server room or secure facility, any new wireless device is a potential policy violation worth investigating.

  • Accounts for MAC address randomization used by modern iOS, Android, and Windows clients
  • Randomized MACs generate high new-device alert volume; configure new_device_alert for managed environments only
  • OUI lookup enrichment identifies device manufacturer from the MAC prefix for additional context
  • Configurable suppression period prevents alert storms when a legitimate new device is onboarded
PMKID Capture Attempt Detection
Identifying passive WPA2 credential harvesting without deauth
▼

The PMKID attack (using hcxdumptool and hcxtools) extracts the PMKID from a single EAPOL frame during the association process rather than requiring a complete 4-way handshake capture and a preceding deauth flood. This makes it stealthier than traditional handshake capture because it does not require client disassociation. The PMKID is a function of the PMK, AP BSSID, and client MAC, and can be attacked offline using hashcat.

AirSnitch's PMKID detector analyzes EAPOL frames and checks for the PMKID RSN element in EAPOL message 1 of the 4-way handshake. An unusual pattern of short EAPOL exchanges (message 1 with no subsequent messages) from multiple client MACs toward the same AP is flagged as a possible PMKID harvesting session.

  • Requires EAPOL frame capture, which is only possible when the sensor is on the same channel as the targeted AP
  • Alert includes the AP BSSID, the client MAC, and the captured PMKID value for evidence logging
  • WPA3 networks using SAE do not produce PMKIDs in the same manner; this detector is WPA2-specific
Channel Hopping Anomaly Detection
Identifying APs that rapidly change channels to evade detection
▼

A legitimate AP advertises a consistent operating channel in its beacon frames. A BSSID that is observed advertising different channels across successive beacon captures may indicate an attacker using channel-hopping to reduce the probability of being detected by a channel-locked sensor. AirSnitch logs the channel reported in each beacon and flags BSSIDs that change operating channels more than twice within a configurable observation window.

  • Distinguishes from legitimate band-steering beacons, which use separate BSSIDs per band
  • Particularly relevant for detecting evil twin APs that mirror channel changes of the legitimate AP they are mimicking
  • Alert severity is Low by default; combine with other detections for elevated classification
✓
Tuning Detection Thresholds
Default thresholds are calibrated for a typical office environment. High-density deployments (conference venues, large offices) generate substantially more management frame traffic and may require raising the deauth_threshold to reduce false positives from legitimate AP management operations. Run AirSnitch in logging-only mode (no alerts) for 48 hours in a new environment to establish a baseline before enabling alerting.
Section 04

Alert System

Every alert generated by AirSnitch is a structured JSON object containing a severity level, detector name, timestamp, sensor identifier, and the raw evidence fields specific to the detection type. This structured format is intentional: it allows downstream consumers (syslog forwarders, SIEM ingestors, webhook endpoints) to parse and route alerts without custom string-matching against free-text log lines.

Alert Severity Levels

Severity Detector Categories Recommended Response
Critical Confirmed evil twin with active clients, deauth flood exceeding 100 frames/sec, PMKID harvest in progress Immediate SOC escalation, physical security response if facility-based
High Rogue AP detected on authorized SSID, deauth flood (10-100 frames/sec), unauthorized AP with high RSSI Ticket creation, investigation within 1 hour, site survey if warranted
Medium Unauthorized AP on different SSID, SSID spoofing variant detected, WPA2 network observed in WPA3-only environment Ticket creation, investigation within business day
Low New device observed, probe request storm from a single client, channel hopping anomaly detected Log review, trend analysis, no immediate action required
Info Baseline updates, device registry additions, sensor channel change events Informational only, no action needed

  • ▸Rate-limited to prevent alert storms: max 1 email per alert type per 15 minutes

Syslog Configuration

Syslog forwarding is the primary integration path for SIEM ingestion. AirSnitch writes structured key-value pairs in RFC 5424 format. The following rsyslog rule on the receiving syslog server writes AirSnitch events to a dedicated log file for Splunk or Filebeat to consume:

# /etc/rsyslog.d/60-airsnitch.conf — on syslog collection server # Filter AirSnitch events by program name and write to dedicated file if $programname == 'airsnitch' then { action(type="omfile" file="/var/log/airsnitch/alerts.log" Template="RSYSLOG_SyslogProtocol23Format") stop } # Enable UDP reception on port 514 (in /etc/rsyslog.conf) # module(load="imudp") # input(type="imudp" port="514")

Section 05

Passive Monitoring Architecture

Understanding how AirSnitch observes the wireless environment without interfering with it requires understanding two foundational mechanisms: monitor mode operation and channel hopping strategy. These two concepts define both the visibility AirSnitch achieves and the fundamental limitations of any single-sensor passive IDS.

Monitor Mode Operation

A wireless adapter in managed mode filters received frames: it only passes frames addressed to its own MAC to the operating system. In monitor mode, the driver delivers all received frames — regardless of destination address — to the capturing application, wrapped in a RadioTap header that carries signal strength (RSSI), noise floor, data rate, and channel information. AirSnitch processes the full 802.11 frame hierarchy: MAC header, frame body, and FCS.

[ RF Environment ] | | 802.11 frames (all: beacons, probes, data, management) v [ wlan1mon — Monitor Mode Interface ] | | RadioTap + 802.11 raw frame v [ AirSnitch Frame Receiver ] (Scapy AsyncSniffer) | | parsed frame objects v [ Detector Pipeline ] |-- DeauthFloodDetector |-- RogueAPDetector |-- EvilTwinDetector |-- NewDeviceDetector |-- SSIDSpoofDetector |-- PMKIDDetector |-- ChannelAnomalyDetector | | alert objects (JSON) v [ Alert Router ] |-- Syslog Forwarder --> SIEM / syslog-ng / rsyslog |-- Webhook Emitter --> Slack / PagerDuty / SOAR |-- Email Sender --> SOC Mailbox |-- Local Log --> /var/log/airsnitch/alerts.log

Channel Hopping Strategy

A radio transceiver can only be tuned to one frequency at a time. The fundamental limitation of a single-adapter passive sensor is that while it is listening on channel 6, an attack occurring on channel 11 is invisible. AirSnitch addresses this through configurable channel hopping: the adapter cycles through a list of channels, dwelling for a configurable period before moving to the next.

🖌 2.4 GHz Channel Plan

  • ▸Channels 1, 6, 11 are the three non-overlapping channels in most regions
  • ▸For IDS purposes, hop all 14 channels to detect attackers using non-standard channels
  • ▸Deauth floods are high-volume and persist long enough to catch even at 300ms dwell
  • ▸Beacon frames repeat every 100ms per AP; a 300ms dwell catches all beacons reliably

5 GHz Channel Plan

  • ▸Up to 29 non-overlapping 20 MHz channels across the UNII-1, UNII-2, UNII-2e, UNII-3 bands
  • ▸DFS channels (UNII-2, UNII-2e) require radar avoidance; some adapters skip them by default
  • ▸Evil twin attacks often deploy on 5 GHz to avoid crowded 2.4 GHz detection environments
  • ▸Dual-adapter setups assign one adapter per band for continuous gap-free coverage
ℹ
The Hopping Blind Spot
With a single adapter hopping across 14 channels at 300ms per channel, the total cycle time is 4.2 seconds. A short burst attack on channel 6 may complete and disappear in the 3.9 seconds the sensor spends on other channels. This is not a flaw unique to AirSnitch — it is the fundamental physics of frequency-division multiplexing. The mitigation is multi-sensor deployment with dedicated per-band coverage, or channel-locked sensors on the highest-priority channels.
Section 06

Deployment Strategies

Where you place AirSnitch sensors determines the attacks you will detect and those you will miss. Coverage planning for a wireless IDS shares concepts with physical security camera placement: every sensor has a field of view determined by antenna characteristics and RF propagation physics, and blind spots between sensors are exploitable gaps. The goal is coverage of the protected RF perimeter, not just the areas served by authorized APs.

Sensor Placement Principles

  • ▲
    Perimeter Coverage First
    Place sensors at the RF perimeter of the monitored space — near exterior walls, parking areas, and loading docks. Attackers operating from outside the building generate high RSSI at perimeter sensors. Interior sensors may not see a parking-lot evil twin at all if building materials provide adequate attenuation.
  • ▲
    Cover All Floors and Stairwells
    RF propagates through floor and ceiling materials. A sensor on floor 3 may receive frames from an attacker on floor 2. However, detection reliability decreases with distance and obstruction. Multi-story buildings require at least one sensor per floor.
  • ▲
    High-Value Area Focus
    Server rooms, executive conference rooms, and finance areas warrant dedicated sensors. A rogue AP placed near the server room is categorically more dangerous than one in a public lobby. Sensor density should reflect asset criticality.
  • ▲
    Avoid RF Dead Zones
    Metal equipment racks, server enclosures, and HVAC units create RF shadows. Conduct a quick RF survey with the sensor hardware before finalizing placement. A sensor inside a metal enclosure will have severely degraded range. Mount sensors externally or in open ceiling spaces.
  • ▲
    Use PoE for Reliable Power and Connectivity
    Power over Ethernet (PoE) with a compatible Pi HAT delivers both power and a wired network connection through a single cable. This eliminates dependence on wireless management interfaces that can be disrupted by the very attacks the sensor is trying to detect. The Raspberry Pi PoE+ HAT is compatible with the Pi 4B.

Multi-Sensor Architectures

Home Lab (1-2 Sensors)
A single dual-band sensor placed centrally provides adequate coverage for a home or small office. Use channel hopping across all bands. Alert routing via email or Slack is sufficient. Log locally with optional forwarding to a home SIEM instance such as Elastic Stack on a Pi 5.
Home / SOHO
Small Office (3-6 Sensors)
One sensor per floor, with dedicated perimeter sensors. Assign one adapter per sensor to a specific band to eliminate hopping gaps. Centralize syslog to a local server and forward to a cloud SIEM. Distribute known_bssids.txt via Ansible or a shared Git repository.
SMB
Enterprise (10+ Sensors)
Sensor per zone, with dedicated channel-locked sensors on high-priority channels (1, 6, 11 in 2.4 GHz; 36, 40, 44, 48 in 5 GHz). Centralized configuration management via Ansible. Full SIEM integration with correlation rules. AirSnitch complements commercial WIPS rather than replacing it.
Enterprise
Industrial / OT Site
OT environments using wireless for sensor networks or HMI communications benefit from strict new-device alerting. Any unexpected wireless transmission in a process control zone is a high-severity event. Channel-locked sensors on the specific channels used by the OT wireless infrastructure.
ICS / OT
Section 07

Integration with SIEM

AirSnitch's structured JSON alert output makes it a natural fit for ingestion into any SIEM platform. The integration model is consistent regardless of which SIEM is in use: a log shipping agent on the sensor or syslog collector forwards alert events to the SIEM, where field extractions, correlation rules, and dashboards turn raw alerts into actionable visibility.

Splunk Integration via HEC
HTTP Event Collector for real-time alert streaming
▼

Splunk's HTTP Event Collector (HEC) accepts JSON events over HTTPS, making it straightforward to configure AirSnitch's webhook output to stream directly to Splunk without an intermediate syslog hop. The HEC token is configured with a specific index and sourcetype for AirSnitch events:

# airsnitch.conf — Splunk HEC webhook configuration [alerts] webhook_enabled = true webhook_url = https://splunk.corp.internal:8088/services/collector/event webhook_headers = Authorization: Splunk YOUR_HEC_TOKEN_HERE webhook_min_sev = low # send all severities to Splunk # Splunk Search — Critical and High AirSnitch alerts in the last hour # index=wireless sourcetype=airsnitch severity IN ("critical","high") # | table _time, sensor_id, alert_type, src_bssid, ssid, rssi, channel
  • Create a dedicated Splunk index (e.g., wireless) for AirSnitch events to simplify retention policy management
  • Configure a CIM-compliant sourcetype transform to map AirSnitch fields to the Splunk Common Information Model Network Traffic data model
  • Use Splunk Adaptive Response to create notable events from Critical AirSnitch alerts for SOC queue workflow integration
Elastic Stack (ELK) Integration
Filebeat shipping to Elasticsearch with Kibana dashboards
▼

The Elastic Stack integration uses Filebeat on the AirSnitch sensor to ship the local alert log file to Elasticsearch. Configure AirSnitch to write JSON-formatted alerts to /var/log/airsnitch/alerts.json (one JSON object per line), then configure Filebeat to monitor that file:

# /etc/filebeat/filebeat.yml (on AirSnitch sensor) filebeat.inputs: - type: log enabled: true paths: - /var/log/airsnitch/alerts.json json.keys_under_root: true json.add_error_key: true fields: data_stream.dataset: airsnitch.alerts data_stream.namespace: default output.elasticsearch: hosts: ["https://elastic.corp.internal:9200"] api_key: "YOUR_API_KEY_HERE" index: "airsnitch-alerts-%{+yyyy.MM.dd}"
  • Import the provided AirSnitch Kibana dashboard template from the project repository for out-of-the-box visualization
  • Configure an index lifecycle management (ILM) policy to roll over hot indices after 7 days and delete after 90 days
  • Use Kibana Lens to build a real-time alert rate map segmented by sensor_id and alert_type

Correlation with Wired IDS

Wireless alerts gain significantly more analytical value when correlated with wired network events. A deauth flood detected by AirSnitch followed by an authentication event from a new MAC on the wired network within minutes suggests a wireless-to-wired lateral movement attempt. The following correlation chains are the most operationally valuable to build as SIEM rules:

  • Deauth flood (AirSnitch) + new association from affected MAC (RADIUS logs) + anomalous DNS or lateral movement (Zeek/Suricata) = likely credential capture and reuse within the same session
  • Rogue AP detected (AirSnitch) + new DHCP lease from unknown MAC (DHCP server) + outbound connection to unexpected IP (firewall logs) = possible insider-placed device or visitor hardware
  • PMKID harvest alert (AirSnitch) + no subsequent failed authentication attempts (RADIUS) = attacker obtained PMKID for offline cracking; watch for authentication attempts over next 24-48 hours
  • Evil twin detected (AirSnitch) + DNS queries for corporate internal resources from wireless segment (DNS logs) = victim device associating with attacker AP and attempting internal resource access
ℹ
Building Correlation Rules in Your SIEM
Write correlation rules using a 5-minute sliding window. A deauth event in AirSnitch is only meaningful for correlation if the follow-on event (new association, lateral movement) occurs within a few minutes. Longer windows increase false positive rates significantly. Store AirSnitch alert data and RADIUS/DHCP/firewall data in the same SIEM index tier so correlation searches do not require cross-cluster queries.
Section 08

🏘 Building a Wireless SOC

AirSnitch provides wireless-specific detection at Layer 2. A mature wireless security operations capability combines AirSnitch with complementary tools that cover network traffic analysis, connection metadata, and behavioral analytics. The goal is a full-spectrum sensor grid where no significant wireless threat can occur without generating at least one detectable signal across the sensor stack.

📡
AirSnitch
Layer 2 wireless IDS. Detects management frame attacks, rogue APs, evil twins, SSID spoofing, deauth floods, and PMKID harvesting. Operates at the 802.11 protocol level, below IP. Essential for threats that are invisible to any wired network sensor.
RF / Layer 2
Zeek (Bro)
Network traffic analyzer producing structured protocol logs: DNS, HTTP, SSL/TLS, SMB, and connection metadata. Deployed on the wired segment bridging wireless traffic. Detects C2 callbacks, data exfiltration, and lateral movement that AirSnitch cannot see inside encrypted frames.
Network / Layer 3-7
RITA
Real Intelligence Threat Analytics — a threat hunting framework that consumes Zeek logs and identifies beaconing patterns consistent with C2 communication, long connections, and DNS anomalies. Particularly effective at detecting slow-and-low exfiltration from wireless devices.
Behavioral Analytics
Suricata / Snort
Signature-based IDS on the wired segment. Detects known attack patterns in Layer 3-7 traffic from wireless clients. Complements AirSnitch's Layer 2 detection with application-layer visibility. ET Open and ET Pro rulesets cover web exploitation, malware C2, and reconnaissance patterns.

SOC Response Runbooks

Runbook: Evil Twin / Rogue AP Alert
Investigation and response steps for unauthorized AP detection
▼
  • Step 1 — Verify the alert. Confirm the flagged BSSID is not in the authorized AP inventory. Check if the SSID matches an authorized network name exactly or is a spoofed variant (spacing, Unicode, capitalization).
  • Step 2 — Assess proximity. Review the RSSI value from the alert. RSSI above -60 dBm suggests the rogue AP is within approximately 15 meters of the detecting sensor.
  • Step 3 — Identify associated clients. Query RADIUS or DHCP logs for any devices that associated with the rogue BSSID. Any client that connected is potentially compromised.
  • Step 4 — Physical investigation. Dispatch facilities or security personnel to conduct a walk-through in the area of the detecting sensor. Look for rogue hardware on network drops, in ceiling spaces, or in public areas.
  • Step 5 — Isolate affected clients. If clients associated with the rogue AP, remove them from the network for forensic review. Check for credential reuse, malware installation, or data exfiltration in Zeek and RITA logs.
  • Step 6 — Document and close. Record the BSSID, physical location if found, affected clients, and remediation actions. Update the incident report and add the rogue BSSID to a block list if wired NAC is deployed.
🚫
Runbook: Deauthentication Flood Alert
Distinguishing DoS attacks from handshake capture attempts
▼
  • Step 1 — Check PMF status. If the target AP has PMF Required enabled, deauth frames from an external attacker will be rejected by clients. The flood is a failed attack. Document and monitor for escalation.
  • Step 2 — Assess impact. Query RADIUS logs for re-authentication events from clients on the targeted SSID. High re-authentication rates during the deauth window confirm client disruption is occurring.
  • Step 3 — Look for handshake capture follow-on. Check AirSnitch logs for PMKID or handshake-related alerts in the same time window. A deauth flood immediately followed by captured handshakes confirms a credential theft attempt.
  • Step 4 — Identify attacker source. The src_bssid in the alert is the attacker's adapter MAC. Check if this MAC appears in DHCP or RADIUS logs, suggesting the attacker may also be operating on the wired network.
  • Step 5 — Mitigate. If the AP supports WPA3 with PMF Required, enable it. If PMF cannot be enforced immediately, notify affected users to avoid reconnecting to the network until the situation is resolved.
Section 09

Key Takeaways

📡
Passive Monitoring Is Legal and Safe
AirSnitch never transmits. Passive RF observation with no frame injection avoids the legal and regulatory exposure that comes with active WIPS countermeasures in the United States.
Chipset Selection Is Critical
Monitor mode capability depends entirely on the wireless chipset and its Linux driver. Verify chipsets before procurement. The Alfa AWUS036ACM and AWUS036ACHM are reliable, tested choices.
Structured Alerts Enable Automation
JSON-structured alert output makes AirSnitch a first-class SIEM data source. Field-level extraction in Splunk or Elasticsearch enables correlation rules, dashboards, and automated response workflows.
Placement Determines Coverage
A sensor cannot detect what it cannot receive. Perimeter placement, multi-floor coverage, and high-value area focus maximize detection. Channel hopping blind spots require multi-sensor mitigation.
Correlation Multiplies Value
AirSnitch alerts combined with RADIUS, DHCP, Zeek, and RITA data create correlation chains that reveal attack sequences invisible to any single sensor. Context transforms raw alerts into actionable incidents.
Tune Before You Alert
Baseline your environment for 48 hours before enabling alerting. High-density environments require elevated thresholds. Alert storms erode analyst trust in a detection system faster than false negatives.
