01 / 04 -- Incident Response Lifecycle: The Six Phases

SY0-701 Objective 4.3 requires knowledge of the IR lifecycle phases as defined by NIST SP 800-61. The exam presents scenarios and asks which phase is being executed or which action belongs in a specific phase.

01Preparation
02Detection & Analysis
03Containment
04Eradication
05Recovery
06Lessons Learned
Phase What Happens Exam-Testable Actions
Preparation Building response capability before incidents occur. Policies, playbooks, tools, training, communication trees. Incident response plan (IRP), communication plan, CSIRT team formation, tabletop exercises, legal retainer, forensic toolkit maintenance.
Detection and Analysis Identifying that an incident has occurred (or is occurring), determining its scope and impact, and categorizing its severity. SIEM alert triage, log analysis, threat hunting, incident classification (P1 through P4), establishing incident timeline, identifying affected systems.
Containment Stopping the spread of the incident without destroying evidence. Short-term containment isolates immediately; long-term containment stabilizes for continued operations. Network isolation (VLAN segregation, firewall rule), system quarantine, disabling compromised accounts, blocking C2 IP ranges. Evidence is preserved before remediation begins.
Eradication Removing the threat from affected systems: malware removal, closing exploited vulnerabilities, patching, credential reset. Malware removal and re-imaging, applying patches that were exploited, rotating compromised credentials, reviewing for persistence mechanisms (scheduled tasks, registry entries, backdoors).
Recovery Restoring affected systems to production with confidence that the threat is eliminated and monitoring is enhanced. System restoration from verified backups, validation testing, enhanced monitoring (honeypots, increased SIEM sensitivity), gradual return to normal operations with close observation.
Lessons Learned Post-incident review to understand root cause, evaluate response effectiveness, and improve processes. After-action report (AAR), root cause analysis (RCA), timeline reconstruction, identifying detection and response gaps, updating playbooks, training updates, metrics (MTTD, MTTR).
Facility Context: The facility's containment breach protocol is a 47-page playbook updated quarterly. Every staff member with BSL-3 or higher clearance completes a tabletop exercise against the protocol twice per year. The Incident Response Lead maintains a pre-positioned evidence kit in each lab sector. The most recent after-action report identified three detection gaps that were closed before the quarter ended.

02 / 04 -- Digital Forensics: Evidence Preservation and Chain of Custody

SY0-701 Objective 4.4 tests forensic principles: how evidence is collected, preserved, and maintained to ensure it is admissible and reliable. Forensic errors during an incident can destroy evidence and eliminate legal options.

Order of volatility defines the sequence in which evidence should be collected, from most volatile (disappears when power is lost) to least volatile (survives indefinitely). Collecting from most to least volatile preserves the most data.

Priority Evidence Type Volatility Reason
1 (First) CPU registers and cache Lost immediately when process ends or power off.
2 RAM (memory contents) Lost on power off. Contains running processes, decrypted data, network connections, passwords in memory.
3 Network state (active connections, ARP cache) Changes continuously. ARP entries expire within minutes. Active connections terminate quickly.
4 Running processes and open files Process list changes as malware may have persistence that keeps it running but can be terminated.
5 Disk contents (storage) Survives power off but can be overwritten. Forensic image must be created before any analysis to preserve the original state.
6 (Last) Remote logs, archived data, physical media Most durable. Logs may be overwritten based on rotation policy, but typically survive for extended periods.

Chain of custody is the documented, unbroken record of who had possession of evidence, when they had it, and what was done with it. Every transfer of evidence must be logged. Breaks in chain of custody can render evidence inadmissible in legal proceedings. A forensic image (bit-for-bit copy verified by hash) is made before any analysis so the original is never touched.

Forensic Failure Pattern An incident responder finds malware on a server and immediately runs a removal tool to clean the system. The malware is gone -- but so is the evidence. The attacker's tools, persistence mechanisms, and C2 configuration are all destroyed. Containment must happen before eradication, and forensic imaging must happen before any remediation changes the system state. The order matters. Every time.
Facility Context: When a specimen containment anomaly is detected, the affected lab server is immediately network-isolated. Before any remediation: RAM is imaged using approved forensic tools, network connections are documented, running processes are captured, and a forensic disk image is taken with the hash recorded on the evidence form. The original system is sealed in a tamper-evident bag with a chain of custody tag. Analysis happens on the copy only.

03 / 04 -- Disaster Recovery: Hot, Warm, and Cold Sites

Disaster recovery (DR) is the set of policies, tools, and procedures to enable the continuation or quick resumption of vital technology infrastructure and systems following a disaster. SY0-701 Objective 4.4 tests recovery site types and their trade-offs.

Site Type Readiness RTO Cost Description
Hot site Fully operational, real-time Minutes to hours Highest Mirrors primary infrastructure with live data replication. Can assume operations almost immediately. Used for critical systems with very short RTO requirements.
Warm site Partially configured, near-current data Hours to days Moderate Hardware and connectivity in place. Software installed. Data restored from recent backup (not real-time replication). Requires some setup time before operations resume.
Cold site Empty facility, no equipment pre-staged Days to weeks Lowest Physical space, power, and connectivity only. Equipment must be procured and installed. Acceptable only for non-critical systems with generous RTO requirements.

Business continuity planning (BCP) is broader than DR. DR focuses on IT systems. BCP addresses continuity of the entire business operation including people, processes, communications, and facilities. DR is a component of BCP.

Tabletop exercises are structured, discussion-based exercises where team members walk through a simulated scenario to test the IR plan without deploying actual systems. They identify gaps in playbooks and communication without the cost or disruption of a full-scale drill.

Facility Context: The facility's primary data systems replicate to a hot site at the secondary containment campus 40 kilometers away. The replication lag is under 60 seconds, satisfying the 15-minute RPO for the specimen catalog. The secondary campus can assume full operations within 90 minutes, meeting the 2-hour RTO. Cold site arrangements exist for administrative systems where a week-long RTO is acceptable.

04 / 04 -- IR Exam Scenarios and Common Question Patterns

SY0-701 presents IR scenarios and asks which phase is occurring or which action is appropriate. These are the patterns that appear most frequently.

Scenario Correct Phase / Answer Reason
Security team creates incident response playbooks and conducts tabletop exercises Preparation All activities before an incident occurs are preparation phase.
SIEM alerts on unusual outbound traffic. Team reviews logs and determines a host is communicating with a known C2 server. Detection and Analysis Identifying and scoping an incident in progress.
A compromised host is disconnected from the network while the IR team begins forensic imaging. Containment Limiting spread while preserving evidence. Forensic image must be taken before eradication begins.
The malware is removed from all affected systems and the exploited vulnerability is patched. Eradication Removing the threat root cause after containment is complete.
Systems are restored from verified backup and placed under enhanced monitoring for two weeks. Recovery Returning to production with elevated observation to confirm the threat is fully resolved.
Team meets to review the incident timeline and documents that initial detection was delayed because SIEM rules were not tuned for this attack pattern. Lessons Learned Post-incident review identifying gaps. Result is updated playbooks and SIEM tuning.
Investigator needs to collect evidence from a running server. What should be collected first? RAM (memory) Order of volatility: RAM is collected before disk because it is lost on power off and contains live execution data.
Facility Context: Every containment breach at the facility follows the same documented protocol regardless of severity. The protocol exists for Level 1 anomalies (minor sensor deviation) and Level 5 outbreaks (full specimen escape) -- the scale differs, the process does not. Consistency across drills is what makes response automatic under pressure. The protocol is the training. The training is the response.
Key Takeaways
  • IR phases (NIST SP 800-61): Preparation, Detection and Analysis, Containment, Eradication, Recovery, Lessons Learned -- in that order.
  • Containment stops the spread. Eradication removes the threat. Evidence must be collected and preserved before eradication changes the system.
  • Order of volatility: CPU registers, RAM, network state, running processes, disk, remote logs. Collect from most to least volatile.
  • Chain of custody is the unbroken documented record of who possessed evidence and when. A break in chain of custody can make evidence inadmissible.
  • Hot site = real-time replication, minutes to hours RTO. Warm site = near-current data, hours to days RTO. Cold site = empty space, days to weeks RTO.
  • BCP is broader than DR -- BCP covers people, process, and facility. DR is an IT-focused component of BCP.
