What is Information Security

Information security is the practice of protecting information and information systems from unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction. The goal is not to make systems impenetrable -- that is impossible. The goal is to reduce risk to an acceptable level while enabling the organization to function.

Security protects three categories of assets: data (the information itself), systems (the infrastructure that stores and processes it), and people (who create, use, and depend on it). Every decision a security professional makes involves trade-offs between these three and the resources available to protect them.

Security+ SY0-701 Domain 1.0 defines information security in terms of controls, concepts, and cryptographic solutions. This refresher establishes the vocabulary you need before the domain modules begin.

The CIA Triad

The CIA triad is the foundational model of information security. Every security control, policy, and technology exists to preserve one or more of these three properties. Understanding the triad lets you evaluate any security decision by asking: which property does this protect, and what is the cost if it fails?

C Confidentiality Information is accessible only to those authorized to have it. Encryption, access controls, and need-to-know policies serve confidentiality.
I Integrity Information is accurate and has not been altered without authorization. Hashing, digital signatures, and audit logs serve integrity.
A Availability Systems and data are accessible to authorized users when needed. Redundancy, backups, and DDoS mitigation serve availability.

Real breaches violate specific triad properties. The 2017 Equifax breach (147 million records exposed) was a confidentiality failure. The 2016 Bangladesh Bank SWIFT attack ($81 million stolen via fraudulent transfers) was an integrity failure. A ransomware attack that encrypts all company data until payment is received is an availability failure.

Core Security Terminology

Security professionals use precise language to describe threats and defenses. Imprecise language causes miscommunication during incidents. These are the foundational terms you will use throughout this course and on the Security+ exam.

TermDefinition
AssetAnything of value to the organization -- data, hardware, software, people, or reputation.
ThreatAny potential event or action that could cause harm to an asset. A threat is not yet realized.
VulnerabilityA weakness in a system, process, or control that a threat can exploit.
RiskThe probability that a threat will exploit a vulnerability, combined with the impact if it does. Risk = Likelihood x Impact.
ExploitThe actual method or code used to take advantage of a vulnerability.
ControlA safeguard or countermeasure designed to reduce risk. Controls can be technical, administrative, or physical.
IncidentA security event that has been confirmed to have negative impact -- a breach, data loss, or unauthorized access.
Non-repudiationThe assurance that someone cannot deny having performed an action. Digital signatures provide non-repudiation.

The AAA Framework

AAA -- Authentication, Authorization, and Accounting -- is the framework that governs access to systems and resources. It answers three questions for every access attempt: Who are you? What are you allowed to do? What did you actually do?

ComponentQuestion It AnswersExample
AuthenticationWho are you? Prove it.Username + password + MFA token
AuthorizationWhat are you allowed to access?Role-based access control -- BSL-1 operators cannot access BSL-4 labs
AccountingWhat did you do, when did you do it?Audit logs, SIEM event records, session recordings

AAA systems in practice include RADIUS (Remote Authentication Dial-In User Service), TACACS+ (Terminal Access Controller Access-Control System Plus), and LDAP/Active Directory. Accounting without authentication is meaningless. Authorization without accounting creates deniability.

Every door in this facility uses AAA. You authenticate at the perimeter, your clearance level authorizes what labs you enter, and every access is logged. There is no anonymous entry here.

Why Security Fails in Practice

Security failures rarely happen because a technology is fundamentally broken. They happen because of predictable, documented failures in how security is implemented and maintained.

Complexity as the enemy of security. The more complex a system, the larger the attack surface. The 2020 SolarWinds supply chain attack compromised approximately 18,000 organizations through a single trusted software update mechanism.

The people problem. IBM's 2023 Cost of a Data Breach report found that phishing and stolen credentials accounted for 36% of all breach initial access vectors. Technology alone cannot solve a problem that begins with human behavior.

Patch lag. The Equifax 2017 breach exploited CVE-2017-5638, a vulnerability in Apache Struts for which a patch had been available for 66 days before the breach. The vulnerability was known. The fix was available. The organization did not apply it in time.

Operational Context:

Containment fails the same way every time: a known vulnerability, an unpatched system, a trusted insider, or a process that was never questioned. The protocol exists because someone did not have it.
