Why Security Exists
Every control traces back to one cause: information has value, and adversaries want it.
Data Has Intrinsic Value
Patient records, credentials, IP, and access rights all trade on secondary markets. The value drives the attack.
Systems Are Always Targeted
Automated scanning never stops. Every network-exposed system is being probed right now. The question is whether your defenses hold.
Perfect Security Is Impossible
Security is risk management, not risk elimination. The job is balancing protection, friction, and cost — under audit.
Slide 4 of 25  |  Topic 1: CIA Triad
The CIA Triad
Three pillars. Every security control maps to at least one of them. Every attack violates at least one.
Anchor question for every decision: Which pillar(s) does this control protect — or which does this attack violate?
Slide 5 of 25
Confidentiality
Information is only accessible to those authorized to access it.
When it works
Quiet defense
Encryption protects data at rest and in transit. Access control lists restrict who can read what. Need-to-know minimizes the blast radius of any single account compromise.
When it fails
Disclosure
Data breaches. Stolen credentials. Eavesdropping on unencrypted traffic. Insider exfiltration. Each is a confidentiality violation regardless of whether the data was modified.
Real-world anchor
2017 Equifax breach. 147 million credit records exposed. No data was altered. No service was knocked offline. The integrity and availability pillars stood. Confidentiality alone failed — and that was enough to be one of the largest breaches in history.
Slide 6 of 25
Integrity
Information has not been modified without authorization — and you can prove it.
When it works
Verifiable trust
Cryptographic hashes prove a file hasn't changed. Digital signatures prove who created it. Database transactions enforce ACID consistency. Configuration management baselines detect drift.
When it fails
Tampering
Modified financial records. Forged log entries to hide an attack. Ransomware that corrupts files. Man-in-the-middle that rewrites traffic. Integrity violations are often more dangerous than confidentiality breaches because they poison decisions.
Real-world anchor
2020 SolarWinds supply chain attack. Attackers tampered with the legitimate Orion software update, embedding a backdoor. Customers downloading signed updates trusted the signature — but the build pipeline itself was compromised. Integrity failed at a layer most defenders had never thought to check.
Slide 7 of 25
Availability
Systems and data are reachable when needed by authorized users.
When it works
Resilience
Redundant systems. Load balancing. Disaster recovery sites. Backups tested by restore drills. Capacity planned for surge. SLAs measured and reported.
When it fails
Outage
DDoS floods. Ransomware encrypts critical files. Hardware fails. Misconfigured DNS. A natural disaster takes the data center offline. The data is intact and confidential — but it might as well not exist if nobody can reach it.
Real-world anchor
2021 Colonial Pipeline ransomware. The pipeline operator preemptively shut down operations to prevent ransomware from spreading to operational systems. East Coast fuel supply was disrupted for days. No data was technically lost; availability simply collapsed — and a healthcare-style life-safety availability problem emerged in critical infrastructure.
Slide 8 of 25
Vulnerability · Threat · Risk
Three words. Most people use them interchangeably. They are not the same.
VULNERABILITY
A weakness in the system. An unpatched OS. A missing input validator. A default password. Vulnerabilities exist whether or not anyone exploits them.
THREAT
Anything with the potential to exploit a vulnerability. A hostile actor. A natural disaster. A bug under active exploitation in the wild.
RISK
The probability that a threat exploits a vulnerability multiplied by the impact. Risk is what we measure. Risk is what we manage.
RISK = THREAT × VULNERABILITY × IMPACT  —  remove any one factor, and risk goes to zero. That's the whole security playbook.
Slide 9 of 25
The Other Triad: AAA
Authentication, Authorization, Accounting. CIA describes what we protect. AAA describes how we control access.
Authentication
Who are you? Verifying identity. Something you know (password), have (token), or are (biometric). Multi-factor combines two or more.
Authorization
What can you do? Determining permissions for an already-authenticated identity. RBAC, ABAC, MAC, DAC are the major models.
Accounting
What did you do? Logging actions for audit and forensics. Without accounting, breach detection and post-incident review are impossible.
Authentication answers who. Authorization answers what. Accounting answers when and how. Miss any one and you have either an open door, a confused permission system, or no way to know what happened.
Slide 10 of 25  |  Topic 2: Threat Actors
The Threat Actor Taxonomy
Five distinct profiles. Each has different motivations, resources, and patience.
Script Kiddie
Curiosity, status. Borrowed tools. Low skill. High noise.
Hacktivist
Ideology. Politics. Defacement and disclosure for cause.
Cybercriminal
Money. Organized. Ransomware, fraud, identity theft.
Insider
Access already granted. Disgruntled, negligent, or coerced.
Nation-State
Espionage, sabotage. Unlimited resources. Years of patience.
The defender's burden
Each profile demands a different defense posture. A control set tuned for script kiddies will not stop a nation-state. A control set tuned for nation-states is unaffordable for most organizations. Know who you are most likely to face.
Slide 11 of 25
Advanced Persistent Threat — APT
The most dangerous adversary class. Three words. Each matters.
Advanced
Custom tooling. Zero-day exploits. Multi-stage payloads. Operational tradecraft refined over years.
Persistent
Stays hidden for months or years. Establishes redundant footholds. Survives detection of any single tool or account.
Threat
Deliberate intent. Specific targets. Strategic objectives that align with state-level interests.
Recognition pattern
2010 Stuxnet. Nation-state malware that crossed an air gap, targeted specific Siemens PLCs running Iranian centrifuges, and physically destroyed the equipment by manipulating rotation speeds while feeding the operators normal-looking telemetry. Designed by a state. Operated for years before detection. That's APT.
Slide 12 of 25
The Attack Surface
Every point where an adversary could enter, extract, or disrupt. Reducing it is the defender's permanent project.
Attack-surface reduction in three rules
(1) Disable what you do not use.  (2) Restrict what you do use to the smallest scope necessary.  (3) Monitor everything that remains.
Slide 13 of 25  |  Topic 3: Malware
The Malware Family Tree
Six categories cover almost every malicious program you will encounter on the exam — or in the wild.
The differentiator that always trips students
Virus needs a host file and user execution. Worm replicates itself across the network without a host. Both replicate; only the worm needs no help to spread. Remember this and you've solved half the malware questions on SY0-701.
Slide 14 of 25
Ransomware — The Modern Threat
Why this single malware family dominates incident response budgets in 2024-2026.
Single Extortion
Encrypt files. Demand ransom for the key. Defeated by backups. Old playbook.
Double Extortion
Exfiltrate first, encrypt second. Now even with backups, the threat of public release forces payment. Standard since 2019.
Triple Extortion
Add DDoS or direct customer threats. Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) operators run this as a business with affiliates and 24/7 support.
The defender's hardest truth
Healthcare, education, and local government dominate ransomware victim lists because their availability requirements are highest and their security budgets are lowest. Attackers know exactly where to push. Backups alone no longer protect you — once exfiltration is part of the playbook, you need data-at-rest encryption, network segmentation, EDR, and tested IR plans.
Slide 15 of 25
Modern Evasion Techniques
Old AV signatures cannot keep up. Here is how today's malware hides.
Fileless
Lives entirely in memory. Uses legitimate OS tools (PowerShell, WMI). Writes nothing to disk for signature scanners to find.
Polymorphic
Changes its code signature with each infection while keeping the same payload behavior. Static signatures useless.
Metamorphic
Completely rewrites itself per infection — not just a different stub. Even structural fingerprints change.
Supply Chain
Compromises a trusted vendor or build pipeline. Reaches every customer through a legitimately-signed update.
Why behavior-based detection matters
Signature-based AV asks: "Is this binary on my blocklist?" Behavior-based EDR asks: "Is PowerShell spawning a child process that just enumerated all domain users?" The first misses everything modern; the second sees through every technique on this slide.
Slide 16 of 25  |  Topic 4: Social Engineering
The Human Vector
You cannot patch a person. Social engineering bypasses every technical control you ever build.
The Mitnick principle
"It's much easier to fool somebody into giving you their password than it is to spend the effort to crack into the system." Kevin Mitnick said this in 1995. Every breach report in 2026 still proves it.
Slide 17 of 25
The Phishing Family
Same technique. Different precision. Different targets.
Phishing
Mass email. Broad net. Generic lures. Low effort, high volume.
Spear Phishing
Targeted. Personal details. References real coworkers or projects.
Whaling
Executive targets. CEO fraud. Wire transfer impersonation.
Vishing
Voice phone calls. Pretexts IT support or law enforcement.
Smishing
SMS text. Package delivery, bank alert, MFA prompt scams.
Detection across the family
All five exploit the same psychology: urgency, authority, fear, helpfulness. The technical defense (DMARC, link-rewriting, SMS gateway filtering) catches a fraction. The real defense is user training plus a culture where verification is normal and not embarrassing.
Slide 18 of 25
Beyond Phishing
The non-email social engineering techniques that appear constantly on exams and in real incidents.
Pretexting
Fabricates a scenario to extract information or access. "I'm calling from IT, we're seeing alerts on your account, can you confirm your password?"
Baiting
Leaves something tempting. USB drives in the parking lot labeled "Q4 Salaries". Plugged in out of curiosity, executing malware on insertion.
Tailgating
Walks in behind an authorized employee. Defeats badge readers when nobody questions the friendly face holding a coffee.
Quid Pro Quo
Offers something in exchange. "I can give your team free IT support — just install this remote access tool on your machine."
Watering Hole
Compromises a website the target group frequents. Targets land themselves. Used against specific industries or communities.
Shoulder Surfing
Direct observation. Reading the screen at a coffee shop. PIN entry at an ATM. Low-tech, still effective.
Slide 19 of 25  |  Topic 5: Security Controls
Security Controls — The 3×3 Matrix
Every security control fits in one box. Memorize this grid and most exam questions decompose instantly.
PREVENTIVE
Stop before
DETECTIVE
See during
CORRECTIVE
Recover after
TECHNICAL
Firewall
Blocks unauthorized traffic.
IDS / SIEM
Detects intrusion in progress.
Backup Restore
Recovers data after ransomware.
ADMINISTRATIVE
Security Policy
Defines acceptable use.
Audits
Reveals policy violations.
Disciplinary Action
Corrects post-violation behavior.
PHYSICAL
Locks & Mantraps
Prevents unauthorized entry.
CCTV / Sensors
Detects intrusion attempts.
Fire Suppression
Recovers after physical event.
Two extras worth knowing: Deterrent controls discourage attempts (visible cameras, warning signs). Compensating controls substitute when the primary cannot be implemented.

Defense in Depth
No single control is sufficient. Layer them. When one fails, the next is already in place.
Data
Application
Host
Network
Perimeter
5Perimeter — firewalls, WAFs, IDS at the edge. First contact with the internet.
4Network — segmentation, VLANs, internal east-west firewalls.
3Host — OS hardening, EDR, host firewall, patching.
2Application — input validation, authentication, authorization checks.
1Data — encryption at rest, classification, DLP, key management.
Plus: physical layer below, and human/policy layer above. Seven layers in the full model.
Slide 21 of 25
Two Principles That Limit Blast Radius
If you take only two ideas from controls, take these.
Principle 1
Least Privilege
Every user account, service, and process gets the minimum permissions needed to perform its function. Nothing more. When an account is compromised, the attacker inherits only that minimum scope — not your whole environment.
Principle 2
Separation of Duties
Critical actions require two or more people. The person who approves a wire transfer is not the person who initiates it. The developer who writes code is not the person who deploys it to production. Fraud requires collusion, not a single bad decision.
Why these are the bedrock
Both are administrative controls — they're rules, not products. They cost nothing to implement and they cap the damage from every other failure. Most breaches that escalate to "catastrophic" did so because least privilege wasn't enforced and one compromised account had keys to everything.
Slide 22 of 25
The Risk Management Cycle
Security is not a project that finishes. It's a loop. This is the loop.
Avoid
Don't do the activity at all.
Mitigate
Add controls to reduce probability or impact.
Transfer
Cyber insurance. Outsource the function.
Accept
Document and monitor; risk is below threshold.
Slide 23 of 25  |  Synthesis
Week 1: Nine Things to Remember
If you walk out of this lecture with only these nine sentences, you can answer 80% of Week 1 questions.
1CIA Triad — Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability. Every control protects at least one. Every attack violates at least one.
2Vulnerability is a weakness. Threat is a potential exploiter. Risk is probability × impact. Three different words for three different ideas.
3AAA — Authentication (who), Authorization (what), Accounting (when and how).
4Threat actors range from script kiddies (low skill, high noise) to nation-state APTs (years of patience, unlimited resources).
5Virus needs a host file. Worm self-propagates. Ransomware double-extorts: exfiltrate + encrypt.
6Modern malware uses fileless, polymorphic, metamorphic, and supply-chain techniques to evade signature-based AV. Behavior-based detection (EDR) is the answer.
7Social engineering bypasses every technical control. Phishing family: phish, spear, whale, vish, smish. Plus pretexting, baiting, tailgating, quid pro quo, watering hole, shoulder surfing.
8Controls 3×3: Technical / Administrative / Physical × Preventive / Detective / Corrective. Plus Deterrent and Compensating.
9Defense in Depth + Least Privilege + Separation of Duties. Three principles. Free to implement. Cap the damage from every other failure.
