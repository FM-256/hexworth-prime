Slide 2 of 12  |  Scenario
Veridian Financial Group
Read the full brief before starting any task. Decisions you make on Task 1 affect Tasks 2 and 3.
Organization profile
Veridian Financial Group is a mid-size investment advisory firm with 340 employees, two offices (Tampa and Atlanta), and a hybrid infrastructure: on-premises Active Directory (Windows Server 2022, domain veridian.local), Microsoft Azure AD for cloud SSO, and a Salesforce CRM accessible via SAML 2.0 federation. Employees authenticate via Azure AD with TOTP-based MFA (Microsoft Authenticator push notifications). The firm is subject to SEC Regulation S-P (safeguarding client financial data) and SOC 2 Type II requirements.
What the SOC Detected
At 02:14 UTC, the SIEM fired a high-severity alert: a service account (svc-reporting) authenticated successfully to Azure AD from IP 45.33.32.156 (a DigitalOcean VPS in the Netherlands, not a known office or VPN egress). Within 6 minutes, the account queried the Salesforce API and exported 4,200 client contact and account records. The export completed at 02:20 UTC. Azure AD logs show the service account's last legitimate activity was 11 days ago from Tampa office IP.
What Was in Place
Azure AD Conditional Access policies: MFA required for all user accounts. Service accounts are excluded from Conditional Access (a documented exception approved 14 months ago to avoid breaking automated reporting jobs). The svc-reporting account has a long-lived client secret stored in the firm's password manager. The account has: Salesforce API access (read + export), SharePoint read access to the Finance team site, and Exchange read access to a shared mailbox. No PAM platform is deployed. Service account credentials are rotated annually.
Current State at Incident Activation
CSIRT has been activated. The SIEM alert is confirmed. The Salesforce export has already completed — data exfiltration is a past event, not ongoing. The svc-reporting account is still active in Azure AD. Three other systems are potentially in scope: the password manager (how the credential was likely obtained), the SharePoint Finance site (was it accessed?), and the Exchange shared mailbox (same question). Logs for all three are available for your review.

Three Tasks, 35 Minutes
Do them in order. Task 1 determines what the attacker could use. Task 2 determines what they actually reached. Task 3 is the response.
01
Authentication Forensics (10 min)
Analyze the authentication failure that allowed this breach. Which MFA mechanism was in use? Which attack technique defeats it? What architecture change would have prevented or significantly complicated this access?
Relevant concepts: TOTP vs. FIDO2, AiTM proxy attacks, service account Conditional Access exclusions, NIST SP 800-63B-4 assurance levels.
02
Identity Blast Radius Audit (10 min)
Using the permissions table on the next slide, map every resource the svc-reporting account can reach. Identify RBAC violations (over-permissioned roles). List the emergency deprovisioning actions in priority order.
Relevant concepts: least privilege, RBAC over-permissioning, service account hygiene, PAM just-in-time access, account deprovisioning order.
03
Containment Decision + Risk Register Entry (10 min)
The exfiltration has already occurred. Make a containment decision for each of the three in-scope systems. Then create a risk register entry for this incident: threat, vulnerability, likelihood, impact, treatment, residual risk, owner, review date.
Relevant concepts: NIST SP 800-61r3 containment phases, isolation vs. shutdown decision, risk register fields, risk treatment options (mitigate/transfer/avoid/accept).
04
Group Presentation (5 min)
One spokesperson presents the authentication failure analysis, one RBAC violation finding, and the containment decision for the highest-priority system. Instructor will ask one clarifying question per group. Other groups may challenge.
Be ready to defend: why the MFA mechanism failed (mechanistically, not just that "it was weak"), and why your containment priority order is what it is.

Task 1: Authentication Forensics
The attacker authenticated as svc-reporting from a foreign IP with no MFA challenge. Understand why before recommending a fix. A recommendation without a mechanism is not a security decision.

Question A: Why No MFA?
The account authenticated via OAuth 2.0 client_credentials flow. This flow authenticates the application (client_id + client_secret), not a human user. Conditional Access MFA policies apply to interactive user sign-ins. Client credentials flow is a non-interactive machine-to-machine flow that bypasses interactive sign-in policies. The Conditional Access exemption is therefore redundant here — this flow would bypass the MFA policy regardless. Confirm this understanding in your written answer.
Question B: How Was the Secret Obtained?
The svc-reporting client secret was stored in the firm's shared password manager. Identify two plausible vectors by which an attacker could have exfiltrated the secret, given that: (1) the password manager is cloud-hosted, (2) multiple employees have access to the shared vault containing this secret, (3) the secret was last rotated 10 months ago. You do not have password manager logs — reason from the attack surface.
Question C: The Architectural Fix
Recommend the architectural change that eliminates the credential-theft attack surface for service account machine-to-machine authentication. Your recommendation must: (1) name the specific Azure mechanism, (2) explain why it removes the need for a stored client secret, (3) state the NIST SP 800-63B-4 assurance level equivalent (AAL1/AAL2/AAL3) for your recommendation. Do not recommend "rotate secrets more frequently" — that is a procedural mitigation, not an architectural fix.
Slide 5 of 12  |  Task 1 Reference
Task 1 Reference: Authentication Mechanism Comparison
Use this to verify your understanding of the attack surface and to anchor your architectural recommendation. Not rules to copy — constraints to reason against.
Why Client Secrets Are Structurally Weak
An OAuth 2.0 client_secret is a symmetric shared secret: anyone who holds it can authenticate as the application. Unlike a password, there is no second factor requirement in the client_credentials flow. The secret must be stored somewhere accessible to the automation (password manager, environment variable, secrets manager). Every location where the secret is readable is an attack surface. Rotation reduces the exposure window; it does not eliminate the exposure surface.
Workload Identity Federation (the architectural fix)
Azure Workload Identity Federation allows an external identity (an Azure-managed identity, a GitHub Actions workflow, a Kubernetes service account) to authenticate to Azure AD using a short-lived federated token rather than a stored secret. No client secret is issued or stored. There is nothing to exfiltrate. The token is bound to the specific workload and is valid for minutes. Source: Microsoft Entra Workload Identity Federation documentation.
Conditional Access and Non-Interactive Flows
Azure AD Conditional Access policies that require MFA apply to interactive user authentication flows (Authorization Code, Device Code). The client_credentials flow is non-interactive — there is no user browser session, no redirect, no challenge prompt. Conditional Access MFA enforcement does not apply to this flow by design. A Conditional Access exemption for a service account is therefore not the cause of this breach — it is a red herring. The root cause is client secret issuance for a machine-to-machine flow.
Common mistake to avoid
Recommending "enable MFA for service accounts" is architecturally incorrect. Service accounts use non-interactive flows because there is no human to approve a push notification. The correct recommendation is to eliminate the shared secret entirely by using certificate-based authentication (X.509 client certificate) or workload identity federation, both of which use asymmetric cryptography where the private key never leaves the service's controlled environment.

Task 2 Reference: Identity Audit Anchor Points
Use these to calibrate your specificity and validate your RBAC violation findings. Not answers to copy — the reasoning model to apply.
Least Privilege for Service Accounts
A service account that generates weekly Salesforce reports needs: Salesforce API read access scoped to the specific objects the report queries (Contact, Opportunity — not all objects). It does not need SharePoint, Exchange, or Azure AD Directory read. Those permissions were added incrementally without a least-privilege review and represent classic privilege creep. The principle: each service account's permission set should be derivable from its documented purpose, with no permissions that cannot be traced to a specific function.
Exchange Send As: The Business Email Compromise Risk
Send As permission on a shared mailbox allows the account to send email that appears to come from ops@veridian.com. An attacker with this permission can: impersonate the firm's operations team to clients, send fraudulent wire transfer instructions, or reset external service credentials by controlling the reply-to address. This is a Business Email Compromise (BEC) attack surface. A service account that generates reports has no legitimate need for Send As on any mailbox.
Deprovisioning Priority Logic
Revocation priority follows blast radius x active risk: (1) Revoke Exchange Send As immediately — BEC attacks can cause irreversible financial or reputational damage with a single sent email. (2) Revoke Salesforce bulk export — ongoing exfiltration risk even though the first export is complete. (3) Revoke SharePoint and Azure AD Directory — reconnaissance risk. (4) Rotate the client secret — invalidates any copies the attacker holds. (5) Audit the password manager vault for other entries accessible to the same attacker session.
The second-order risk from Password Manager Read access
The attacker authenticated using a secret they obtained from the password manager. If svc-reporting had read access to a vault that contained other service account secrets (a common pattern when a service manages its own rotation), the attacker may have exfiltrated all secrets in that vault, not just the one used for initial access. The password manager audit log must be reviewed for API calls from the attacker's source IP and for any vault exports that occurred in the same window as the Salesforce breach.

Task 3: Containment Decision + Risk Register
The data exfiltration is complete. Containment now means: stop further access, preserve evidence, and document the risk posture accurately. Every decision has a trade-off. Document the trade-off, not just the decision.
A
Containment Decision for Each In-Scope System
Three systems require a containment decision. For each one, state the action and the reason: 1. Azure AD / svc-reporting account: disable or leave active for monitoring? 2. Salesforce CRM: revoke API access only, or isolate the entire org from external API calls? 3. Password Manager vault: lock the vault, rotate all secrets it contains, or both?
There is no universally correct answer. State your decision, your reasoning, and the residual risk you accept with each choice. A decision to leave the account active for intelligence gathering requires documented authorization.
B
Risk Register Entry for This Incident
Create a new risk register entry using these fields: Threat: the agent and action. Vulnerability: the exploited weakness. Likelihood (post-incident): how likely is recurrence before mitigation? Impact: business consequence (financial, regulatory, reputational). Treatment: which option (mitigate/transfer/avoid/accept) and the specific control. Residual Risk: what risk remains after treatment is applied. Owner: named role. Review Date: when will residual risk be reassessed?
Use qualitative ratings (Low/Medium/High/Critical) for likelihood and impact. The treatment must reference a specific control, not a generic action like "improve security."
C
Regulatory Notification Assessment
Veridian is subject to SEC Regulation S-P. The Salesforce export included 4,200 client contact and account records. Determine: (1) Does this breach trigger an SEC Reg S-P notification obligation? (2) What is the notification window under Reg S-P 2023 amendments? (3) Who at Veridian must be notified internally before any external notification is sent?
SEC Reg S-P was amended in 2023. The amended rule requires covered institutions to notify affected customers within 30 days of detecting a breach of customer information. Internal notification chain typically runs: CISO → General Counsel → CEO → Board (Audit Committee).

Task 3 Reference: Containment + Risk Register Anchor Points
Do not copy these examples directly. Use them to calibrate the precision of your decisions and identify gaps in your own analysis.
Example: svc-reporting Containment Decision
Recommended action: disable the account immediately in Azure AD, revoke all active OAuth tokens.
Reasoning: Exfiltration is complete. There is no ongoing attacker action to monitor. The intelligence value of leaving the account active (understanding the attacker's next move) is outweighed by the risk of a second export window or lateral movement using the Azure AD Directory Read access.
Residual risk from disabling: attacker is aware of detection and may pivot to other credentials obtained from the password manager.
Example: Risk Register Entry
Threat: External threat actor using stolen OAuth client secret.
Vulnerability: Service account using long-lived client secret with no MFA, Conditional Access exclusion, and over-permissioned RBAC.
Likelihood (post-incident, pre-remediation): High — secret may still be held by attacker if not rotated.
Impact: High — client PII exported; SEC Reg S-P notification obligation triggered; reputational risk with investment clients.
Treatment: Mitigate — migrate to Workload Identity Federation (eliminates client secret); implement PAM for remaining service accounts; quarterly RBAC review.
Residual Risk: Medium (post-migration) — federation token misconfiguration is possible.
Owner: CISO.
Review Date: 90 days post-remediation.
SEC Reg S-P 2024 Amendments: What You Need to Know
The SEC adopted final amendments to Regulation S-P on May 16, 2024 (Release No. 34-100155). Compliance deadlines: June 3, 2026 (larger entities) and December 3, 2026 (smaller entities). Key obligations: (1) Customer notification within 30 days of becoming aware of unauthorized access to or use of customer information, unless determined that customer information has not been used or is not reasonably likely to be used in a manner that would result in substantial harm or inconvenience. (2) Covered information: non-public personal information about customers of broker-dealers, investment advisers, investment companies, and transfer agents. (3) The notice must describe the incident in general terms, the type of information involved, and the steps customers can take to protect themselves.
The containment decision the exam tests on NIST SP 800-61r3
When exfiltration is already complete, containment shifts from "stop the data leaving" to "stop further access and preserve evidence of what left." The correct NIST SP 800-61r3 framing: (1) short-term containment = disable the account and revoke tokens (stop active access), (2) long-term containment = remediate the vulnerability (migrate to workload identity, fix RBAC), (3) do not skip directly to recovery (reimaging, rotating secrets) without preserving evidence of the attack path for the PIR and potential regulatory investigation.

Slide 11 of 12  |  Discussion Prompts
Defend Your Decisions
Instructor will ask one of these per group. Other groups may challenge. These are the real trade-off questions a security practitioner faces during and after an incident.
The Intelligence Trade-off
Your team recommends disabling the svc-reporting account immediately. One team member argues for leaving it active for 24 hours to gather intelligence on the attacker's next move. What authorizations are required before you can legally and ethically pursue that option? What is the specific risk of leaving it active that must be weighed against the intelligence value?
Conditional Access Exclusion Accountability
The service account was excluded from Conditional Access 14 months ago. That exclusion was "documented and approved." Who approved it? What should the approval process have required? Would a proper risk acceptance form have changed the outcome? Describe what a well-designed exception process would have included.
The Architectural vs. Procedural Distinction
Your risk register entry lists "migrate to Workload Identity Federation" as the treatment. The CISO asks: "What do we do in the next 90 days before migration is complete?" Describe a procedural compensating control that reduces likelihood during the remediation window, and explain why it is only a temporary measure and not a permanent fix for the underlying architectural flaw.
SEC Reg S-P Notification Scope
The exported data contained client names, phone numbers, emails, and account IDs. Does this constitute "non-public personal information" under Reg S-P? The clients have not yet been notified. The CISO wants to delay notification until the forensic investigation is complete to avoid panicking clients. Under the amended Reg S-P 2023 rule, what is the legal position of delaying notification beyond 30 days?
RBAC Accountability After the Incident
The SharePoint Finance site access and the Exchange Send As permission had no justification on file. Who is responsible for that gap: the system owner who provisioned it, the security team that should have reviewed it, or the access review process that should have caught it? What structural change ensures permissions without justification cannot persist past the next review cycle?
Slide 12 of 12  |  Debrief
Task Debrief
Every control failure in this incident was a known class of failure. None of them required novel attacker capability. The lessons are architectural, not tactical.
Authentication: What Most Groups Got Right
The client_credentials flow bypasses MFA by design — this is not a configuration error, it is how the protocol works. Recognizing that the Conditional Access exclusion is irrelevant to the attack path is the key insight. The fix (Workload Identity Federation or X.509 client certificate) eliminates the shared secret entirely. "Rotate more often" treats the symptom; eliminating the secret treats the cause.
Identity: The RBAC Insight
Permissions accumulate over time without a forcing function to remove them. The Exchange Send As access had no justification and was never caught by an access review because Veridian did not have a regular access review process. Least privilege is not a one-time provisioning decision — it is an ongoing operational process. Quarterly access reviews with mandatory justification for every permission are the control that prevents this class of finding.
Response: The Risk Register Is Not Optional
A risk register entry created during incident response with an incomplete treatment or a generic remediation action is a compliance artifact, not a risk management document. The treatment must name a specific control, an owner, and a review date. Residual risk must be quantified, even qualitatively. When the next audit comes, the question is not "did you document it" but "did you act on it."
What carries into the final
The Final Part 1 (written) and Patient Zero practical (Part 2) draw on all eight weeks simultaneously. Week 4 contributes authentication mechanism analysis, RBAC violation identification, and IR lifecycle execution. The Patient Zero scenario includes an authentication failure in Phase 1, identity-based lateral movement in Phase 3, and a risk register requirement in the synthesis phase. The reasoning you practiced today is the reasoning the final tests under pressure.
