Slide 2 of 12  |  Scenario
MedCore Regional Medical Center
Read the full brief before starting any task. Decisions you make on Task 1 will affect Tasks 2 and 3.
Organization profile
MedCore Regional Medical Center operates a 450-bed hospital, 12 outpatient clinics, and approximately 2,200 staff. The hospital is subject to HIPAA requirements. Clinical staff access the EHR from workstations on the hospital LAN and from remote locations via personal and hospital-issued laptops.
New System: EHR Deployment
Tier 1 (Web): Patient portal — accessible from the internet. Patients log in to view records and schedule appointments.
Tier 2 (App): EHR application server — contains business logic, HL7 FHIR API. No direct internet access.
Tier 3 (DB): Database cluster — contains protected health information (PHI). Strictest isolation required.
Existing Network Elements
MedCore already has: a core network switch, a perimeter firewall, internet connection (1 Gbps), Active Directory (Windows Server 2022), a RADIUS server (FreeRADIUS), and an existing PKI (internal CA: medcore-ca.medcore.local). You build on top of these — you do not replace them.
Threat Concerns (from CISO brief)
  1. Ransomware. The last major regional hospital breach used lateral movement from a compromised workstation to the EHR database in under 4 hours.
  2. Credential theft. Clinical staff reuse passwords and click phishing links at above-average rates.
  3. Rogue access points. The building has poor physical security in waiting areas.

Three Tasks, 35 Minutes
Each task builds on the last. Do them in order. A rushed Task 1 will produce a broken Task 2.
01
Network Architecture Diagram (10 min)
Sketch the network segmentation for the EHR deployment. Identify zones, firewalls between them, and how the three tiers are placed. Include remote clinical staff access. Use a whiteboard or paper — not a description, a diagram.
Relevant concepts: DMZ, VLANs, firewall placement, ZTNA vs. VPN for remote access, NAT at the internet boundary.
02
Wireless + Certificate Design (12 min)
Specify the wireless architecture for clinical staff and patient/visitor Wi-Fi. Then describe the certificate infrastructure needed for the EHR — which systems need certs, what the CA chain looks like, and how you handle revocation.
Relevant concepts: WPA3-Enterprise + 802.1X + RADIUS for staff, WPA3-Personal or captive portal for patients, SSID isolation, X.509 certificate chain, OCSP or CRL for revocation.
03
Security Operations Design (8 min)
Identify three specific log sources you would ingest into a SIEM for this environment. Write one correlation rule. Describe one automated SOAR response action.
Relevant concepts: Windows Security Event logs, firewall logs, authentication logs, SIEM correlation, SOAR playbooks, IDS placement.
04
Group Presentation (5 min)
One spokesperson presents the architecture diagram and one key decision from each task. Instructor will ask one clarifying question per group. Other groups may challenge.
Be ready to defend why your remote clinical access design is ZTNA over VPN (or VPN over ZTNA), and what your blast radius limitation is in each case.

Task 1: Network Architecture Diagram
Draw a network diagram placing all three EHR tiers, all firewalls, all network zones, and the remote access path. Be specific about what is blocked between zones.
Start here: The internet boundary and the hospital's existing perimeter firewall are given. Everything you draw inside that firewall is your decision.
Required elements in your diagram
1. Internet zone (external)
2. DMZ zone (with web tier in it or not — your call, justify it)
3. Application tier placement and which zone it is in
4. Database tier placement and isolation mechanism
5. Firewall positions (at minimum: internet-to-DMZ, DMZ-to-app, app-to-DB)
6. Remote clinical staff access path (label the technology)
7. Guest / patient Wi-Fi network and its isolation from clinical systems
8. At least one VLAN label on the internal clinical network
Decision to Justify: Remote Access
Clinical staff access EHR from home and from clinic sites. You must choose a remote access architecture. VPN places them on the hospital network. ZTNA gives them per-application access. Which do you choose, and what is the security justification?
The Ransomware Constraint
CISO said ransomware moved workstation to EHR database in 4 hours in a previous incident. Your diagram must make that lateral movement path more expensive — if workstations are compromised, the attacker must cross at least two controlled boundaries to reach PHI. Label those boundaries.

Common mistake to avoid
Placing the EHR application server in the DMZ because it is "semi-public." The app server is never internet-accessible — only the web tier is. The web tier proxies requests to the app server across a controlled firewall boundary. If the app server is in the DMZ, a compromised web server has direct access to the EHR business logic and its database connection string.

Task 2: Wireless + Certificate Design
Two separate SSIDs. Two separate authentication models. One shared PKI infrastructure. Design both, then connect them through the CA chain.
Part A: Clinical Staff Wireless
Answer these questions in writing:
  1. Which WPA3 mode? (Personal or Enterprise) — justify.
  2. What authenticates the user: password, certificate, or both?
  3. What is the role of RADIUS in this design?
  4. If a nurse is terminated at 9:00 AM, how quickly is wireless access revoked, and what triggers it?
Part B: Patient / Visitor Wi-Fi
Answer these questions in writing:
  1. Shared passphrase, open + captive portal, or WPA3-Personal with a public key? Justify.
  2. How is this SSID isolated from the clinical VLAN? (L2 or L3 isolation?)
  3. Can a patient device ARP-scan the clinical network? Why or why not?
  4. Where does rogue AP detection sit in this design?
Part C: EHR Certificate Chain
MedCore has an internal CA (medcore-ca.medcore.local). The patient portal is internet-accessible. Answer:
  1. Does the patient portal use the internal CA or a public CA? Why?
  2. Does the app server → DB connection use TLS? Who issues those certs?
  3. Do clinical staff need personal certificates? For what purpose?
Part D: Revocation Plan
When a server certificate is compromised or a staff member certificate is revoked:
  1. What revocation mechanism does MedCore use — CRL, OCSP, or both?
  2. What is the maximum time between revocation and a client discovering the cert is revoked?
  3. If a server cert is compromised, what else must be revoked or rotated?
Slide 7 of 12  |  Task 2 Reference
Task 2 Reference: Wireless + Certificate Anchor Points
Use these to check your design decisions. Not rules to copy — constraints to reason against.
Clinical Wireless: 802.1X Pattern
WPA3-Enterprise + 802.1X: client sends identity to AP, AP forwards to RADIUS, RADIUS validates against AD. EAP-TLS (certificate-based) is more secure than EAP-PEAP (password-based) because there is no password to phish. Termination revokes AD account → RADIUS rejects authentication → access denied. No manual key rotation needed.
Patient Wireless: Isolation Requirement
VLAN separation is L2 isolation. The patient SSID maps to a VLAN that has no inter-VLAN routing to clinical VLANs. A patient device gets a guest DHCP subnet (e.g., 10.99.0.0/24). The inter-VLAN routing table has no route from the guest VLAN to the clinical VLANs. ARP is confined to the guest VLAN. Wireless isolation (client isolation) prevents patient-to-patient attacks on the same SSID.
Internet-Facing Certificate Rule
The patient portal is publicly accessible. Patients' browsers do not have MedCore's internal CA in their trust store. The patient portal certificate must come from a publicly trusted CA (Let's Encrypt, DigiCert, Sectigo, etc.). Internal systems (app-to-DB TLS, staff EAP-TLS certs) can use the internal CA because the internal CA is in the trust store of MedCore-managed devices.
The revocation timing problem — think through this
If a server certificate is compromised at 2:00 PM and the CRL is distributed on a 12-hour schedule, clients will not know about the revocation until as late as 2:00 AM. OCSP provides near-real-time status but requires clients to query the OCSP responder on every connection. OCSP Stapling moves that query to the server, eliminating client privacy leakage and the latency penalty. For MedCore's internet-facing portal: what is the acceptable exposure window? That answer drives the revocation mechanism choice.

Task 3: Security Operations Design
Detection is not a product. It is a set of decisions about what data to collect, what patterns to watch for, and what to do when the pattern fires.
A
Three SIEM Log Sources
Name three specific log sources you would ingest into the SIEM for this environment. Be specific: not "logs" but "Windows Security Event Log ID 4625 (failed logon) from all Domain Controllers." For each source, state what threat it helps detect.
Think about: authentication failures, network traffic anomalies, database access patterns, wireless authentication events.
B
One Correlation Rule
Write one SIEM correlation rule that detects a realistic threat to the EHR environment. Use this format: IF [condition 1] AND [condition 2] WITHIN [time window] THEN alert [severity] AND [action]. The rule must reference specific log fields from your three sources.
Examples of threats to detect: failed auth burst + successful auth (brute force), EHR access from new device + VPN from unusual geography, large data export from DB tier at 3 AM.
C
One SOAR Response Action
For the correlation rule you wrote, define one automated response action a SOAR platform would take immediately when the rule fires — before a human analyst reviews the alert. State what the action does, what system it affects, and what the analyst must do next.
Examples: disable the AD account, isolate the endpoint from the clinical VLAN, block the source IP at the perimeter firewall, create an incident ticket with full event context.

Task 3 Reference: Log Sources + Correlation Patterns
Do not copy these examples directly. Use them to calibrate your specificity level and identify gaps in your own design.
Example Log Sources
1. Windows Security Event ID 4625 (failed logon) from all Domain Controllers and EHR workstations.
2. Palo Alto firewall session logs: source IP, destination IP, port, bytes transferred — filtered for traffic crossing from workstation VLAN to DB VLAN.
3. FreeRADIUS authentication logs: username, MAC address, AP MAC, timestamp, result (accept/reject).
Example Correlation Rule
IF Windows Event 4625 count > 10 for same username
WITHIN 5 minutes
AND followed by Windows Event 4624 (successful logon) for same username
AND source IP is not in known-clinical-subnet
THEN alert CRITICAL
AND label as possible brute-force → credential success.
Example SOAR Action
On alert fire:
1. Disable the AD account via LDAP write call.
2. Log the action with timestamp and alert ID.
3. Create an incident ticket in ServiceNow with full event context (source IP, username, logon timeline).
4. Notify on-call SOC analyst via PagerDuty.
Analyst responsibility: confirm whether this is a true positive before re-enabling the account.
Placement reminder for IDS/IPS in this environment
A network IPS placed on the workstation VLAN to app VLAN boundary detects lateral movement attempts crossing that boundary. A host-based IDS on the database server catches queries that look like data exfiltration (e.g., a single connection pulling 50,000 rows from the PHI table in under 60 seconds). Both are useful; neither replaces the other.

Slide 11 of 12  |  Discussion Prompts
Defend Your Decisions
Instructor will ask one of these per group. Other groups may challenge. These are not gotcha questions — they are the real trade-off questions a security architect faces.
Remote Access Trade-off
You chose VPN or ZTNA for remote clinical staff. What is the blast radius if a clinician's credential is stolen and used by an attacker? Walk through what the attacker can reach in each design. Which is more forgiving of credential compromise?
Certificate vs. Password for Wireless
EAP-TLS uses per-user certificates. EAP-PEAP uses passwords inside a TLS tunnel. MedCore's clinical staff reuse passwords at above-average rates (from the CISO brief). Does that fact change your EAP choice? What is the cost of the more secure option?
SOAR Automation Risk
Your SOAR immediately disables the AD account when the brute-force rule fires. A false positive means a nurse cannot access the EHR during a medical emergency. How do you tune the rule to minimize false positives without making the detection so narrow it misses real attacks?
Rogue AP Response
The WIPS detects a rogue AP in the radiology waiting room matching the clinical SSID name (evil twin). What is the automated response, if any? What must a human do? What is the evidence you need to take the AP physically offline?
Scope of SIEM
Your three log sources cover authentication, network, and wireless. An attacker who already has valid credentials and legitimate network access will not trigger any of those rules. What fourth log source would catch authorized but anomalous EHR access — and what correlation rule would you write for it?

Task Debrief
Security architecture is not a single answer. It is a set of documented decisions, each with a rationale and a known residual risk. That is what you practiced today.
Architecture: What Most Groups Got Right
Three-tier isolation with firewall boundaries between each tier. Guest VLAN isolation. The debate over ZTNA vs. VPN is healthy — both are defensible if the access scope is correctly limited. The wrong answer is "VPN that gives full internal access with no further segmentation."
Wireless + PKI: The Key Insight
The patient portal needs a public CA cert because patients' browsers ship with public CAs, not MedCore's internal CA. Internal systems use the internal CA because MedCore manages those trust stores via Group Policy. Mixing up which CA issues which cert breaks either browser trust or internal certificate validation.
SecOps: The Hardest Part Is Specificity
Vague rules produce vague alerts produce alert fatigue. A rule that fires on "suspicious activity" is useless. A rule that fires on ">10 Event 4625 for the same account within 5 minutes, followed by Event 4624 from a non-enrolled device" is actionable. Specificity is the work.
What carries into Week 4
Authentication is the mechanism that makes all of this work. Firewalls, VLANs, 802.1X, TLS — every control depends on a reliable answer to "who is this?" Week 4 (Authentication & Identity Management, Risk Governance) is the machinery behind that answer. The architecture you designed today assumes authentication works. Week 4 describes how to make that assumption defensible.
