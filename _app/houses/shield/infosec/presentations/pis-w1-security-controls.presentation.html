What Is a Security Control?
Any safeguard or countermeasure designed to reduce the likelihood or impact of a threat exploiting a vulnerability.
Control vs. Countermeasure
The terms are used interchangeably on SY0-701. A control is a formal term from risk frameworks. A countermeasure is the operational term. Both refer to any mechanism -- technical, procedural, or physical -- that reduces risk to an asset.
Controls Reduce Risk
Controls do not eliminate risk. They reduce it. A firewall reduces the likelihood of unauthorized network access. An encrypted hard drive reduces the impact of physical theft. Risk reduction is the goal; risk elimination is not achievable in practice.
Controls Have Costs
Every control has a financial cost, an operational friction cost, and a maintenance burden. The decision to implement a control must weigh those costs against the risk reduction it provides. This is the economic foundation of every security architecture decision.
Two Classification Axes
SY0-701 tests controls along two independent axes. The first axis is implementation category: how the control is deployed (technical, administrative, physical). The second axis is functional type: what the control does in time relative to an incident (preventive, detective, corrective, and others). A single control can have one value on each axis -- for example, a firewall is both technical and preventive.
Slide 3 of 35
Control Implementation Categories
How the control is deployed. Three categories cover every security mechanism you will encounter.
Technical Controls
Implemented through hardware, software, or firmware. These controls enforce policy automatically without requiring human action at the point of use. Firewalls, intrusion detection systems, encryption, access control lists, and multi-factor authentication are all technical controls.
Administrative Controls
Also called managerial controls. Implemented through policy, procedure, training, and organizational practice. Acceptable use policies, security awareness training, background checks, separation of duties, and incident response plans are all administrative controls.
Physical Controls
Prevent unauthorized physical access to assets and systems. Locks, fences, badge readers, security guards, CCTV cameras, mantraps, and cable locks are physical controls. Physical security is the outermost layer of defense in depth -- if an attacker can touch hardware, most technical controls fail.
Facility context: the SHIELD-7 lab uses badge readers (technical/physical), requires all personnel to complete biosafety training annually (administrative), and installs reinforced specimen vault doors (physical). Each category addresses a different attack vector.
Slide 4 of 35
Technical Controls in Depth
Software and hardware mechanisms that enforce policy without human intervention at execution time.
Network-Layer Technical Controls
Firewalls filter traffic by port, protocol, and IP. Intrusion Prevention Systems (IPS) block traffic matching attack signatures in real time. Network Access Control (NAC) enforces posture checks before granting access. VPNs encrypt traffic in transit. These operate at the perimeter and internally.
Host-Layer Technical Controls
Antivirus and EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response) software monitors processes and files on individual systems. Host-based firewalls filter traffic at the OS level. Full-disk encryption (FDE) protects data if the device is stolen. Patch management removes known vulnerabilities from software.
Identity and Access Technical Controls
Multi-factor authentication (MFA) requires two or more verification factors. Role-based access control (RBAC) limits permissions to what the role requires. Privileged Access Management (PAM) vaults and audits administrative credentials. Single Sign-On (SSO) centralizes authentication across systems.
Data-Layer Technical Controls
Encryption at rest protects stored data. Encryption in transit (TLS) protects data moving across networks. Data Loss Prevention (DLP) tools scan outbound traffic for sensitive data patterns and block or alert on violations. Database activity monitoring logs and audits all queries against sensitive tables.
Slide 5 of 35
Administrative Controls in Depth
Policy, procedure, and organizational practice. The most commonly overlooked control category -- and the most frequently tested on SY0-701.
Security Policies and Standards
Formal documents that define what the organization requires of its personnel. An Acceptable Use Policy (AUP) defines permitted uses of organizational assets. A password standard specifies minimum complexity requirements. Policies set the expectation; other controls enforce it.
Security Awareness Training
Humans are consistently the weakest link in security. Phishing simulations, onboarding training, and regular refresher modules train personnel to recognize and report social engineering attempts. Training is an administrative control because it changes behavior through education rather than technical enforcement.
Separation of Duties
No single person controls all steps of a critical process. A person who initiates a wire transfer cannot also approve it. A developer who writes code cannot also deploy it to production without a second reviewer. Separation of duties reduces both fraud risk and error risk simultaneously.
Job Rotation and Mandatory Vacation
Rotating personnel through roles limits the accumulation of unchecked privilege and exposes fraud that depends on a single individual's continued presence. Mandatory vacation policies force employees to be absent long enough that irregular activity patterns become visible to those covering their duties.
Slide 6 of 35
Physical Controls in Depth
Barriers and mechanisms that prevent or detect unauthorized physical access. Often underestimated in digital-focused security programs.
Perimeter Physical Controls
Fences, bollards, and vehicle barriers prevent unauthorized vehicles from approaching the facility. Lighting reduces concealment opportunities for intruders. Security guards provide human judgment that automated systems cannot replicate. CCTV provides deterrence and post-incident evidence collection.
Access Control Entry Points
Mantraps (also called airlocks or sally ports) require a person to badge into a small enclosed space, wait for the outer door to close, then badge through an inner door. This prevents tailgating because only one person can be authenticated at a time before proceeding. Common in data centers and secure facilities.
Asset Physical Protection
Cable locks secure laptops and workstations to desks. Server rack locks prevent unauthorized access to hardware. Equipment cages isolate critical systems from shared colocation environments. Media safes store backup tapes and drives with fire and water resistance. Physical destruction of decommissioned media prevents data recovery.
Environmental Controls
Fire suppression systems protect equipment and personnel. Temperature and humidity monitoring prevents hardware failure. UPS (Uninterruptible Power Supply) and generators ensure continuity during power events. These are physical controls because they protect hardware availability through environmental management rather than software configuration.

Control Functional Types
What the control does in relation to an incident. The second axis of every control classification question on SY0-701.
Preventive
Stops an incident before it occurs. The control blocks the threat action entirely. Examples: firewalls, MFA, locked doors, background checks, security awareness training (prevents employees from being successfully phished). The gold standard -- if you can prevent the incident, detection and correction are unnecessary.
Detective
Identifies an incident during or after it occurs. Does not stop the attack but generates awareness that enables response. Examples: SIEM, IDS, audit logs, CCTV footage review, file integrity monitoring (FIM), security audits. Detective controls are critical when preventive controls fail or are bypassed.
Corrective
Reduces the impact of an incident after it is detected. Restores systems to a known-good state. Examples: backups and restore procedures, patch management (corrects vulnerabilities), incident response playbooks, antivirus quarantine actions. Corrective controls close the gap opened by a successful attack.
The Security Control Timeline
Preventive controls act BEFORE an incident (attack blocked). Detective controls act DURING or AFTER (incident identified). Corrective controls act AFTER detection (damage contained or reversed). A mature security program deploys all three across every critical asset. Gaps in any phase create exploitable windows.
Slide 8 of 35
Preventive Controls
Block the threat action before it succeeds. The most cost-effective controls when the threat is well-defined.
Technical Preventive
Firewall rules block unauthorized traffic before it reaches the target system. Intrusion Prevention System (IPS) drops packets matching known attack signatures. Application whitelisting prevents unauthorized software from executing. MFA blocks credential-stuffing attacks even when passwords are compromised.
Administrative Preventive
Background checks prevent hiring individuals with histories of fraud or espionage. Security awareness training prevents employees from falling for phishing attacks before credentials are entered. Separation of duties prevents any single actor from initiating and completing a fraudulent transaction without oversight.
Physical Preventive
Mantraps prevent tailgating into secured areas. Bollards prevent vehicle-ramming attacks. Badge readers allow only authorized personnel to access server rooms. Fences deter casual unauthorized access to facility perimeters. Preventive physical controls create barriers the attacker must overcome before reaching the asset.
SHIELD-7 preventive layer: Sector 9 requires a retinal scan before entry -- a technical preventive control. Personnel policy prohibits sharing access credentials -- an administrative preventive control. Reinforced steel doors require a physical attack to bypass -- a physical preventive control.
Slide 9 of 35
Detective Controls
Identify and alert when an incident has occurred or is occurring. The assumption: preventive controls will sometimes fail.
Technical Detective
Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) aggregates logs and triggers alerts on anomalous patterns. Intrusion Detection System (IDS) generates alerts on suspicious traffic without blocking it. File Integrity Monitoring (FIM) alerts when critical system files are modified. Log analysis pipelines correlate events across systems to surface indicators of compromise.
Administrative Detective
Security audits review access logs, configuration settings, and user activity to identify policy violations. Penetration tests identify vulnerabilities before attackers do. Periodic access reviews identify accounts with inappropriate privileges. Variance reports flag unusual transaction patterns in financial systems. Human review remains critical for context that automated systems miss.
Physical Detective
CCTV footage captures unauthorized physical access and is reviewed after incidents. Motion detectors trigger alerts when movement is detected in secured areas outside working hours. Security patrols provide mobile detection coverage that fixed cameras cannot. Badge reader logs show which credentials were used to access which area at what time.
Slide 10 of 35
Corrective Controls
Restore systems to a known-good state and limit damage after an incident is detected.
Technical Corrective
Backup and restore systems allow recovery to a state before the incident. Patch management corrects the vulnerability the attacker exploited. Antivirus quarantine isolates malicious files without destroying them (preserving evidence). System reimaging from a known-good baseline removes persistent malware that manual remediation misses.
Administrative Corrective
Incident response plans provide step-by-step procedures for containing, eradicating, and recovering from specific incident types. Lessons-learned reviews after incidents update policies and procedures to prevent recurrence. Personnel actions (termination, retraining) address insider threats or human error that caused the incident.
Physical Corrective
Replacing damaged or stolen hardware restores physical availability. Relocating critical assets from a compromised area to a secure zone limits continued exposure. Fire suppression systems, once triggered, correct the environmental threat before it destroys equipment. Emergency power restoration procedures correct availability failures caused by power events.

Additional Functional Types
SY0-701 extends the three primary types with three additional classifications. All six appear on the exam.
Deterrent
Discourages a threat actor from attempting an attack. Does not technically prevent or detect -- it influences attacker decision-making. Warning banners on login screens, visible CCTV cameras, security guard presence, and notification of employee monitoring are deterrents. Effective only against rational actors who assess costs before acting.
Compensating
A substitute control implemented when the ideal control cannot be used due to cost, technical, or operational constraints. A system that cannot be patched (legacy medical device) might have compensating controls of network isolation, application-layer filtering, and enhanced monitoring. Compensating controls are formally documented in risk frameworks and compliance audits.
Directive
Guides or instructs desired behavior. Policies, procedures, signage, and training materials are directive controls. They specify what people should do rather than technically enforcing or monitoring behavior. "Authorized Personnel Only" signs are directive. When ignored without consequence, directive controls become ineffective -- enforcement elevates them to administrative preventive controls.
Exam Note: Six Types
Preventive, Detective, Corrective, Deterrent, Compensating, Directive. Know each by definition and example. Questions often present a scenario and ask you to identify which type applies, or ask which control type is missing from a described security program.

NIST Control Frameworks
National Institute of Standards and Technology provides the authoritative control catalogs used in federal and enterprise environments.
NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5
Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations. The comprehensive control catalog used by federal agencies. Organized into 20 control families (AC, AU, CA, CM, CP, IA, IR, MA, MP, PE, PL, PM, PS, PT, RA, SA, SC, SI, SR, AT). Each family contains specific controls with implementation guidance and assessment procedures.
NIST CSF 2.0
Cybersecurity Framework organizes security activities into six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. Each function maps to control categories and specific outcomes. CSF 2.0 added Govern as a new top-level function in 2024, recognizing that governance is the foundation that all other functions depend on.
CIS Controls v8.1
Center for Internet Security 18 Critical Security Controls prioritized by attack prevalence. Implementation Groups (IG1, IG2, IG3) allow organizations to scale controls to their size and maturity. IG1 is essential hygiene -- every organization should implement it. IG3 is enterprise-level defense. Used in assessments to benchmark control maturity.
ISO/IEC 27001 Annex A
International standard for information security management systems. Annex A contains 93 controls organized into four themes: Organizational, People, Physical, Technological. Organizations pursuing ISO 27001 certification implement controls from Annex A and justify any exclusions. Widely used in international and regulated industries.

Control Selection Process
Selecting controls is not arbitrary. The process flows from risk assessment to implementation to verification.
1Identify Assets: Catalog what needs protection. Apply a criticality and sensitivity rating. Without a complete asset inventory, controls will have gaps you cannot see.
2Identify Threats and Vulnerabilities: For each asset, what threats apply and what vulnerabilities exist? Threat modeling and vulnerability scanning feed this step.
3Assess Risk: Combine likelihood and impact to produce a risk score. Prioritize high-risk combinations. Not all risks receive equal investment.
4Select Controls: Choose controls from a framework catalog that address identified risks. Balance cost against risk reduction. Prefer controls with multiple functional types (a SIEM is both detective and enables corrective response).
5Implement Controls: Deploy the control in the environment. Configure it correctly. Misconfigured controls often provide no real protection while consuming maintenance resources.
6Assess and Monitor: Verify the control functions as intended. Test detective controls with simulated attacks. Test preventive controls by attempting the action they should block. Continuous monitoring detects control degradation over time.

Defense in Depth and Layered Controls
No single control is sufficient. Layering controls across categories and functional types creates resilience against control bypass.
Why Layers Are Necessary
Every control can fail. Firewalls have bypass vulnerabilities. Encrypted channels can be terminated at compromised endpoints. Security awareness training does not prevent all phishing. Defense in depth ensures that when one control fails, additional controls remain. An attacker bypassing the perimeter should still face host, application, and data controls.
Layer Independence
Controls should fail independently. If a technical preventive control fails, the detective control should still function. If both fail, a corrective control should limit damage. The failure of one layer must not automatically compromise adjacent layers. This is why a firewall vendor should not also provide the SIEM -- a single vendor compromise would weaken multiple layers.
The Seven Layers of Defense
Perimeter (firewall, IPS), Network (segmentation, VLAN), Host (EDR, FDE, patching), Application (input validation, WAF), Data (encryption, DLP), Identity (MFA, PAM, least privilege), Physical (mantraps, guards, CCTV). Controls at each layer should span all three implementation categories.
Control Gaps vs. Redundancy
A gap is a layer or functional type with no control. A gap means an attacker who reaches that layer faces no resistance. Redundancy is multiple controls at the same layer providing the same function -- useful for availability, but less valuable than filling gaps at other layers. Audit control coverage by layer AND by functional type, not just by total count.

Principle of Least Privilege
Users, processes, and systems should have the minimum access rights required to perform their function -- and nothing more.
What Least Privilege Reduces
Blast radius limitation. If a user account with minimal permissions is compromised, the attacker inherits only those minimal permissions. An admin account compromise gives the attacker full control. Least privilege limits what an attacker gains from any single credential theft, malware infection, or insider abuse scenario.
Applying Least Privilege
Role-based access control assigns permissions to roles, not individuals. Users receive roles matching their job function. Privileged Access Management (PAM) requires explicit justification for elevated access, with time-limited grants. Just-In-Time (JIT) access provisions elevated rights only when the task requires it and revokes them immediately afterward.
The Admin Account Problem
Administrators who perform daily tasks (email, web browsing) with elevated accounts expose those privileges to every attack vector they encounter. Dual-account policy requires administrators to use a standard account for daily tasks and a separate privileged account only when performing administrative tasks. The privileged account should have no email access.
Privilege Creep
Over time, users accumulate permissions they were temporarily granted and never lost. A user who moved from accounting to IT still has accounting system access. Quarterly access reviews audit all user permissions against current role requirements and revoke excess. Privilege creep is a chronic administrative failure in organizations without formal reviews.
Slide 17 of 35
Separation of Duties
Divide critical tasks across multiple individuals so that no single person can commit fraud or cause major damage alone.
Classic Financial Example
Person A can initiate a payment request. Person B can approve it. Person C processes the transfer. No single person has all three permissions. An attacker compromising one account cannot complete a fraudulent transaction without also compromising the other two. The cost of fraud increases to require collusion of multiple parties.
IT/DevOps Application
Developers write code. A separate QA team reviews and tests it. A separate operations team deploys to production. No developer deploys their own code to production. This prevents accidental or intentional insertion of backdoors that bypass peer review. Change management systems enforce these boundaries with approval workflows.
Two-Person Integrity
A specific form of separation of duties where two authorized individuals must both be physically present for an action to occur. Used in nuclear weapon control, high-security vault access, and certain government cryptographic key operations. Both parties must verify before the action proceeds -- neither can act alone under any circumstance.
Exam Distinction: SoD vs. Least Privilege
Least privilege limits what any one account can access. Separation of duties limits what any one person can accomplish using their total set of permissions across systems. Both are needed. An account with least privilege but no SoD enforcement still allows a single person to abuse all their permitted actions.

Access Control Models
The technical framework that determines how permissions are assigned and enforced. Each model suits different environments.
Discretionary Access Control (DAC)
Resource owners decide who has access to their resources. Windows NTFS permissions are DAC -- the file owner grants read/write/execute to specific users or groups. Flexible but difficult to manage at scale. A compromised account can grant its own permissions to an attacker, making DAC weaker against insider threats than MAC.
Mandatory Access Control (MAC)
Access is determined by the system based on security labels (classification levels). No individual user can grant access -- the system enforces policy. Used in government and military systems where data classified SECRET cannot flow to a process cleared only at CONFIDENTIAL. SELinux implements MAC concepts in Linux environments.
Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)
Permissions are assigned to roles. Users are assigned to roles. A "Help Desk" role has password reset permissions. A "DBA" role has database query permissions. When a user changes jobs, their role assignment changes -- all permissions update automatically. RBAC scales to large organizations far better than individual permission assignment.
Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC)
Access decisions evaluate multiple attributes simultaneously: user department, data classification, device security posture, time of day, location, and project membership. "Grant access if user is in HR department AND device is enrolled in MDM AND request is from corporate network." More granular than RBAC; used in Zero Trust architectures and cloud IAM policies.

Compensating Controls
Substitute controls that provide equivalent protection when the preferred control cannot be implemented.
When Compensating Controls Apply
Legacy systems that cannot be patched because vendor support has ended. Medical devices with FDA-approved firmware that cannot be modified. Industrial control systems running on operating systems that cannot support modern security agents. Compensating controls formally acknowledge the gap and document the alternative protection.
Designing Valid Compensating Controls
A compensating control must provide protection equivalent to the missing control -- not weaker. Isolating an unpatched system on a separate VLAN, adding application-layer filtering, and increasing monitoring frequency together may be equivalent to patching. The auditor or QSA must accept the compensating control as sufficient before it can be credited.
PCI DSS Compensating Controls
PCI DSS explicitly defines requirements for compensating controls in Appendix B. The organization must document the legitimate technical or business constraint, the compensating control, the additional risk created, and the additional controls implemented to address that risk. Compensating controls are not permanent -- they require annual review.
Common Compensating Control Combinations
Unpatched legacy OS: network isolation + enhanced monitoring + host-based IDS. No MFA capability: hardware token + IP allowlisting + session timeout reduction. No encryption at rest on legacy database: full network encryption (TLS for all access) + strict access control + enhanced audit logging. Each combination must close the specific gap the missing control would address.

Technical Control Deep Dive: Firewalls
The most foundational technical preventive control. Understanding firewall types is heavily tested on SY0-701.
Packet Filtering Firewall
Inspects individual packets at OSI Layers 3 and 4. Filters by source/destination IP, source/destination port, and protocol (TCP/UDP/ICMP). Does not inspect packet contents or maintain connection state. Fast and low overhead, but cannot detect attacks that span multiple packets. The simplest and oldest firewall type.
Stateful Firewall
Tracks the state of TCP connections in a connection table. Allows return traffic for established connections without requiring explicit outbound rules for responses. Can detect port scanning and SYN flood attacks by monitoring connection state. The standard firewall in modern environments; most "firewalls" are stateful unless otherwise specified.
Application-Layer Firewall (WAF, NGFW)
Next-Generation Firewalls (NGFW) and Web Application Firewalls (WAF) inspect payload contents up to OSI Layer 7. NGFWs identify applications regardless of port (blocking Tor even on port 443). WAFs protect web applications by filtering HTTP/S traffic for SQL injection, XSS, and OWASP Top 10 attack patterns.
Implicit Deny
The foundational firewall rule: any traffic not explicitly permitted is denied. Firewall rules are processed top to bottom, and if no rule matches, the implicit deny rule at the bottom blocks the traffic. A firewall without implicit deny (allowing all unmatched traffic) is not providing meaningful perimeter protection -- it is a logging device.

Technical Controls: IDS and IPS
Intrusion Detection and Intrusion Prevention Systems -- the distinction between alerting and blocking is the most tested IDS/IPS fact on SY0-701.
IDS (Detective)
Monitors traffic and generates alerts when suspicious patterns are detected. Does not block traffic. Sits out-of-band (on a SPAN port or TAP) so it receives a copy of traffic, not the live stream. An IDS that generates no alerts provides false assurance. An IDS that generates too many alerts is ignored. Tuning is continuous, ongoing work.
IPS (Preventive)
Sits inline in the traffic path. Can drop packets, reset connections, or block IPs in real time. The inline position means a malfunctioning IPS can disrupt legitimate traffic. Must be tuned carefully before deploying in blocking mode. Typically deployed initially in detection-only mode until the environment's normal traffic baseline is established.
Detection Methods
Signature-based detection matches traffic against known attack patterns. Fast and accurate for known threats, blind to zero-days. Anomaly-based (behavioral) detection identifies deviations from a learned baseline -- detects unknown attacks but generates more false positives. Policy-based detection alerts on rule violations regardless of attack signatures. Enterprise systems use all three.
False Positives vs. False Negatives
False positive: legitimate traffic flagged as malicious. Analysts must investigate, wasting time. Erodes confidence in the system. False negative: malicious traffic not detected. Silent attack success. Both are failures with different costs. Signature-heavy systems minimize false negatives. Strict anomaly thresholds reduce false positives. The tuning balance is a core security operations skill.

Technical Controls: Encryption
The primary technical control for confidentiality. Also supports integrity when combined with hashing and digital signatures.
Encryption at Rest
Protects data stored on disk, in databases, and in backups. Full-disk encryption (FDE) encrypts the entire storage volume. Transparent database encryption (TDE) encrypts the database files at the storage layer. Application-level encryption encrypts specific fields (SSNs, passwords). Each layer provides protection against different attacker access scenarios.
Encryption in Transit
TLS (Transport Layer Security) encrypts data moving between client and server. TLS 1.2 minimum; TLS 1.3 preferred. HTTPS is HTTP over TLS. Email encryption (S/MIME, PGP) protects message contents. VPN encrypts traffic between sites or between remote users and corporate networks. Certificate pinning prevents man-in-the-middle attacks using rogue certificates.
Key Management
Encryption is only as strong as its key management. Keys stored on the same system as the encrypted data provide minimal protection -- an attacker with system access has both. Hardware Security Modules (HSMs) store keys in tamper-resistant hardware. Key rotation policies limit the duration of exposure if a key is compromised. Key escrow ensures recovery access.
Hashing vs. Encryption
Encryption is reversible with the correct key. Hashing is one-way -- you cannot recover the original from the hash. Hashing is used for integrity verification and password storage (with salting). Encryption is used when the original data must be recovered. Never confuse the two on the exam. Saying a database "encrypts" passwords when it hashes them is a common mistake.

Administrative Controls: Key Policies
The policies that every organization's security program must maintain. Each reduces a specific category of risk.
Acceptable Use Policy (AUP)
Defines permitted and prohibited uses of organizational systems, networks, and devices. Employees acknowledge the AUP during onboarding and annually thereafter. Without a signed AUP, organizations cannot take disciplinary action for misuse -- there was no documented agreement defining misuse. The AUP is a directive administrative control that becomes the basis for corrective action.
Password Policy
Defines minimum complexity, length, rotation frequency, and reuse restrictions. NIST SP 800-63B-4 guidance: longer passwords (minimum 15 characters) matter more than complexity rules that create predictable substitutions (P@ssw0rd). Do not rotate passwords on a schedule unless compromise is suspected -- forced rotation produces weaker, more predictable passwords.
Data Handling Policy
Defines how data is classified, stored, transmitted, and disposed of based on sensitivity level. Maps data classifications (Public, Internal, Confidential, Restricted) to handling requirements. Employees who move sensitive data to personal cloud storage are violating this policy. Data handling policy is the governance basis for DLP technical controls.
Change Management Policy
Requires all changes to production systems to follow a documented approval and testing process. Prevents unauthorized configuration changes that introduce vulnerabilities. Provides an audit trail for changes that correlate with incidents -- most post-breach investigations reveal that the attacker exploited a recent unauthorized change or configuration drift.
Slide 24 of 35
Physical Security: Data Center Controls
Data centers concentrate high-value assets. Physical security failures here can bypass all technical controls simultaneously.
Site Selection and Perimeter
Data center location avoids flood plains, flight paths, and high-crime areas. Unmarked exterior -- no company signage identifies the building as a data center. Multiple ISP connections enter from different physical directions. Perimeter fencing with barbed wire or prickle strips. Vehicle barriers (K-rated bollards) prevent ram attacks against entry points.
Multi-Factor Physical Access
Data center access requires at least two factors: badge (something you have) plus PIN (something you know) or biometric (something you are). Mantraps enforce one-at-a-time entry and prevent tailgating. Visitor logs are maintained with photo ID. Escorts are required for all non-authorized personnel. Unescorted access by maintenance contractors is a common breach vector.
Rack and Equipment Controls
Individual racks are locked with electronic locks that log access by badge. Cage deployments in colocation facilities separate customer equipment with physical barriers. Equipment labels and asset tags provide physical inventory tracking. Cameras cover all rack aisles from multiple angles. Hard drive removal requires logging -- drives are never removed without authorization and documentation.
Media Disposal
Decommissioned drives must be sanitized before disposal. Overwrite (software wipe, NIST 800-88 compliant), degaussing (magnetic erasure, applies to HDDs and tapes, not SSDs), or physical destruction (shredding, pulverizing). Cloud storage decommissioning requires cryptographic erasure -- destroy the encryption key so encrypted data becomes unrecoverable without the physical drive being destroyed.
Slide 25 of 35
Control Effectiveness Measurement
Implementing controls is not enough. Organizations must verify that controls work as intended and remain effective over time.
Key Risk Indicators (KRIs)
Metrics that signal increasing risk before an incident occurs. Failed authentication attempts per hour, unpatched critical vulnerabilities by age, percentage of systems with overdue access reviews, time-to-detect metric for simulated attacks. KRIs give security leadership early warning that control effectiveness is degrading before the degradation produces an incident.
Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)
Metrics that measure security program performance. Mean time to detect (MTTD), mean time to respond (MTTR), patch compliance percentage, phishing simulation click rate, security training completion rate. KPIs tell you whether your program is improving. Trends matter more than point-in-time values -- a declining MTTD is more meaningful than achieving a specific number.
Vulnerability Assessment vs. Penetration Test
A vulnerability assessment identifies vulnerabilities in systems using automated scanners. It tells you what vulnerabilities exist. A penetration test goes further -- it attempts to exploit vulnerabilities to determine if they are actually reachable and chainable into a breach. Pen tests verify that controls work in practice, not just in theory. Both are administrative detective controls.
Red Team vs. Blue Team vs. Purple Team
Red team simulates adversaries using realistic tactics to test defenses. Blue team defends and detects. Purple team combines both -- red shares attack techniques with blue in real time so the defenders can improve detection coverage. Purple team exercises improve the security program faster than pure red vs. blue engagements because the feedback loop is immediate.

System Hardening
Reducing attack surface by removing unnecessary capabilities before systems are deployed. A foundational technical preventive control.
OS Hardening Steps
Remove unnecessary services and applications. Disable unused network ports. Apply security baseline configurations from CIS Benchmarks or DISA STIGs (Security Technical Implementation Guides). Rename or disable default accounts (Administrator, root, guest). Enable OS-level auditing. Set secure defaults for all configuration parameters before connecting the system to the network.
Application Hardening
Change all default passwords before deployment. Disable features not required by the use case. Enable logging at the appropriate verbosity level. Remove development-mode settings (debug logging, verbose errors, development credentials). Web servers: disable directory listing, server version disclosure, and unused HTTP methods (PUT, DELETE if not required by the application).
Network Hardening
Block unnecessary protocols at the firewall and switch level. Disable ICMP redirect messages (used in MITM attacks). Implement 802.1X port authentication to prevent rogue devices from connecting to physical switch ports. Disable CDP/LLDP on external-facing interfaces (discloses device model and IOS version to adjacent nodes). Enable DHCP snooping and ARP inspection.
Configuration Baselines
A documented secure configuration that all systems in a class must match. Baselines enable detection of configuration drift -- when a system deviates from baseline, it has either been misconfigured or compromised. Security Configuration Management (SCM) tools continuously compare running configurations against the baseline and alert or auto-remediate on deviations.

Patch Management
The corrective technical control that closes known vulnerabilities after vendor fixes are released.
The Patch Management Process
Monitor vendor security advisories and vulnerability databases (NVD, CVE). Assess patches for applicability and severity (CVSS score). Test patches in a non-production environment before deploying to production. Deploy in priority order (critical first, workarounds second). Verify deployment success. Document exceptions with compensating controls and review dates.
Patch Timing: The Window of Exposure
The window of exposure begins when a vulnerability is publicly disclosed and ends when the patch is deployed. Every hour in between is a period when attackers can exploit the vulnerability. Emergency patching (within 24-48 hours) for critical vulnerabilities. Standard patching cycles (monthly, weekly) for lower severity issues. Patch SLAs define maximum acceptable window widths by severity.
Zero-Day Vulnerabilities
A zero-day is a vulnerability with no available vendor patch at the time of exploitation. Patch management cannot address zero-days. Defense relies on detective and behavioral controls: anomaly detection, application whitelisting, sandboxing of untrusted code, and limiting the blast radius through least privilege. Zero-days are the reason layered controls are not optional.
CVE and CVSS
CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures) assigns a unique identifier to each disclosed vulnerability. CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System) scores severity 0-10 based on exploitability and impact. Critical (9.0-10.0) requires immediate action. High (7.0-8.9) requires rapid response. Medium (4.0-6.9) fits standard patch cycles. Low (0.1-3.9) can be scheduled with routine maintenance.

Monitoring and SIEM
Security Information and Event Management -- the central technical detective control that aggregates, correlates, and alerts across all log sources.
What a SIEM Does
Collects logs from firewalls, servers, endpoints, identity systems, network devices, and cloud platforms. Normalizes them into a common format. Applies correlation rules to identify patterns that span multiple log sources -- a single event on one system is noise; the same event pattern across ten systems is a detection. Generates alerts for analyst review.
Log Sources
Firewall logs (connection allow/deny), DNS logs (queries and responses), authentication logs (success and failure, from AD/LDAP/SSO), endpoint EDR events, email gateway logs (inbound/outbound), web proxy logs (user browsing activity), cloud API activity logs (AWS CloudTrail, Azure Monitor). The more log sources, the better the correlation and detection capability.
Correlation Rules
Rules that identify attack patterns from multiple events. "Five failed logins on the same account within 60 seconds" correlates authentication logs to detect brute force. "A process on endpoint A communicated with an IP that also received DNS queries for a known malware C2 domain" correlates EDR and DNS logs to detect C2 communication. Rules require continuous tuning.
UEBA and SOAR
User and Entity Behavior Analytics (UEBA) applies machine learning to establish individual behavioral baselines and detect anomalies -- a user accessing systems they have never accessed before, or downloading 10x their normal data volume. Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response (SOAR) automates response playbooks, reducing analyst time-to-response for common alert types.

Incident Response as a Corrective Control
The IR process is the organization's most important corrective administrative control. Execution speed limits breach damage.
1Preparation: Build IR plans, identify team members and roles, establish communication trees, pre-stage tools and forensic environments. Controls that exist before the incident define maximum response speed.
2Identification: Determine that an incident has occurred and its scope. Use SIEM alerts, EDR findings, threat intelligence feeds. Scope definition drives all downstream decisions about containment strategy.
3Containment: Isolate affected systems to stop spread. Short-term containment (disconnect from network) preserves evidence. Long-term containment (rebuild or patch) allows business operations to resume. Containment is a corrective technical control.
4Eradication: Remove the threat from all affected systems. Malware removal, backdoor elimination, compromised account disabling. Failure to complete eradication results in re-compromise.
5Recovery: Restore systems to operational status. Restore from clean backups if needed. Verify integrity before returning to production. Apply missing patches and close the vulnerability exploited.
6Lessons Learned: Document what happened, what controls failed, what was done well, and what changes are needed. Update IR plan, policies, and controls based on findings. This is the administrative corrective control that prevents recurrence.

Control Failures and Residual Risk
Every control can fail. Planning for control failure is as important as implementing controls in the first place.
Why Controls Fail
Misconfiguration: firewall with an overly permissive rule set. Bypass: attacker uses an encrypted channel that the firewall inspects at Layer 4 but the attack is Layer 7. Social engineering: attacker convinces an administrator to create an exception. Technology failure: security appliance crashes and fails open. Organizational failure: security team did not receive an alert because the mailbox was full.
Residual Risk
The risk that remains after controls are applied. Controls reduce risk -- they do not eliminate it. Residual risk is formally calculated as Inherent Risk minus Control Effectiveness. Residual risk must be accepted, transferred, or reduced through additional controls. All organizations carry some residual risk because perfect control is not economically achievable.
Control Assurance
The confidence that a control is operating as designed. High assurance controls have been tested (penetration testing verified the firewall blocks the expected traffic). Low assurance controls are assumed to work but have not been verified. Regulatory frameworks require assurance evidence -- saying a control exists is not sufficient; you must prove it works.
Single Point of Failure
A control that, if it fails, eliminates the entire protection for that attack vector. A single firewall with no redundant security controls behind it is a single point of failure for the perimeter. Defense in depth and control layering exist specifically to eliminate single points of failure from the security architecture. Every critical asset should have no single point of failure in its protection chain.

Compliance Frameworks and Required Controls
Regulatory frameworks mandate specific control implementations. Understanding what each framework requires is tested on SY0-701.
PCI DSS Required Controls
12 requirements covering: maintain firewall configuration, do not use vendor-supplied defaults, protect stored cardholder data (encryption), encrypt transmission of cardholder data, protect against malware, develop secure systems, restrict access by need to know, identify and authenticate access, restrict physical access, log and monitor, test systems, maintain information security policy.
HIPAA Technical Safeguards
Access control (unique user identification, emergency access procedure, automatic logoff, encryption/decryption). Audit controls (hardware/software/procedural mechanisms to record and examine activity). Integrity controls (mechanisms to confirm PHI has not been improperly altered). Transmission security (guard against unauthorized access to ePHI in transit). Each maps directly to the three control implementation categories.
CMMC for Defense Contractors
Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification is required for companies working with the Department of Defense. Level 1 covers 17 basic practices from FAR 52.204-21. Level 2 covers 110 practices from NIST SP 800-171. Level 3 adds 24 additional practices from NIST SP 800-172. Controls are not optional -- unaudited companies cannot receive DoD contracts.
FedRAMP for Cloud
Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program requires cloud service providers serving federal agencies to undergo a standardized security assessment. Controls are selected from NIST SP 800-53 based on the system's impact level (Low, Moderate, High). A FedRAMP Authorization requires third-party assessment organization (3PAO) validation -- not self-attestation.

SHIELD-7 Control Architecture
Applying the control framework to the facility context. Every element of facility security maps to the classification system you just learned.
Specimen Lab (Preventive Layer)
Retinal scanner (Technical + Preventive). Mantrap at lab entrance (Physical + Preventive). Dual-authorization required for specimen cabinet access (Administrative + Preventive, two-person integrity). Personnel must complete biosafety protocol training before lab access is granted (Administrative + Preventive).
Monitoring Infrastructure (Detective Layer)
CCTV covering all corridors and specimen areas (Physical + Detective). Environmental sensors alert on temperature and air pressure anomalies that signal containment breach (Technical + Detective). Badge reader logs capture all access events correlated by the facility SIEM (Technical + Detective). Periodic access reviews ensure only active personnel have lab authorization (Administrative + Detective).
Incident Response (Corrective Layer)
Automated lockdown protocol isolates the affected sector on breach detection (Technical + Corrective). Containment response teams execute a pre-approved incident plan (Administrative + Corrective). Backup communication systems restore coordination capability if primary systems are compromised (Technical + Corrective). Breach review boards update procedures after each incident (Administrative + Corrective).

Module Summary
Security controls are classified by how they are implemented and what they do. Both axes must be understood to answer SY0-701 questions correctly.
The two-axis framework is the core mental model for this module. When you encounter any security mechanism, ask: what category is it (Technical, Administrative, Physical)? And what does it do in time relative to an incident (Preventive, Detective, Corrective, Deterrent, Compensating, Directive)? That classification tells you when it acts, what it requires, and how to audit it.
9 Facts to Carry Out of This Lecture
1Three categories: Technical (hardware/software), Administrative (policy/procedure), Physical (barriers/environment). Every control fits one.
2Six functional types: Preventive (before), Detective (during/after), Corrective (recovery), Deterrent (discourages), Compensating (substitute), Directive (instructs).
3IDS is detective; IPS is preventive. The difference is inline placement and the ability to block. Both use the same detection methods.
4Least privilege limits what an account can access. Separation of duties limits what one person can accomplish. Both are administrative preventive controls.
5Compensating controls require formal documentation of the constraint, the substitute controls, the residual risk, and an annual review date.
6Defense in depth layers controls across all three categories and all functional types. Gaps in any layer or functional type create exploitable windows.
7CVSS 9.0-10.0 is Critical. 7.0-8.9 is High. Patch SLAs must define maximum response times for each severity level. Zero-days require non-patch compensating controls.
8Residual risk is what remains after controls are applied. It must be accepted, transferred, or further reduced. It is never zero.
9Hardening, patch management, and configuration baselines are technical preventive controls that reduce attack surface before adversaries probe it.
