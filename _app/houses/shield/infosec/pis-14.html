CA Hierarchy -- Root, Intermediate, and Leaf Certificates

Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) is the framework of policies, procedures, and technology that enables the issuance and management of digital certificates. At its core is the Certificate Authority (CA) hierarchy. SY0-701 Objectives 5.1 and 5.2 require you to understand how the hierarchy works and why it is structured this way.

A PKI hierarchy has three levels: the Root CA at the top, one or more Intermediate CAs in the middle, and leaf (end-entity) certificates at the bottom. The Root CA is the ultimate trust anchor -- it signs its own certificate (self-signed) and signs the certificates of Intermediate CAs. The Root CA is kept offline and air-gapped to protect it from compromise.

Intermediate CAs are online and issue leaf certificates to servers, users, and devices. If an Intermediate CA is compromised, only the certificates it issued need to be revoked -- the Root CA and other Intermediate CAs are unaffected. This is why the two-tier hierarchy exists: to limit blast radius.

Root CA (self-signed, offline, air-gapped) | +-- Intermediate CA 1 (online, issues server certificates) | | | +-- server.example.com (leaf / end-entity certificate) | +-- mail.example.com (leaf / end-entity certificate) | +-- Intermediate CA 2 (online, issues user/device certificates) | +-- user@example.com (S/MIME user certificate) +-- device-laptop-001 (device authentication certificate)
Facility Context:

The facility's Root CA certificate is stored on an HSM (Hardware Security Module) in a physically locked vault with two-person integrity requirement for access. It is brought online twice per year to re-sign the Intermediate CA certificates. Every certificate issued to a lab workstation, server, or analyst traces back to this root. Compromise of the root would invalidate the entire clearance credential system -- which is why it never goes online.

Certificate Lifecycle -- CSR, Issuance, Renewal, and Revocation

A certificate's lifecycle begins with a Certificate Signing Request (CSR) and ends with expiration or revocation. SY0-701 tests each stage of this lifecycle and the mechanisms used to manage it.

Stage Process Exam Notes
CSR Generation The certificate requester generates a key pair. The private key never leaves the requester. The public key and identity information are packaged into a CSR and submitted to the CA. The private key is generated locally and kept secret. The CA never sees the private key. If a CA has the private key, something is wrong.
Validation and Issuance The CA verifies the requester's identity (DV/OV/EV depending on certificate type), signs the certificate with its private key, and delivers the signed certificate. CA signature proves identity. Anyone with the CA's public key (in the trust store) can verify the signature without contacting the CA.
Renewal Certificates have a validity period (typically 1-2 years). Renewal generates a new CSR and issues a new certificate before the old one expires. Expired certificates cause the same browser warning as untrusted certificates. Monitor expiration dates. Automated certificate management (ACME protocol) prevents expiration failures.
Revocation A certificate is revoked before its expiration when the private key is compromised, the subject changes, or the certificate was issued in error. Two revocation mechanisms: CRL (Certificate Revocation List) and OCSP (Online Certificate Status Protocol). Know the difference for the exam.

CRL vs OCSP: A CRL is a periodically published list of revoked certificate serial numbers, signed by the CA. Clients download the full CRL and check if the certificate serial appears. CRLs can be large and are updated on a schedule, creating a revocation window. OCSP allows clients to query the CA in real time for the status of a specific certificate. OCSP Stapling has the server periodically fetch its own OCSP response and include it in the TLS handshake, eliminating the client-to-CA lookup.

Real-World: DigiNotar Compromise (2011)

A rogue DigiNotar intermediate CA issued fraudulent certificates for Google.com, enabling man-in-the-middle attacks against Iranian users. The entire DigiNotar CA was removed from browser trust stores, immediately invalidating hundreds of legitimate certificates. Root CA compromise is existential -- there is no partial recovery. Every certificate in the chain is untrusted until re-issued under a new root.

Facility Context:

When a lab analyst's laptop is reported stolen, the first action is certificate revocation. The analyst's device certificate is added to the CRL and an OCSP responder is updated immediately. Within minutes, every system that checks certificate status will refuse connections from that certificate. The stolen laptop cannot authenticate to any facility system, regardless of whether the attacker knows the analyst's credentials.

Certificate Types, Pinning, and Key Escrow

Not all certificates are the same. SY0-701 tests certificate types by validation level and by use case. You must match the correct certificate type to the scenario.

Certificate Type Validation Level Use Case
DV (Domain Validation) CA verifies only control of the domain -- no identity verification Basic HTTPS for websites. Fast, cheap, automated (Let's Encrypt). No assurance of who owns the domain beyond DNS control.
OV (Organization Validation) CA verifies domain control plus organization identity Business websites. Organization name appears in certificate details. More assurance than DV.
EV (Extended Validation) Strictest identity verification: legal entity, physical address, operational existence High-trust sites (banks, e-commerce). Reduced phishing risk because identity is verified by the CA.
Wildcard Certificate Same as DV or OV but covers all subdomains *.example.com covers mail.example.com, www.example.com, etc. If the wildcard private key is compromised, all subdomains are affected.
SAN (Subject Alternative Name) Multiple specific domains in one certificate Covers a defined list of domains. More secure than wildcard (compromise affects only listed names). Common for Microsoft Exchange.

Certificate pinning is a technique where a client application hard-codes or pins the expected certificate (or public key hash) for a specific server. If the server presents a different certificate -- even one signed by a trusted CA -- the connection is rejected. Pinning prevents CA compromise attacks but makes certificate rotation more complex.

Key escrow is the process of depositing cryptographic keys with a trusted third party. It enables recovery of encrypted data if the key holder is unavailable. Key escrow is required in some government and enterprise contexts but creates a high-value target: whoever holds the escrow holds the keys to all encrypted data.

Facility Context:

The facility uses certificate pinning for all internal applications that connect to the specimen database. If an attacker compromises an intermediate CA and issues a fraudulent certificate for the database server, the pinned application will reject it. The application knows exactly which certificate to expect -- any deviation is treated as a potential MITM attack and the connection is terminated with an alert.

Digital Signatures and PKI Exam Scenarios

Digital signatures provide authentication, integrity, and non-repudiation. The process: the sender hashes the message and encrypts the hash with their private key -- this is the signature. The recipient decrypts the signature with the sender's public key to recover the hash, then independently hashes the received message. If the hashes match: the message has not been altered (integrity) and only the private key holder could have created the signature (authentication and non-repudiation).

Critical distinction: digital signatures use the sender's private key to sign and the sender's public key to verify. This is the opposite of encryption (which uses the recipient's public key to encrypt and the recipient's private key to decrypt). Getting this backwards is one of the most common exam errors.

Scenario Correct Answer Why
A server certificate shows an untrusted CA in a browser The CA that signed the certificate is not in the browser's trust store -- likely a private or self-signed CA Trust chains from the leaf cert through intermediates to the root; the root must be in the OS/browser trust store
An application must verify the identity of a server without querying the CA each time Certificate pinning or pre-installed certificate trust with OCSP stapling Pinning embeds expected cert/key hash; OCSP stapling pre-fetches revocation status so the client does not need to query the CA live
A private key is compromised before the certificate expires Revoke the certificate immediately via CRL/OCSP; generate a new key pair and new CSR; re-issue a new certificate Revocation invalidates the old certificate; the new CSR generates a new key pair (the old private key is discarded)
Organization needs to recover encrypted data from a terminated employee Key escrow -- copy of the encryption key was deposited with a recovery agent at issuance Without key escrow, encrypted data is unrecoverable if the only holder of the private key is gone
Facility Context:

Every specimen transfer record is digitally signed by the analyst who handled it. Non-repudiation is enforced: a signed record cannot later be denied. The signature is created with the analyst's private key, which lives only on their hardware token. If the analyst claims they did not sign a record, the signature can be verified against their public key in the clearance certificate -- and the claim is refuted by cryptographic proof.

Key Takeaways

  • Root CA is offline and air-gapped -- it signs Intermediate CA certificates only; compromise of the root invalidates the entire PKI
  • The CA never receives the private key -- CSR contains only the public key and identity information
  • CRL is a periodic downloaded list; OCSP is real-time per-certificate query; OCSP Stapling has the server pre-fetch and bundle its OCSP response
  • EV certificates provide the strongest identity assurance (legal entity verified by CA); DV certificates only verify domain control
  • Digital signatures: sender signs with private key, recipient verifies with sender's public key -- the opposite direction from encryption
  • Certificate pinning defeats fraudulent CA-signed certificates but requires application updates when the legitimate certificate rotates
