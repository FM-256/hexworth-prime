Mission Context

The human vector. The most dangerous exposure point in any containment facility. Technology can be patched. People can be manipulated. Every technique in this module exploits a predictable human response -- urgency, authority, trust, or fear. Your defense starts with recognizing the pattern before you act on it.

Social Engineering Techniques

Social engineering is the manipulation of people into performing actions or divulging information that circumvents security controls. It exploits psychology, not software. SY0-701 requires you to identify and classify each technique and recognize the indicators in scenario-based questions.

Phishing Mass-distributed fraudulent emails that appear to come from legitimate sources. Designed to harvest credentials or deliver malware. The attacker casts a wide net -- even a 0.1% success rate on a million-recipient campaign yields 1,000 victims.
Spear Phishing Targeted phishing against a specific individual or organization. The attacker researches the target using OSINT (LinkedIn, company website, social media) to craft a believable message. Success rates can exceed 30%.
Vishing Voice phishing -- phone calls impersonating IT support, banks, IRS, or other authorities. Attackers use caller ID spoofing to appear legitimate. Urgency and authority are the primary psychological levers.
Smishing SMS phishing. Text messages containing malicious links or requests for credentials. Mobile users are statistically more likely to click links in SMS than in email. Common scenarios: package delivery notifications, bank fraud alerts, two-factor authentication requests.
Pretexting Creating a fabricated scenario to extract information. The attacker assumes a false identity -- IT technician, auditor, new employee -- and uses the pretext to justify requests for access, credentials, or sensitive data. Pretexting succeeds in over 70% of physical penetration tests.
Baiting Leaving malware-infected physical media (USB drives, CDs) for targets to find and use. Classic experiment: 297 USB drives dropped in parking lots of a financial institution -- 98% were plugged in; 45% of users clicked files on the drive.
Tailgating Physically following an authorized person through a secured entrance without using one's own credentials. Exploits social norms -- people are reluctant to challenge someone following them through a door. Directly bypasses badge readers and mantraps.
Watering Hole Compromising a website that the target population is known to visit. The attacker infects the "watering hole" and waits for targets to come to it. Used extensively by nation-state actors targeting specific industries.
Typosquatting Registering domain names that are typographical variations of legitimate sites (gooogle.com, paypa1.com). Targets who mistype a URL land on the attacker's site, which may harvest credentials, deliver malware, or display spoofed content.
Operational Context

In this facility, social engineering is classified as a human vector attack. The pathogen does not need to breach a firewall if the operator opens the door. Every technique above has been used against containment facilities. The most successful breach in Hexworth history started with a phone call.

Psychological Principles That Attacks Exploit

Social engineering succeeds because it triggers predictable cognitive responses. SY0-701 Objective 2.1 includes understanding the psychological principles behind these attacks. Recognizing which principle is being exploited is the first step in defending against it.

Authority

"This is IT. I need your password to fix a critical issue." People comply with authority figures even when it violates policy.

Urgency / Scarcity

"Your account will be locked in 15 minutes. Act now." Urgency bypasses rational evaluation -- the target acts before thinking.

Social Proof

"Everyone on your team has already updated their credentials." Conformity -- if others did it, it must be legitimate.

Liking / Familiarity

People comply more readily with those they like or feel familiar with. Attackers research targets to impersonate trusted colleagues or reference shared connections.

Reciprocity

People feel obligated to return favors. An attacker who "helps" first (resolves a fake problem) creates a sense of obligation to comply with their subsequent request.

Intimidation / Fear

"You will be audited if this is not resolved." Fear of consequences disables careful evaluation and drives compliance to avoid a perceived threat.

Phishing Variant Classification

The Security+ exam tests your ability to distinguish phishing variants by target and channel. These distinctions are frequently tested in scenario questions.

Technique Channel Target Key Characteristic
Phishing Email Mass / undifferentiated Generic content, high volume
Spear Phishing Email Specific individual or org OSINT-researched, personalized content
Whaling Email C-suite executives High-value context, large financial impact
Vishing Voice/Phone Any individual Caller ID spoofing, urgency/authority triggers
Smishing SMS Mobile users Text with malicious link, higher mobile click-through rates
Pharming DNS / Web Any user of a targeted site Redirects users via DNS poisoning or host file modification
Operational Context

The most dangerous phishing attack against this facility was a whaling attempt targeting the facility director. The attacker had researched the director's LinkedIn connections, referenced a real conference they both attended, and requested an urgent wire transfer for equipment. The only thing that stopped it was a verification call -- the process saved the facility $2.3 million.

Social Engineering Defenses

Because social engineering exploits human behavior rather than technical vulnerabilities, technical controls alone are insufficient. Defense requires a combination of training, process, and technology.

Defense What It Addresses
Security Awareness Training Teaches employees to recognize phishing, pretexting, and tailgating. Simulated phishing exercises reduce click rates from 30% to under 5% within 12 months (SANS Institute research).
Verification Procedures Policy requiring out-of-band verification of any request for credentials, access, or financial transactions. Call back using a known-good number, not the one provided in the request.
Email Security (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) SPF validates sender IP. DKIM adds a cryptographic signature. DMARC enforces policy on failures -- p=reject rejects unauthenticated email impersonating your domain.
Physical Access Controls Mantraps (two-door vestibule where the first door must close before the second opens) prevent tailgating. Visitor escort policies prevent unauthorized physical access.
Removable Media Policy Administrative policy prohibiting personal USB devices. Technical enforcement via Group Policy or endpoint controls that disable unauthorized removable media.
Operational Context

The containment facility's human vector training runs quarterly. Every employee receives simulated phishing campaigns with immediate feedback. Every access request is verified through an independent channel. The protocol is not optional -- a single successful social engineering attack against an operator with BSL-4 clearance could end this facility.
