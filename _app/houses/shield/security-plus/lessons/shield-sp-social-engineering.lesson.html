Section 01

What Is Social Engineering

SY0-701 Obj 2.2

Social engineering is the use of deception to manipulate individuals into revealing confidential information or performing actions that compromise security. It does not exploit software vulnerabilities; it exploits human psychology. [RFC 4949] The attack targets the person holding the key rather than the lock itself.

The Internet Security Glossary (RFC 4949) defines social engineering as "an attempt by an unauthorized entity to gain the trust of an authorized entity by pretending to be one." [RFC 4949] Because no malware needs to be deployed, social engineering attacks frequently bypass perimeter defenses that organizations invest heavily to maintain. Technical controls can harden systems, but a single employee who trusts the wrong caller can hand an attacker everything that firewall rules and endpoint detection were designed to protect.

For the Security+ SY0-701 exam, social engineering appears under Objective 2.2, which covers threat vectors and attack surfaces including phishing, vishing, smishing, spam, spear phishing, business email compromise, pretexting, watering-hole attacks, typosquatting, and brand impersonation. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2] Understanding why these attacks work requires first understanding the psychological mechanisms attackers exploit.

The Human Is the Attack Surface CISA notes that phishing and related social engineering attacks are among the most prevalent and effective threats facing organizations today. They are effective precisely because human beings are wired to respond to social cues such as authority, urgency, and familiarity. [CISA]
Key Takeaway
  • Social engineering manipulates people, not systems. It is defined in RFC 4949 as gaining trust by pretending to be an authorized entity. [RFC 4949]
  • Technical defenses alone cannot stop social engineering because the human is the vulnerability.
  • SY0-701 Objective 2.2 covers the full taxonomy: phishing, vishing, smishing, BEC, pretexting, watering-hole, typosquatting, and brand impersonation. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Section 02

Psychological Principles

SY0-701 Obj 2.2

Effective social engineering attacks do not succeed by accident. Attackers deliberately invoke one or more well-understood psychological principles to lower a target's critical judgment and produce a desired action before the target has time to question the request. The Security+ SY0-701 exam requires candidates to recognize all seven of these principles and identify which one is being used in a given scenario. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]

Authority
The attacker claims or impersonates a figure with power: an executive, law enforcement officer, auditor, or IT administrator. People comply with authority figures because questioning them has perceived social or professional consequences.
"This is the IRS. You must provide your account number immediately to avoid a tax lien."
Urgency
The attacker creates a time-compressed scenario that forces an immediate decision, deliberately preventing the target from pausing to verify the request or consult a colleague.
"Your account will be suspended in 10 minutes unless you click this link and re-verify your credentials right now."
Scarcity
The attacker implies that an opportunity or resource is limited, driving action through fear of loss. This is closely related to urgency but focuses on availability rather than time.
"Only three licenses remain in your allocation. Act now before they are reassigned."
Social Proof
The attacker suggests that others have already taken an action, making the target feel that compliance is normal and expected. Also called "consensus."
"All of your colleagues in the finance team have already completed the new payroll verification step. You're the only one left."
Intimidation
The attacker uses threats, hostile language, or implied consequences to force compliance. Intimidation can accompany authority, applying coercive pressure on top of a claimed power differential.
"If you don't approve this transfer immediately, your manager will be hearing about your refusal to cooperate with the audit."
Familiarity / Trust
The attacker exploits existing relationships or creates the appearance of one. Using names of known colleagues, referencing recent events, or pretending to be a vendor the target already works with all invoke trust.
"Hi, it's Mike from the help desk. I was just on a ticket with your teammate Sarah about this same issue."
Consensus
Closely related to social proof, consensus emphasizes group agreement to normalize a request. When a target believes that a group they belong to has collectively endorsed something, they are less likely to deviate.
"The entire security team approved this change during the change control meeting you missed on Tuesday."
Exam Tip: Combining Principles Real attacks rarely invoke just one principle. A well-crafted phishing email might combine authority (an executive sender name), urgency (a deadline), and familiarity (referencing a real internal project name). On the exam, identify the primary principle being leveraged in the scenario. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Key Takeaway
  • Seven principles drive social engineering compliance: Authority, Urgency, Scarcity, Social Proof / Consensus, Intimidation, Familiarity / Trust. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
  • Urgency and authority are the most commonly combined pair in phishing attacks because together they compress decision time and suppress questioning. [CISA]
  • Countermeasure: always verify via a known-good out-of-band channel (a phone number you already have, not one in the message) before acting on any high-pressure request.
Section 03

Phishing & Electronic Lures

SY0-701 Obj 2.2

Phishing is the use of deceptive electronic messages to trick recipients into revealing credentials, clicking malicious links, or downloading malware. MITRE ATT&CK catalogs phishing as technique T1566, documenting three sub-techniques that map directly to exam content: spearphishing via attachment (T1566.001), spearphishing via link (T1566.002), and spearphishing via service (T1566.003). [MITRE ATT&CK T1566] Understanding the taxonomy is essential for the Security+ exam because each variant has a distinct delivery channel and targeting scope. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]

Phishing (Generic)
Broad, mass-sent deceptive emails impersonating trusted brands (banks, cloud providers, government agencies) to harvest credentials or deliver malware. Volume-based: the attacker sends millions of messages and relies on a small percentage of recipients clicking. The message typically creates urgency and directs the target to a spoofed login page. [MITRE ATT&CK T1566]
Email authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC), phishing-resistant MFA, security awareness training. [CISA]
Spear Phishing
A targeted variant of phishing directed at a specific individual or organization. The attacker performs reconnaissance first, tailoring the message with the target's name, role, colleagues' names, and internal project references to increase credibility. Detection rates are much lower than generic phishing because the message is contextually believable. MITRE ATT&CK T1566 documents spear phishing as a primary initial access technique. [MITRE ATT&CK T1566]
Verified sender policies, mandatory secondary confirmation for credential or wire-transfer requests, privileged account monitoring.
Whaling
Spear phishing targeting senior executives, board members, or other high-value individuals. The attacker invests substantial reconnaissance effort because the payoff of compromising a CEO or CFO account is correspondingly high. A successful whaling attack often leads directly to Business Email Compromise (BEC). [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Executive-level security awareness, out-of-band verification for financial approvals, email banners on externally-originating messages.
Business Email Compromise (BEC)
The attacker gains access to or spoofs a legitimate business email account to authorize fraudulent wire transfers, redirect payroll deposits, or modify vendor payment details. BEC is distinct from phishing in that it often involves a real or convincingly spoofed email thread rather than a mass-blast message. BEC operations frequently begin with spearphishing used to gain initial account access, making phishing awareness training a direct BEC countermeasure. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2] [CISA]
Dual-approval requirements for wire transfers, phone verification of any payment change request, DMARC enforcement.
Vishing (Voice Phishing)
Phishing conducted over a telephone call. Attackers use caller-ID spoofing to appear to call from a bank, government agency, or internal IT department. The real-time conversational pressure of a phone call is significantly harder to resist than a written email because the target cannot pause and independently verify without ending the call. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2] [CISA]
Never provide credentials or OTPs over an inbound phone call; hang up and call the institution at a verified number.
Smishing (SMS Phishing)
Phishing delivered via SMS text message. Messages typically impersonate shipping services, banks, or government agencies and contain shortened URLs that redirect to credential-harvesting sites. The mobile context encourages quick taps rather than careful examination of the URL. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2] [CISA]
Do not click shortened URLs in unsolicited texts; navigate directly to the institution's known website instead.
Credential Harvesting: The Phishing Payload The objective of most phishing attacks is credential harvesting: collecting usernames and passwords entered into a spoofed login page. Harvested credentials are immediately useful for direct account compromise or can be sold and used in credential-stuffing attacks against other services. T1566 documents phishing as an Initial Access technique used by a wide range of tracked adversary groups. [MITRE ATT&CK T1566]
Key Takeaway
  • Phishing = mass email lure. Spear phishing = targeted. Whaling = targeted at executives. BEC = fraudulent financial action via spoofed/compromised executive email. [MITRE ATT&CK T1566]
  • Vishing uses voice calls; smishing uses SMS. Both bypass email filters. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
  • T1566 documents phishing as an Initial Access technique tracked across a broad range of adversary groups. [MITRE ATT&CK T1566]
  • CISA recommends phishing-resistant MFA, user training, and DMARC enforcement as the primary mitigations. [CISA]
Section 04

Impersonation & Pretexting

SY0-701 Obj 2.2

Impersonation is the act of pretending to be another person or entity to gain trust, access, or information. Pretexting is the creation of a fabricated scenario (a pretext) that provides the cover story for an impersonation attack. In practice, every impersonation attack requires a pretext: the attacker must explain why they are calling, why they need access, or why the request is legitimate. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]

Pretexting scenarios range from simple ("I'm IT, I need your password to fix your account") to elaborate multi-stage operations in which the attacker builds a relationship over weeks before making the final request. RFC 4949 defines impersonation as a form of masquerade attack in which one entity pretends to be another entity. [RFC 4949]

Brand Impersonation
The attacker impersonates a trusted brand (a bank, cloud provider, government agency, or popular software vendor) in email, SMS, or voice communications. The goal is to inherit the trust users already extend to the real brand. MITRE ATT&CK T1566 catalogs brand impersonation as a phishing lure mechanism used by numerous tracked adversary groups. [MITRE ATT&CK T1566]
Identity Fraud
The attacker uses stolen personal identifying information (PII) to assume a victim's identity with financial institutions, government agencies, or employers. Identity fraud is the downstream consequence of successful credential harvesting or data breaches. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Pretexting
The construction of a false narrative to justify a request. Classic pretexts include posing as an IT technician who needs to verify credentials, a vendor who needs building access, or a manager who needs an urgent wire transfer approved. The strength of the pretext determines how convincing the attack is. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2] [RFC 4949]
Reconnaissance Role
Effective pretexting depends on reconnaissance: gathering information about the target organization (org chart, employee names, project names, vendor relationships, public social media) to make the pretext believable. Open-source intelligence (OSINT) from LinkedIn, job postings, and public company filings provides attacker with naming conventions, role titles, and internal vocabulary. [MITRE ATT&CK]
Exam Distinction: Impersonation vs. Pretexting On the SY0-701 exam, recognize that impersonation describes who the attacker is pretending to be, while pretexting describes the story they use to justify their request. A single attack can involve both: "I'm calling from Microsoft support" (impersonation of a brand) "because your system flagged an unusual login from Russia" (pretext). [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Key Takeaway
  • Impersonation = pretending to be a trusted person or entity. Pretexting = fabricating the cover story that makes the impersonation believable. [RFC 4949] [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
  • Brand impersonation inherits the trust a target already has for the real organization. [MITRE ATT&CK T1566]
  • All impersonation attacks are more effective after reconnaissance. The better the attacker knows the target, the more convincing the pretext. [MITRE ATT&CK]
  • Mitigation: verify caller identity via a known-good number; enforce a "no credential over the phone" policy regardless of how convincing the caller sounds.
Section 05

Web & Network-Based Social Engineering

SY0-701 Obj 2.2

Several SY0-701 attack techniques abuse the web layer rather than (or in addition to) email. These attacks redirect users to attacker-controlled content, poison search results, or exploit trust in community websites. Recognizing each technique by its mechanism is critical for the exam.

Watering-Hole Attack
The attacker compromises a legitimate website that the target group is known to visit (an industry news site, a forum, or a professional association portal), then uses that site to deliver malware to visitors. The name comes from the predator strategy of waiting at a watering hole for prey to arrive. The attacker does not need to directly contact the target; the target self-identifies by visiting the site. This technique is documented in the MITRE ATT&CK framework under Drive-by Compromise (T1189), which describes watering-hole as a delivery mechanism. [MITRE ATT&CK T1189] [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Browser isolation, up-to-date browser and plugin versions, web content filtering, endpoint protection with exploit detection.
Typosquatting
The attacker registers a domain name that is visually or typographically similar to a legitimate domain, relying on users mistyping a URL or failing to notice a subtle character substitution. Common methods include adding or removing characters (paypa1.com), substituting visually similar characters (rn for m in rnicrosoft.com), using alternative TLDs (.net instead of .com), or inserting hyphens. The typosquatted site typically presents a convincing replica of the legitimate site. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Bookmark critical sites rather than typing URLs; check the address bar carefully; organizations should register common misspellings of their own domain proactively.
Pharming
A redirect attack that sends users to a malicious site even when they type the correct URL. The two main mechanisms are DNS poisoning (corrupting a DNS resolver's cache so that a legitimate hostname resolves to a malicious IP) and host-file manipulation (modifying the local hosts file on the victim's machine to redirect specific domains). Unlike phishing, pharming does not require the user to click a deceptive link; simply navigating to the correct URL redirects them to the attacker's server. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2] [RFC 4949]
DNSSEC validation, use of trusted DNS resolvers, HTTPS with certificate validation (a valid certificate for the legitimate domain will not exist on the attacker's server), endpoint monitoring for hosts file changes.
Baiting
The attacker exploits curiosity or greed by leaving infected physical media (USB drives, optical discs) in locations the target will find them, or by offering a digital download ("free movie download," "leaked internal document") that delivers malware. The target is motivated to plug in or open the bait item by curiosity or perceived value. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
USB port restrictions via endpoint policy, employee training to never connect found storage media, AutoRun disabled on all endpoints.

Key Takeaway
  • Watering-hole attacks wait for the target to come to the attacker by compromising a site the target already trusts. [MITRE ATT&CK]
  • Typosquatting exploits URL-typing errors; pharming redirects even correct URLs via DNS or hosts-file manipulation. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
  • Pharming is particularly dangerous because the user does nothing wrong: they type the correct address and still land on a malicious site. [RFC 4949]
  • Baiting exploits curiosity. The canonical example is a USB drive left in a parking lot. Endpoint USB port restrictions are the technical control. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Section 06

Disinformation & Influence Campaigns

SY0-701 Obj 2.2

Influence operations represent social engineering conducted at scale, targeting not a single employee but an entire organization, population, or public discourse. The Security+ SY0-701 exam includes disinformation and influence campaigns as a distinct threat vector within Objective 2.2 because they can be used to manipulate employee behavior, discredit organizations, or soften a target's defenses before a technical attack. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]

CISA, as the United States government's lead cybersecurity agency, addresses disinformation and influence operations as part of its election security and critical infrastructure protection mandates. CISA's guidance on countering influence operations emphasizes identifying narratives, understanding their mechanisms of spread, and building resilience through media literacy. [CISA]

Disinformation
False information deliberately created and spread to deceive. In a security context, disinformation can be used to confuse incident responders (planting false indicators), discredit security warnings ("that alert is a false positive from the vendor"), or manipulate employees into taking actions based on fabricated events. Distinguishing disinformation from honest error is that disinformation is intentional. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Influence Campaigns
Coordinated operations that use multiple channels (social media accounts, fake news sites, email newsletters, chat groups) to amplify a message, create the appearance of broad agreement, and shift behavior at scale. Nation-state actors use influence campaigns against democratic processes; criminal actors use them to manipulate stock prices or extort organizations by threatening reputation damage. [CISA] [MITRE ATT&CK]
Insider Risk Amplification
Influence operations can be directed at specific employees to radicalize them, turn them against their employer, or convince them that a particular action (leaking data, sabotaging systems) is justified or even heroic. This is the social engineering threat vector that intersects with insider threat programs. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Exam Note: Disinformation as Attack Preparation On the SY0-701 exam, recognize that disinformation can precede technical attacks: an attacker might spread false information to cause defenders to focus on the wrong threat vector, then execute the real attack while attention is diverted. This is a force-multiplier for social engineering at the organizational level. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Key Takeaway
  • Influence campaigns are social engineering at scale: coordinated, multi-channel operations to shift beliefs and behavior. [CISA]
  • Disinformation is intentional false information; it differs from misinformation (honest error) by intent. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
  • Influence operations intersect with insider threat: targeted campaigns can radicalize or manipulate specific employees.
  • Countermeasures: media literacy training, source verification policies, OSINT monitoring of brand reputation. [CISA]
Section 07

Physical Attacks

SY0-701 Obj 2.2

Physical security attacks exploit weaknesses in the control of physical space, access hardware, and on-site personnel behavior. They are often the final step in an attack chain that begins with social engineering: an attacker who has researched the target and constructed a believable pretext can then use that pretext to gain physical entry, observe credentials, or plant malicious hardware. The Security+ SY0-701 exam covers five physical attack techniques under Objective 2.2. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]

Tailgating / Piggybacking
An unauthorized individual follows an authorized person through a controlled entry point (a badge-access door, turnstile, or security checkpoint) without presenting credentials. Tailgating relies on social norms: people feel uncomfortable refusing entry to someone who appears to belong, especially if they are carrying packages or appear in a hurry. Piggybacking is the same technique but with the authorized employee's knowledge and (often reluctant) cooperation. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Mantraps (airlock vestibules), security awareness training on strict badge-only entry, security guards, and enforcement of the "one person per badge swipe" policy.
Shoulder Surfing
The attacker directly observes a target entering credentials, PINs, or sensitive data by watching over their shoulder. This can occur in person (public spaces, open-plan offices) or remotely via a compromised webcam or a nearby camera. In high-density environments such as airports, cafes, and co-working spaces, credential interception by shoulder surfing is a practical threat. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Privacy screens on laptops and terminals, positional awareness in public spaces, screen-blanking policies in sensitive areas, and shielding PIN entry at ATMs and point-of-sale terminals.
Dumpster Diving
Searching discarded materials for information that can be used in an attack. Corporate waste frequently contains network diagrams, employee directories, printed credentials, old hard drives, and documents revealing internal processes. In most jurisdictions, once material is in a public trash receptacle it is legally accessible. This technique directly feeds the reconnaissance phase of social engineering attacks by providing authentic internal naming conventions, org chart information, and project details. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Cross-cut shredding of all paper waste, documented media destruction for drives and removable media, locked and secured dumpsters, and a clear-desk policy.
RFID / Badge Cloning
Physical access cards using RFID or NFC communicate wirelessly with card readers. An attacker in proximity to a badge holder can use a concealed reader to capture the card's credentials without physical contact, then write a clone card. RFID readers capable of this attack are inexpensive and widely available. Older 125 kHz proximity cards (HID Prox, EM4100) transmit their ID in plaintext without authentication and are trivially cloned. Newer 13.56 MHz smart cards (HID iCLASS, MIFARE DESFire) provide cryptographic mutual authentication and are significantly harder to clone. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Upgrade to cryptographically authenticated smart cards, use RFID-blocking badge holders, implement multi-factor physical access (badge plus PIN), and deploy camera monitoring at entry points.
Malicious USB Drops
USB drives loaded with malware are deliberately placed in parking lots, lobbies, or mail rooms. When a curious employee plugs in the device, the payload executes automatically (via AutoRun) or presents itself as a document that exploits the preview engine. Research commissioned by academic security groups has shown that a meaningful percentage of people who find USB drives will plug them into work computers. MITRE ATT&CK documents hardware additions (T1200) and replication through removable media (T1091) as related techniques. [MITRE ATT&CK T1200] [MITRE ATT&CK T1091] [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Disable AutoRun via Group Policy, restrict USB port access through endpoint DLP, and conduct security awareness training that specifically addresses found media. Never plug in a USB drive of unknown origin.
Physical + Social = Combined Attack Physical attacks are rarely standalone. An attacker who tailgates into a building uses their physical presence to shoulder-surf credentials, plant a malicious USB, or steal documents. A social engineering pretext ("I'm a contractor here to service the HVAC") provides the cover to walk in freely. These techniques compound: each layer of the attack makes the next one easier. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Key Takeaway
  • Tailgating / piggybacking: follow authorized person through a controlled door. Mitigation: mantrap + strict badge-only entry policy. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
  • Shoulder surfing: observe credential entry. Mitigation: privacy screens, positional awareness. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
  • Dumpster diving: extract intelligence from waste. Mitigation: cross-cut shredding + locked receptacles. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
  • RFID / badge cloning: wirelessly copy access credentials. Mitigation: cryptographic smart cards + multi-factor physical access. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
  • Malicious USB drops: bait employees with infected media. Mitigation: AutoRun disabled + USB port restrictions + training. [MITRE ATT&CK] [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Section 08

Password Attacks

SY0-701 Obj 2.2

Password attacks are attempts to obtain or defeat authentication credentials through automated guessing, precomputed lookups, or credential reuse. They represent the intersection of social engineering (credentials harvested via phishing) and technical attacks (offline cracking of stolen hash databases). The Security+ SY0-701 exam requires understanding both the attack mechanics and the mitigations recommended by NIST, which are specified in NIST SP 800-63B, the federal standard for digital identity and authentication. [NIST SP 800-63B] [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]

Brute Force
A brute force attack systematically tries every possible combination of characters until the correct password is found. The attack is guaranteed to succeed eventually; the practical question is how long it takes. Password length is the dominant factor: each additional character multiplies the search space multiplicatively. A six-character password composed of lowercase letters has 26^6 (approximately 309 million) possible values; an eight-character password with mixed case, digits, and symbols has billions. NIST SP 800-63B addresses brute-force resistance by requiring a minimum password length of at least 8 characters and allowing up to at least 64 characters. [NIST SP 800-63B]
Account lockout after failed attempts, rate limiting on authentication endpoints, long password minimums per NIST SP 800-63B, MFA. [NIST SP 800-63B]
Dictionary Attack
Rather than trying all possible combinations, a dictionary attack tries a curated list of likely passwords: common words, names, phrases, keyboard patterns (qwerty, 123456), and known previously-breached passwords. Dictionary attacks are far faster than pure brute force because they exploit the predictability of human password choice. Appending numbers or substituting characters (l33t-speak: p@ssw0rd) provides minimal additional security against a dictionary attack because these transformations are themselves in attack wordlists. [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Block passwords that appear on known breach lists (per NIST SP 800-63B); rate limiting; MFA. [NIST SP 800-63B]
Password Spraying
Instead of trying many passwords against one account (which triggers lockout), password spraying tries a small number of commonly-used passwords (often just one or two per cycle) against a large number of accounts. The attack exploits lockout thresholds: by staying below the lockout limit per account while rotating across thousands of accounts, the attacker avoids detection. The passwords tried are typically the most commonly used: Password1, Welcome1, Summer2024!, and similar. NIST SP 800-63B specifically addresses this attack by requiring verifiers to implement rate limiting that restricts guessing attempts regardless of the distribution pattern. [NIST SP 800-63B] [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
Intelligent rate limiting that detects distributed low-and-slow patterns, MFA, monitoring for authentication failures across all accounts simultaneously. [NIST SP 800-63B]
Credential Stuffing
Credential stuffing uses username and password pairs from previously exposed data breaches and attempts them against other services, exploiting users who reuse the same credentials across multiple accounts. Billions of credential pairs from historical breaches (LinkedIn 2012, Adobe 2013, Yahoo 2016, and hundreds more) are freely available in criminal markets. If a user's email address and password from a breached site are the same as their banking or corporate login, credential stuffing succeeds. NIST SP 800-63B directly addresses this by recommending that verifiers compare candidate passwords against a list of known compromised credentials and require the user to change them. [NIST SP 800-63B] [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
MFA; unique passwords per service (enabled by password managers); blocklists of known breached credentials per NIST SP 800-63B. [NIST SP 800-63B]
Rainbow Table Attack
A rainbow table is a precomputed mapping of hash values to their original plaintext inputs. When an attacker obtains a stolen password hash database, they look up each hash in the table rather than computing hashes in real time. Rainbow tables make cracking fast for unsalted hashes because the computation was already done offline. The defense is a cryptographic salt: a unique random value added to each password before hashing, which means two users with the same password produce different hashes and no precomputed table can cover the salt space. NIST SP 800-63B requires that memorized secrets be stored using a salted hash with an appropriately hardened algorithm. [NIST SP 800-63B] [CompTIA SY0-701 Obj 2.2]
NIST SP 800-63B requires a salted, memory-hard hashing function for stored secrets. [NIST SP 800-63B] Widely-used implementations include bcrypt, scrypt, and Argon2, each of which imposes significant per-attempt computational cost to slow offline cracking. [OWASP Password Storage]
Hybrid Attack
A hybrid attack combines a dictionary list with brute-force mutation rules. For each dictionary word, the attack engine appends numbers, substitutes characters, or appends special characters according to rule sets. This efficiently targets the patterns real users create when forced to meet complexity requirements: Password1!, admin2024, Summer$24. NIST SP 800-63B directly discourages complexity rules that produce these predictable patterns, noting that they increase user frustration without meaningfully improving security against offline attacks. [NIST SP 800-63B]
Long pass phrases instead of complex short passwords; blocklists of common patterns; salted slow hashing; MFA. [NIST SP 800-63B]
Key Takeaway
  • Brute force: try all combinations. Defeated by length. [NIST SP 800-63B]
  • Dictionary: try likely words and patterns. Defeated by blocklists of breached passwords. [NIST SP 800-63B]
  • Password spraying: few passwords against many accounts (avoids lockout). Defeated by distributed rate limiting. [NIST SP 800-63B]
  • Credential stuffing: reuse breach credentials at other services. Defeated by MFA + unique passwords per site. [NIST SP 800-63B]
  • Rainbow table: precomputed hash lookup. Defeated by salt. [NIST SP 800-63B]
  • Hybrid: dictionary + mutations targeting complexity-rule patterns. Defeated by length + pass phrases. [NIST SP 800-63B]
Section 09

Password Attack Mitigations: NIST SP 800-63B

SY0-701 Obj 2.2

NIST Special Publication 800-63B, Digital Identity Guidelines: Authentication and Lifecycle Management, is the authoritative federal standard governing how authentication systems should be designed to resist credential attacks. Released in 2017 and updated with Revision 4 (draft) in 2022, SP 800-63B overturns several widely-held but counterproductive password practices and replaces them with evidence-based recommendations. [NIST SP 800-63B] Every password-mitigation claim in the Security+ SY0-701 curriculum should be traced back to SP 800-63B as the primary source.

The most impactful shift in SP 800-63B is the emphasis on length over complexity. Short passwords with mandatory special characters and numbers are weaker in practice than long passphrases without those constraints, because complexity rules predictably drive users toward easily-guessable patterns. [NIST SP 800-63B]

All recommendations per NIST SP 800-63B. [NIST SP 800-63B]

Length over complexity. SP 800-63B explicitly states that length is the primary contributor to password strength. Verifiers should not impose composition rules (must include a number, must include an uppercase letter) because these rules cause users to make minimal, predictable changes. A 20-character passphrase of four random common words is cryptographically stronger and more memorable than an 8-character mixed-case password. [NIST SP 800-63B]

Breached-password blocklists. SP 800-63B requires that verifiers compare prospective passwords against a list of values known to be commonly-used or compromised. If a user attempts to set their password to one that appears in breach databases, the system must reject it and require a different choice, explaining why. This is the primary technical control against dictionary and credential-stuffing attacks. [NIST SP 800-63B]

No forced periodic rotation. SP 800-63B explicitly recommends against forcing users to change passwords on a predetermined schedule unless there is evidence of compromise. Forced rotation drives predictable incremental changes (Password1 → Password2) and increases help-desk burden without improving security. Passwords should be changed when there is specific evidence of compromise, not on a calendar. [NIST SP 800-63B]

Rate limiting. Authentication systems must throttle the rate of failed guessing attempts. SP 800-63B requires that verifiers limit the number of failed authentication attempts on a single account and, critically, that the rate-limiting logic must handle distributed attacks (password spraying across accounts) and not solely per-account lockout. [NIST SP 800-63B]

Multi-factor authentication (MFA). NIST SP 800-63B defines Authenticator Assurance Levels (AAL1, AAL2, AAL3) and specifies that systems handling sensitive or government data require at least AAL2, which mandates a second authentication factor. Verifier impersonation-resistant authenticators (commonly called phishing-resistant MFA), such as hardware security keys implementing FIDO2/WebAuthn, are required at AAL3 and represent the highest-assurance mitigation against credential attacks. [NIST SP 800-63B]

NIST Guidance Summarized: What Works Long passphrases, breach-list blocking, MFA, rate limiting, and salted slow hashing are the five controls that NIST SP 800-63B supports with evidence. Short complex passwords on 90-day rotation cycles are specifically noted as counterproductive. If an exam question asks "which password policy follows NIST guidance," length + no-forced-rotation + breach-blocklist is the correct answer. [NIST SP 800-63B]
Key Takeaway
  • NIST SP 800-63B is the authoritative source for password policy on the Security+ exam and in federal/industry practice. [NIST SP 800-63B]
  • Length over complexity: 8-character minimum; allow up to 64+; no mandatory composition rules. [NIST SP 800-63B]
  • Block breached passwords: compare against known-compromise lists at set and change time. [NIST SP 800-63B]
  • No forced periodic rotation unless compromise is detected. [NIST SP 800-63B]
  • Rate limiting on authentication endpoints, including distributed spraying patterns. [NIST SP 800-63B]
  • MFA: required at AAL2 and above; verifier impersonation-resistant authenticators (commonly called phishing-resistant MFA, e.g., FIDO2) required at AAL3. [NIST SP 800-63B]
