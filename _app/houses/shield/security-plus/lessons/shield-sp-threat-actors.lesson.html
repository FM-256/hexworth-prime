Section 01

Defining Threat Actors

SY0-701 Obj 2.1

Before an organization can defend itself, it must understand who is attacking it, what they want, and how they operate. The Security+ SY0-701 examination tests candidates on their ability to characterize threat actors across multiple dimensions and to explain how those dimensions predict the types of attacks an organization is likely to face. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]

The Internet Security Glossary (RFC 4949) defines a threat as "A potential for violation of security, which exists when there is an entity, circumstance, capability, action, or event that could cause harm." A threat agent (or threat actor) is the entity that executes or enables the threat. [RFC 4949] Understanding the distinction between a threat (a potential for harm) and an attack (an active realization of a threat) is fundamental for the exam. [RFC 4949]

MITRE ATT&CK is the industry-standard knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques observed in real attacks. It organizes threat actor behaviors into a structured matrix of tactics (the adversary's high-level goals) and techniques (the specific methods used to achieve each goal), making it a primary resource for both understanding and categorizing threat actors. [MITRE ATT&CK]

RFC 4949: Foundational Vocabulary The Internet Security Glossary (RFC 4949) establishes authoritative definitions for the core terms used in threat analysis: threat, attack, vulnerability, exposure, risk, and countermeasure. These definitions underpin the CompTIA Security+ exam and all NIST guidance. Knowing the precise distinction between a vulnerability (a flaw that could be exploited) and a threat (the potential for exploitation) is an exam topic. [RFC 4949]
Key Takeaway
  • A threat is a potential for harm; a threat actor is the entity that carries it out; an attack is an active realization of a threat. Per RFC 4949. [RFC 4949]
  • MITRE ATT&CK organizes real-world adversary behaviors into tactics and techniques, enabling structured threat actor analysis. [MITRE ATT&CK]
  • SY0-701 Objective 2.1 requires candidates to explain threat actor types, attributes, motivations, vectors, and intelligence sources. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
Section 02

Threat Actor Types

SY0-701 Obj 2.1

The Security+ exam organizes threat actors into six categories based on their origin, organization, and operating model. Each type carries a characteristic risk profile and typical attack pattern. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1] MITRE ATT&CK Groups documents real-world named threat actor groups across nation-state, criminal, and hacktivist categories with their observed techniques. [MITRE ATT&CK Groups]

Nation-State / Advanced Persistent Threat (APT) HIGH sophistication
Government-sponsored groups that conduct long-duration, targeted campaigns. APT attacks are characterized by advanced techniques (not merely tools downloaded from the internet), persistent presence in a target environment over months or years, and clear political or economic mandates. CISA advisories document nation-state activity targeting critical infrastructure. [CISA] MITRE ATT&CK Groups catalogs named APT groups and their specific techniques in detail. [MITRE ATT&CK Groups]
External State-funded Long-duration campaigns Zero-day capability
Organized Crime / Criminal Syndicates HIGH motivation
Profit-driven groups that operate cybercrime as a structured business. They run ransomware-as-a-service operations, conduct large-scale payment card fraud, operate underground marketplaces for stolen credentials, and engage in business email compromise. Their primary motivation is financial gain. MITRE ATT&CK Groups tracks criminal threat actors alongside nation-state groups. [MITRE ATT&CK Groups]
External Financially motivated Medium-to-high sophistication Scalable operations
Hacktivist VARIABLE sophistication
Individuals or groups that use cyberattacks to advance a political, social, or ideological cause. Motivations are ideological or political rather than financial. Tactics include website defacement, denial-of-service attacks, and data leaks intended to embarrass targeted organizations. Skill levels and resources vary widely across hacktivist groups. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
External Ideologically motivated Variable resources Disruptive / publicizing intent
Insider Threat HIGH access risk
An employee, contractor, vendor, or other individual with authorized access who uses that access to harm the organization. Insiders may act out of financial gain, revenge, coercion, or espionage. Their primary advantage is legitimate access: they bypass perimeter controls that would stop external attackers. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
Internal Authorized access Variable motivation Bypasses perimeter controls
Unskilled Attacker ("Script Kiddie") LOW sophistication
An attacker who uses publicly available tools and exploits without understanding the underlying techniques. The term reflects their reliance on pre-written scripts rather than independent capability. Despite low sophistication, script kiddies present a real risk because attack tools are freely available, and their volume and lack of target selectivity means they will find vulnerabilities that automated scanners locate. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
External Low resources Opportunistic targeting Tool-dependent
Shadow IT UNINTENTIONAL risk
Employees or business units that deploy hardware, software, or cloud services without authorization from or knowledge of the IT and security organization. Shadow IT introduces unpatched systems, unauthorized data repositories, and bypasses that attackers can exploit. It is not malicious intent, but the security risk is equivalent to an internal threat vector because the systems fall outside security monitoring and patch management controls. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
Internal origin Unintentional risk Unmanaged systems Outside monitoring scope
Key Takeaway
  • Nation-state / APT: government-sponsored, advanced techniques, long campaigns, political/economic mandate. [MITRE ATT&CK Groups] [CISA]
  • Organized crime: profit-driven, ransomware/fraud/BEC, scalable operations. [MITRE ATT&CK Groups]
  • Hacktivist: ideological motivation, defacement and DDoS, variable skills. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
  • Insider threat: authorized access is the distinguishing danger; bypasses perimeter controls entirely. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
  • Script kiddie: low skill, public tools, opportunistic. Shadow IT: unintentional risk from unauthorized systems. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
Section 03

Threat Actor Attributes

SY0-701 Obj 2.1

The Security+ exam requires candidates to characterize threat actors across four standard attributes. These attributes determine the level of risk an actor poses and inform the defenses an organization should prioritize. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1] MITRE ATT&CK Groups documents these attributes for real named threat actor groups, providing empirical grounding for each category. [MITRE ATT&CK Groups]

Internal vs. External
Internal actors have authorized access to systems and networks. They are employees, contractors, or vendors. Their attacks bypass perimeter defenses because their access is legitimate. External actors operate from outside the organization's trust boundary and must overcome network perimeter controls, authentication mechanisms, and access controls to reach target systems. The internal/external distinction is a primary axis for risk prioritization. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
Resources / Funding
Nation-state actors have essentially unlimited resources: full-time development teams, access to zero-day vulnerabilities, custom hardware implants, and the legal and intelligence infrastructure of a government. Organized crime groups operate with significant but commercially constrained budgets. Hacktivists and script kiddies typically have limited resources and rely on publicly available tools. Funding level directly determines the attack surface a defender must address. [MITRE ATT&CK Groups]
Sophistication / Capability
Sophistication describes the technical depth of an actor's attack toolkit and operational tradecraft. Nation-state APTs develop custom malware, exploit zero-day vulnerabilities, and conduct multi-stage intrusions that blend into legitimate traffic. Criminal syndicates often purchase or lease sophisticated capabilities (ransomware-as-a-service). Script kiddies rely entirely on pre-built tools without modification. MITRE ATT&CK Groups records the actual techniques each named group has employed, providing an evidence-based sophistication profile for each. [MITRE ATT&CK Groups]
Intent / Motivation
Intent describes what the actor is trying to achieve. It ranges from espionage and data exfiltration (nation-state) to financial gain (organized crime), ideological disruption (hacktivists), personal revenge or coercion (insiders), and opportunistic exploitation of any available vulnerability (script kiddies). Motivation predicts target selection: a nation-state pursuing economic espionage will target defense contractors and technology firms; ransomware groups target organizations with high recovery urgency (hospitals, municipalities). [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
Actor Type Internal / External Resources Sophistication
Nation-State / APT External Unlimited (state budget) Very high — zero-days, custom implants
Organized Crime External High (RaaS revenue) Medium-to-high — bought or leased tooling
Hacktivist External Variable Variable — from novice to expert
Insider Threat Internal Limited external but high internal access Variable — access advantage compensates
Script Kiddie External Minimal Low — tool-dependent
Shadow IT Internal origin Departmental budget N/A — unintentional risk

Attributes per SY0-701 Obj 2.1; named actor profiles documented in MITRE ATT&CK Groups. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1] [MITRE ATT&CK Groups]

Key Takeaway
  • Four attributes define actor risk: internal vs. external, resources/funding, sophistication/capability, intent/motivation. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
  • Nation-state APTs combine all four risk factors at maximum: external, unlimited funding, very high sophistication, and clear strategic intent. [MITRE ATT&CK Groups]
  • Insiders compensate for lower external resources with authorized access that bypasses all perimeter controls. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
  • MITRE ATT&CK Groups provides empirical, evidence-based technique profiles for named groups. [MITRE ATT&CK Groups]
Section 04

Threat Actor Motivations

SY0-701 Obj 2.1

Motivation is the why behind an attack. Understanding motivation allows defenders to predict target selection, likely attack types, and the adversary's willingness to invest resources. The Security+ SY0-701 exam tests several motivational categories, including financial gain, espionage, ideology, revenge, and others. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1] These motivations map directly to the attack patterns catalogued in MITRE ATT&CK, which organizes techniques by the goal (tactic) each technique serves. [MITRE ATT&CK]

Financial Gain
The most common motivation for organized crime. Includes ransomware (encrypt data and demand payment to restore access), business email compromise (fraud via impersonation), payment card theft, credential theft for resale, and cryptocurrency theft. CISA advisories frequently identify financially motivated criminal groups and their techniques. [CISA]
Espionage
Nation-state actors conduct espionage to collect intelligence on foreign governments, militaries, defense contractors, and technology firms. The goal is to gain strategic advantage without kinetic conflict. MITRE ATT&CK Groups documents espionage-focused APT groups and the specific collection and exfiltration techniques they employ. [MITRE ATT&CK Groups]
Data Exfiltration
The objective of stealing data for secondary use: competitive advantage (intellectual property theft), resale on underground markets (personal data, credentials), or as evidence in a public leak campaign. Data exfiltration is a goal shared across nation-state, criminal, and hacktivist actors, distinguishing only in what data they target and why. [MITRE ATT&CK]
Service Disruption
Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks, ransomware that destroys rather than encrypts, and destructive wiper malware are all aimed at disrupting operations. Disruption serves financial (extortion), political (hacktivists targeting government sites), and wartime (nation-state cyberweapons) goals. CISA advisories document nation-state actors that conduct destructive operations against critical infrastructure. [CISA]
Ideological / Political
Hacktivists attack organizations they view as opposing their political or social values. Targets are selected based on ideology rather than profitability. Tactics are chosen for maximum public impact: website defacement publicizes the message; leaked internal documents expose perceived wrongdoing. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
Revenge
Disgruntled employees or former contractors who feel wronged by an organization may act out of personal grievance. This is a primary insider threat motivation. The attack may be sabotage (deleting critical data), data theft (taking IP to a competitor), or credential abuse (continued access after termination). Revenge motivation often produces more unpredictable damage than calculated criminal attacks. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
Warfare / Geopolitical
Nation-state actors conduct offensive cyber operations as an element of national strategy. Cyberattacks can disable adversary infrastructure, support kinetic military operations, or achieve strategic effects without a formal declaration of war. The CISA advisory program tracks nation-state cyber activity against US critical infrastructure sectors. [CISA]
Ethical / Hacktivism
Some attackers frame their actions as ethical: exposing security vulnerabilities in the public interest ("responsible disclosure" when done properly, but sometimes conducted without permission), or attacking organizations for practices the attacker considers unethical. This motivation spans both individual researchers and organized hacktivist groups. The ethical framing does not change the legal status of unauthorized access. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]

Nation-State Motivations

  • Intelligence gathering (espionage)
  • Intellectual property theft
  • Critical infrastructure disruption
  • Geopolitical influence operations
  • Pre-positioning for armed conflict

Criminal Motivations

  • Direct financial gain (ransomware, fraud)
  • Data theft for resale
  • Credential harvesting
  • Extortion (DDoS for ransom)
  • Money laundering via cryptocurrency

Motivation categories per SY0-701 Obj 2.1; nation-state techniques per MITRE ATT&CK Groups and CISA advisories. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1] [MITRE ATT&CK Groups] [CISA]

Why Motivation Predicts Target Selection An actor motivated by financial gain targets organizations with data worth stealing or operations worth disrupting for ransom: healthcare, financial services, and municipalities with urgency to restore services. An espionage-motivated APT targets organizations with access to strategic secrets: defense contractors, research universities, and government agencies. Matching motivation to target type is a tested Security+ skill. [MITRE ATT&CK Groups] [CISA]
Key Takeaway
  • Motivations include: financial gain, espionage, data exfiltration, service disruption, ideological/political, revenge, warfare/geopolitical, ethical/hacktivism. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
  • Motivation predicts target selection and attack type. Nation-states pursue espionage and infrastructure disruption; criminal groups pursue financial gain. [MITRE ATT&CK Groups]
  • MITRE ATT&CK maps motivation to technique: the "Collection" and "Exfiltration" tactics target data; "Impact" tactics cover disruption and destruction. [MITRE ATT&CK]
Section 05

Threat Vectors and Attack Surfaces

SY0-701 Obj 2.1

A threat vector is the path or mechanism by which a threat actor gains unauthorized access to a system or network. RFC 4949 defines an attack as "an intentional act by which an entity attempts to evade security services and violate the security policy of a system." The attack surface is the sum of the different points where an unauthorized user can try to enter data to or extract data from an environment. [NIST Glossary] Reducing the attack surface is a core defensive principle. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]

MITRE ATT&CK organizes initial access techniques (the tactics by which an adversary first gains entry) and maps each technique to the specific threat vectors below. Understanding these vectors allows defenders to apply controls at each entry point. [MITRE ATT&CK]

Message-Based
Email phishing, spear-phishing, smishing (SMS), and social media messaging. Delivers malicious attachments, credential-harvesting links, or business email compromise attempts. The highest-volume initial access vector in MITRE ATT&CK data. [MITRE ATT&CK]
Image / File-Based
Malicious files disguised as legitimate documents or images: weaponized Office macros, PDF exploits, trojanized software installers, and image files embedding steganographic payloads. Exploits trust in familiar file types. [MITRE ATT&CK]
Voice
Vishing (voice phishing) over phone calls and AI-generated voice deepfake impersonation. Threat actors impersonate IT staff, executives, or vendors to manipulate targets into disclosing credentials or authorizing transfers. Social engineering that bypasses email filtering. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
Removable Media
USB drives, optical media, and other portable storage can carry malware directly into air-gapped networks and bypass perimeter defenses. Autorun exploits and BadUSB attacks reprogrammed USB firmware demonstrate the threat. MITRE ATT&CK documents replication through removable media as an established initial access technique. [MITRE ATT&CK]
Supply Chain
Compromising a trusted software vendor, hardware supplier, or managed service provider to gain access to all of their customers simultaneously. A single supply chain compromise can provide initial access to thousands of targets. NIST SP 800-150 discusses supply chain risks in the context of threat intelligence sharing across sectors. [NIST SP 800-150]
Social Engineering
Psychological manipulation of individuals to disclose information or take actions that benefit the attacker. Includes pretexting (fabricated scenario), baiting (leaving infected media), quid pro quo (offering help in exchange for credentials), and tailgating (physical access). Social engineering exploits human behavior, not technical vulnerabilities. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
Supply Chain Attacks: Multiplied Impact A supply chain attack targets a trusted third party rather than the intended victim directly. Compromising a widely deployed software update mechanism or a managed service provider's remote administration tools provides simultaneous initial access to all downstream customers. NIST SP 800-150 addresses threat information sharing across supply chain partners as a mechanism to detect these threats early. [NIST SP 800-150]
Attack Surface vs. Attack Vector RFC 4949 defines the term attack; the NIST CSRC Glossary defines attack surface. An attack surface is the set of points where an adversary can attempt entry. A threat vector is the specific path used in an actual or potential attack. Reducing attack surface (removing unnecessary services, closing unused ports, deprovisioning accounts) reduces the vectors available to an adversary even before any specific attack occurs. [NIST Glossary] [RFC 4949]
Key Takeaway
  • Six primary threat vectors on SY0-701: message-based, image/file, voice, removable media, supply chain, social engineering. [SY0-701 Obj 2.1]
  • Attack surface = total exposure; reducing services, accounts, and open ports shrinks it. [RFC 4949]
  • Supply chain attacks multiply impact by targeting a trusted provider once to reach all their customers. [NIST SP 800-150]
  • MITRE ATT&CK Initial Access tactic documents the specific techniques used through each vector with real-world examples. [MITRE ATT&CK]
Section 06

Threat Intelligence: Sources, Sharing, and Formats

SY0-701 Obj 2.1

Threat intelligence is analyzed information about the capabilities, intentions, and activities of threat actors, collected and processed to support decision making. Raw threat data becomes intelligence only when it has been analyzed for timeliness, accuracy, and relevance to a specific organization. [NIST SP 800-150]

NIST SP 800-150 "Guide to Cyber Threat Information Sharing" provides the federal framework for collecting, analyzing, and sharing cyber threat information between organizations. It defines the purposes of threat intelligence sharing, the roles of participants (producers, consumers, and coordinators), and the technical standards that enable automated sharing. [NIST SP 800-150]

Open-Source Intelligence (OSINT)
Intelligence collected from publicly available sources: security researcher blogs, vulnerability databases (NVD/CVE), government advisories, dark web monitoring, and social media. OSINT is free to collect but requires analysis to separate signal from noise. NIST SP 800-150 describes OSINT as a foundational source category within the threat intelligence sharing ecosystem. [NIST SP 800-150]
Information Sharing and Analysis Centers (ISACs) and ISAOs
Sector-specific organizations that aggregate, analyze, and distribute threat intelligence to member organizations within a critical infrastructure sector (Financial Services ISAC, Health-ISAC, Energy ISAC, etc.). ISACs provide sector-specific context that general feeds lack. Information Sharing and Analysis Organizations (ISAOs) serve non-sector-specific communities. NIST SP 800-150 describes the ISAC/ISAO model as a primary mechanism for operationalizing threat intelligence sharing. [NIST SP 800-150]
STIX (Structured Threat Information eXpression)
A standardized language for representing cyber threat intelligence in a machine-readable JSON format. STIX objects include threat actors, attack patterns, malware, indicators, campaigns, and relationships between them. NIST SP 800-150 identifies STIX as a primary format for structured threat intelligence sharing. [NIST SP 800-150]
TAXII (Trusted Automated eXchange of Intelligence Information)
The transport protocol for distributing STIX content. TAXII defines how threat intelligence is shared over HTTPS using a server/client model with two service types: Collections (query-based retrieval) and Channels (publish/subscribe). NIST SP 800-150 covers TAXII alongside STIX as the technical infrastructure for automated threat intelligence exchange. [NIST SP 800-150]
CISA Advisories and Government Feeds
The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) publishes joint cybersecurity advisories, alerts, and threat reports on nation-state activity, critical vulnerability exploitation, and critical infrastructure threats. CISA advisories are primary-source government intelligence distributed to critical infrastructure operators. They are a key OSINT source for Security+ candidates and practitioners. [CISA]
MITRE ATT&CK as a Threat Intelligence Framework
MITRE ATT&CK is both a knowledge base and a structured framework for consuming threat intelligence. When a CISA advisory or STIX feed references an ATT&CK technique ID (e.g., T1566 for Phishing), defenders can immediately look up defensive countermeasures in ATT&CK's mitigations and detections sections. ATT&CK Groups provides actor-level intelligence. [MITRE ATT&CK] [MITRE ATT&CK Groups]

NIST SP 800-150 establishes three criteria for assessing the quality of threat intelligence: timeliness (a feed operating on delay may cause you to miss a threat or react after it is no longer relevant); accuracy (whether the information can be relied upon, based on source reliability and cross-referencing); and relevance (whether the threat applies to your organization's specific platforms, software, and risk environment). Data that is timely and accurate but describes threats to unrelated platforms provides no defensive value. [NIST SP 800-150]

STIX + TAXII + ATT&CK: The Automation Stack STIX provides the format; TAXII provides the transport; MITRE ATT&CK provides the common taxonomy. A threat feed published in STIX format over a TAXII server can be consumed by a SIEM or SOAR platform automatically, enriching security events with actor attribution, technique context, and recommended mitigations. NIST SP 800-150 describes this automation stack as the target state for mature threat intelligence programs. [NIST SP 800-150] [MITRE ATT&CK]
Key Takeaway
  • Threat intelligence types: OSINT (public), ISACs/ISAOs (sector-specific sharing), government feeds (CISA advisories), proprietary/vendor feeds. [NIST SP 800-150]
  • STIX is the structured format; TAXII is the transport protocol. Together they enable automated machine-readable threat intelligence exchange. [NIST SP 800-150]
  • Quality criteria (SP 800-150): timely, accurate, relevant. All three must be met for intelligence to be actionable. [NIST SP 800-150]
  • MITRE ATT&CK links threat intelligence feeds to defensive mitigations through a shared technique taxonomy. [MITRE ATT&CK]
Section 07

Indicators of Compromise (IoCs)

SY0-701 Obj 2.1

An Indicator of Compromise (IoC) is a piece of forensic evidence that suggests a system or network may have been breached. IoCs are the observable artifacts left behind by threat actor activity. They are the data points that threat intelligence feeds distribute and that security monitoring tools search for. [NIST SP 800-61r3]

NIST SP 800-61r3 "Incident Response Recommendations" establishes the framework for identifying, collecting, and acting on indicators during the detection and analysis phase of incident response. Precursors (signs that an attack may occur) and indicators (signs that an attack has occurred or is in progress) are the two categories described. IoCs fall into the indicator category. [NIST SP 800-61r3]

MITRE ATT&CK links IoC types to specific techniques, enabling defenders to move from a raw IoC to actor attribution and defensive countermeasures. STIX bundles IoCs as structured threat intelligence objects for automated distribution across TAXII channels. [MITRE ATT&CK] [NIST SP 800-150]

Network-Based IoCs
Malicious IP addresses communicating with a compromised host; domain names used for command-and-control (C2) infrastructure; unusual outbound traffic volume or direction (data exfiltration); DNS queries for newly registered or algorithmically generated domains; unexpected protocol usage on non-standard ports. These are distributed in threat feeds as IP reputation lists and domain blocklists. [NIST SP 800-61r3]
File-Based IoCs
Cryptographic hash values (MD5, SHA-256) of known malware samples; malicious file names or paths; file sizes that match known malware; registry keys created by malware; and scheduled tasks or services installed by an attacker. Hash-based IoCs are the most precise: a matching hash uniquely identifies a specific file binary. [NIST SP 800-61r3]
Behavioral IoCs
Patterns of activity that deviate from established baselines: credential access at unusual hours, lateral movement between systems with no business justification, privilege escalation sequences, large volume of failed authentication attempts preceding a successful login, and unusual process execution chains (e.g., a Word process spawning a command shell). MITRE ATT&CK maps these behaviors to specific techniques with detection guidance. [MITRE ATT&CK]
Email-Based IoCs
Sender domain spoofing mismatches between display name and actual sending address; known-malicious sending IPs; email subjects and body patterns matching active phishing campaigns; malicious attachment hashes; and embedded URLs resolving to credential-harvesting sites. Phishing IoCs are among the most actively distributed in threat intelligence feeds due to the high volume of message-based attacks. [NIST SP 800-61r3]
IoC Limitations: The Pyramid of Pain Not all IoCs are equally difficult for attackers to change. Specific hash values are trivial to alter (recompile the malware); IP addresses are easy to rotate; domain names require slightly more effort; tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) are the hardest to change because they reflect how the actor operates. MITRE ATT&CK's technique-level intelligence targets TTPs, making it more durable than hash or IP-based detection. [MITRE ATT&CK] [NIST SP 800-150]
SP 800-61r3: Detecting and Analyzing Indicators NIST SP 800-61r3 frames incident response around the NIST Cybersecurity Framework and emphasizes detecting and analyzing incidents through indicators. In practice, the quality of IoC detection depends on the logging and baseline measurement an organization establishes before an incident occurs. [NIST SP 800-61r3]
Key Takeaway
  • IoCs are observable artifacts of compromise: network IoCs (malicious IPs/domains), file IoCs (hashes, malicious paths), behavioral IoCs (unusual activity patterns), email IoCs (phishing signatures). [NIST SP 800-61r3]
  • NIST SP 800-61r3 governs IoC collection and use during incident detection and analysis. [NIST SP 800-61r3]
  • STIX bundles IoCs for automated distribution; TAXII distributes them to consuming organizations. [NIST SP 800-150]
  • TTP-level indicators (MITRE ATT&CK techniques) are more durable defenses than hash or IP-based IoCs because TTPs are harder for adversaries to change. [MITRE ATT&CK]

Actor Types Cheat Sheet
  • Nation-State / APT External, state-funded, very high sophistication, espionage / warfare
  • Organized Crime External, high resources, financially motivated, RaaS
  • Hacktivist External, variable skills, ideological / political
  • Insider Threat Internal, authorized access bypasses perimeter, revenge / espionage
  • Script Kiddie External, low skill, public tools, opportunistic
  • Shadow IT Internal origin, unintentional risk, unmanaged systems
Threat Intel Quick Ref
  • STIX Structured format (JSON) for sharing IoCs and threat actor data
  • TAXII Transport protocol (HTTPS) for delivering STIX content
  • ISAC Sector-specific threat sharing organization
  • OSINT Publicly available threat data; free but requires analysis
