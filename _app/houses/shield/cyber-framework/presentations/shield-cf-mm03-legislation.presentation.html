Major Cybersecurity Legislation: CFAA & Data Breach

Understanding the legal landscape of cybercrime and data protection — the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, its judicial interpretations, and the patchwork of state breach notification laws that govern incident response obligations.

Slide 1 — Cybersecurity Law is a "Patchwork"
US CYBER LAW SOURCES — NO SINGLE COMPREHENSIVE CODE EXISTS
US Constitution (4th/5th Amend.) CFAA (18 USC 1030) HIPAA / HITECH COPPA GLBA ECPA SCA 50 State Breach Laws CCPA / CPRA (California) SHIELD Act (New York) CPA (Colorado) Common Law Negligence Contract Law GDPR (EU) UN Charter Budapest Convention

Why No Comprehensive Federal Cybersecurity Law?

The US has historically resisted European-style omnibus data protection legislation. Multiple comprehensive cybersecurity bills (including the American Data Privacy and Protection Act, ADPPA) have failed to advance past committee due to disagreements over preemption of state laws, private rights of action, and scope of coverage.

The result is a sector-specific, multi-layered system where compliance obligations depend entirely on your industry, the type of data you handle, where your customers are located, and what type of incident occurred. A single healthcare company may simultaneously be subject to HIPAA (federal), state breach notification (varies by victim's state of residence), FTC Section 5 (if they collect consumer data), and CCPA (if any California residents are affected).

PROFESSOR'S NOTE

This patchwork structure is not accidental — it reflects American federalism and the political power of industries that prefer sector-specific rules they can influence over comprehensive legislation they cannot. Understanding the patchwork is a competitive advantage for practitioners: attorneys, CISOs, and compliance officers who can navigate multiple overlapping frameworks are exceptionally valuable.

Slide 2 — Sources of Cybersecurity Law

US Constitution

4th Amendment: unreasonable searches and seizures — digital privacy implications. 5th Amendment: self-incrimination — compelled password disclosure. 1st Amendment: speech implications of cybercrime laws and content moderation.

Federal Statutes

CFAA (computer crimes), HIPAA (healthcare), COPPA (children's privacy), GLBA (financial), ECPA (electronic communications), SCA (stored communications), CAN-SPAM, and dozens of sector-specific laws.

Federal Regulations

Agency-issued rules implementing statutory authority: FTC Safeguards Rule, HHS HIPAA Security Rule, SEC cybersecurity disclosure rules, FERC CIP standards, NIST standards incorporated by reference.

State Statutes

All 50 states have breach notification laws. Many states (CA, NY, CO, VA, TX) have comprehensive privacy laws. State computer crime statutes often duplicate or extend CFAA protections at the state level.

Common Law

Negligence (duty to implement reasonable security), breach of contract (failure to protect data per agreement), unjust enrichment, and emerging cyber-specific torts being developed through litigation.

International Law

GDPR (binding on organizations processing EU resident data regardless of location), Budapest Convention on Cybercrime, UN Charter (applies to state-sponsored cyber operations), bilateral mutual legal assistance treaties (MLATs).

Slide 3 — Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA): 18 USC 1030

America's Primary Federal Computer Crime Statute

The CFAA was enacted in 1986, originally as a narrow law targeting hackers accessing government computers. It has been significantly expanded six times — in 1994, 1996, 2001 (USA PATRIOT Act), 2002, 2008, and proposed but not enacted further expansions. Today it is the primary federal vehicle for prosecuting computer crimes AND a frequently used civil litigation tool.

1986 Year enacted — originally targeted only government computer access
7 Key subsections criminalizing different types of computer-related conduct
10yr Maximum prison sentence for many CFAA violations (20yr for repeat offenses)

The Seven Key Subsections of CFAA (18 USC 1030)

Subsection Crime Description
1030(a)(1) National Security Accessing a protected computer to obtain classified national defense or foreign relations information with intent to harm the US or aid a foreign power.
1030(a)(2) Unauthorized Information Access Intentionally accessing a computer without authorization or exceeding authorized access and obtaining information from financial records, US government computers, or any protected computer (if interstate).
1030(a)(3) Government Computer Trespass Intentionally accessing without authorization any nonpublic computer of a US department or agency.
1030(a)(4) Computer Fraud Knowingly accessing a protected computer with intent to defraud, and by means of such access furthering the intended fraud and obtaining something of value.
1030(a)(5) Intentional Damage Transmitting a program, code, or command that intentionally causes damage (malware deployment, ransomware). Causing reckless or negligent damage to protected computers.
1030(a)(6) Password Trafficking Knowingly and with intent to defraud trafficking in passwords or similar access information that allows unauthorized computer access.
1030(a)(7) Cyber Extortion Threatening to damage a protected computer, steal data, or reveal confidential information obtained from unauthorized access to extort money or other things of value.
Slide 4 — The Central CFAA Question: "Without Authorization"

What Does "Without Authorization" Actually Mean?

The CFAA criminalizes accessing a computer "without authorization" or in a manner that "exceeds authorized access." These terms are defined in the statute but their scope has generated decades of litigation. The interpretation significantly impacts what cybersecurity research, employment activities, and technical operations are legal.

Broad Interpretation (Pre-Van Buren)

  • Any violation of a website's Terms of Service = unauthorized access
  • Using employer's computer for personal use could be federal crime
  • Accessing data for an impermissible purpose = exceeds authorization
  • Chilling effect on security research — automated scraping could be criminal
  • DOJ pursued Aaron Swartz under this theory (JSTOR access)

Narrow Interpretation (Post-Van Buren 2021)

  • Only code-based access restrictions count — password-protected, permission-gated
  • Purpose-based restrictions do NOT create CFAA liability
  • Employee using authorized access for bad purpose = policy violation, not CFAA
  • ToS violations cannot create federal criminal liability
  • Security researchers benefit from clearer safe harbor for authorized-access research
VAN BUREN v. UNITED STATES (2021) — SUPREME COURT

Van Buren, a Georgia police officer, ran a license plate search through a law enforcement database in exchange for money — a purpose-based restriction violation. He was convicted under CFAA's "exceeds authorized access" provision. The Supreme Court reversed 6-3, adopting the "gates-up-or-down" inquiry: the CFAA covers circumvention of code-based access barriers (like passwords and permissions), not violations of purpose-based restrictions on how authorized users may use information. Justice Barrett wrote the majority opinion. This decision was celebrated by security researchers and civil liberties advocates who feared the broad reading was turning millions of Americans into federal criminals.

Slide 5 — CFAA in Practice: Notable Cases
2001
US v. Auernheimer ("weev")
AT&T iPad subscriber data (114,000 emails) was accessible by anyone who entered a valid ICC-ID into a publicly accessible API. Auernheimer harvested data using the public API and was convicted under CFAA. The 3rd Circuit reversed on venue grounds. Case highlighted the tension between "any access to information that causes embarrassment" and actual unauthorized access — AT&T's API had no authentication.
2013
US v. Aaron Swartz
MIT graduate student and internet activist Aaron Swartz used MIT's guest network (authorized) to mass-download JSTOR academic articles. DOJ charged him with 13 felony counts under CFAA with a potential 35-year sentence. Swartz died by suicide before trial. His case became a rallying point for CFAA reform — "Aaron's Law" was introduced but never passed. Illustrates prosecutorial overreach concerns with the statute.
2019
hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn
LinkedIn attempted to use CFAA to stop hiQ Labs from scraping publicly visible LinkedIn profiles. 9th Circuit held that scraping publicly accessible data does not violate CFAA — there is no "authorization" requirement for publicly accessible information. After Van Buren, the case was remanded and LinkedIn's CFAA claims weakened further. Key precedent for web scraping legality.
2022
DOJ Revised CFAA Charging Policy
Post-Van Buren, DOJ revised its CFAA charging policy to explicitly exclude prosecutions based solely on Terms of Service violations or accessing information beyond what was authorized by purpose. DOJ also stated it would not prosecute "good-faith security research" — a formal policy shift that provided long-sought clarity for penetration testers and vulnerability researchers operating with proper authorization.
Slide 6 — State Data Breach Notification Laws

All 50 States Have Enacted Breach Notification Laws

In the absence of a comprehensive federal breach notification law, states have filled the gap — creating a patchwork of 50+ different legal regimes. Organizations experiencing a breach affecting residents of multiple states must simultaneously comply with each applicable state law, which may have different definitions, timelines, trigger thresholds, and notification requirements.

FIRST

California (2003)

California enacted the nation's first data breach notification law (SB 1386). It defined personal information, established notification requirements, and served as the model for all subsequent state laws. California has since expanded it significantly under CCPA/CPRA.

LAST

Alabama (2018)

Alabama was the final state to enact a breach notification law — the Alabama Data Breach Notification Act of 2018. It took 15 years from California's first law for all 50 states to establish mandatory breach notification requirements.

NOTABLE

New York SHIELD Act (2019)

Expanded definition of private information to include biometrics, username/email with passwords, and account numbers with security codes. Added reasonable security safeguards requirement — not just notification obligations. Extraterritorial reach: applies to any company with data on NY residents.

NOTABLE

California CCPA/CPRA

Most comprehensive US state privacy law. Grants consumers rights to know, delete, correct, and opt-out of sale of personal information. Creates private right of action for data breaches. CPRA (2023) added California Privacy Protection Agency with enforcement authority.

NOTABLE

Colorado CPA (2023)

Colorado Privacy Act established comprehensive consumer rights, required data protection assessments for high-risk processing, and included an opt-out right for targeted advertising. No private right of action — AG enforcement only. One of several comprehensive state laws following CCPA's lead.

TIMING

Notification Timelines Vary

Most states require notification within 30-60 days of discovery. Some states are faster: Florida requires 30 days, Delaware requires 60 days. A company with customers in all 50 states must comply with the most restrictive timeline applicable to each affected resident.

Slide 7 — Breach Notification Law: Core Components

What Every State Law Must Address

While the specific requirements vary by state, all breach notification laws share a common structure of core components. Understanding these components allows practitioners to analyze any state law, compare requirements, and design a notification program that satisfies the most demanding jurisdiction applicable to their business.

Definition of Personal Information

What data types trigger notification obligations. Most states start with SSN, driver's license, financial account numbers. Newer laws add medical info, biometrics, credentials, geolocation, and more. The definition scope determines whether a breach triggers notification at all.

⛏
Harm Threshold

Many states only require notification if the breach is "reasonably likely to cause harm." Others require notification for any unauthorized access to covered data regardless of harm. The threshold dramatically affects the notification burden — more breaches qualify under threshold-free states.

Encryption Safe Harbor

If stolen data was encrypted and the encryption key was not also compromised, most states exempt the incident from notification requirements. This creates a direct incentive for encryption — a policy design choice that rewards security investment.

Law Enforcement Delay Exception

Nearly all states allow notification to be delayed at the request of law enforcement if notification would impede a criminal investigation. FBI may request delayed notification while investigating to preserve the ability to catch attackers. Delay is temporary — typically 30-60 days maximum.

Who Must Be Notified

Most states require: (1) affected individuals, (2) state Attorney General, and (3) consumer reporting agencies if more than a threshold number of residents are affected (e.g., 500+ in California). Regulators (HHS, SEC) may have additional parallel notification requirements.

📸
How Notification is Delivered

Written notice (mail or email), telephone notice for urgent situations, and substitute notice (prominent website posting, statewide media) when contacting all individuals would cost over a defined threshold (typically $250,000) or reach over 500,000 people.

COMPLIANCE STRATEGY

For multi-state organizations, the practical approach is to design your breach notification program around the most stringent requirements — shortest timeline, broadest definition of personal information, lowest harm threshold — and apply those standards uniformly. This reduces the complexity of state-by-state analysis during the crisis of an active breach response, when legal counsel and communications teams are already under extreme pressure.

Slide 8 — The Federal Preemption Debate

Why There Is Still No Federal Breach Notification Law

The American Data Privacy and Protection Act (ADPPA), introduced in 2022, was the closest Congress has come to a comprehensive federal privacy and breach notification law. It passed committee with bipartisan support — then stalled over a single issue: preemption of California's CCPA/CPRA.

Arguments for Federal Preemption

  • Businesses would face one law instead of 50+ — reduced compliance cost
  • Uniform consumer protections regardless of state of residence
  • Prevents most-restrictive-state de facto setting national policy
  • Enables consistent enforcement and clearer liability rules

Arguments Against Preemption

  • Federal floor may be weaker than states like California have already achieved
  • States serve as "laboratories of democracy" for privacy innovation
  • California voters approved CPRA directly — preemption overrides direct democracy
  • Federal enforcement capacity may be less robust than state AGs
CLASS DISCUSSION

Should Congress enact a federal data breach notification law that preempts state laws?

Consider: Who benefits from uniformity? Who benefits from strong state laws? What role should California — with 40 million residents and the world's 5th largest economy — play in setting de facto national standards? And does the patchwork actually protect consumers, or does it protect industries that prefer complexity over accountability?
