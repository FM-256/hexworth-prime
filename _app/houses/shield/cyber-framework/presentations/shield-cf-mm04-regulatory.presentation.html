Major Regulatory Frameworks

Navigating the sector-specific regulatory landscape — FTC authority, HIPAA Security Rule, financial sector compliance (GLBA, SEC), and the emerging New York cybersecurity requirements that are reshaping enterprise security standards.

Slide 1 — The US Regulatory Environment

Sector-Specific Regulation: No Uniform Federal Standard

Unlike the European Union's GDPR — which applies a single, comprehensive data protection framework to all industries — the United States regulates cybersecurity through a patchwork of sector-specific laws and agency-specific regulations. What applies to your organization depends almost entirely on what industry you operate in.

This system creates both gaps (industries with minimal regulatory oversight) and overlap (organizations like hospitals that accept credit cards and employ contractors simultaneously subject to HIPAA, PCI DSS, GLBA if they offer patient financing, and state laws). Compliance is not security, but regulators increasingly set the floor that defines legally adequate security practice.

The Sector Patchwork

Sector Primary Regulator(s) Key Law / Rule Security Focus
Consumer (General) FTC FTC Act Section 5 Unfair/deceptive security practices; de facto baseline for all consumer-facing companies
Healthcare HHS / OCR HIPAA Security Rule Administrative, technical, and physical safeguards for ePHI; breach notification within 60 days
Financial Services SEC / CFTC / CFPB / OCC GLBA Safeguards Rule Written information security program, qualified individual, annual reporting to board, MFA, encryption
Telecommunications FCC CPNI Rules / CALEA Customer data protection, lawful intercept capability, routing security (BGP), submarine cable
Energy FERC / NERC CIP Standards Critical Infrastructure Protection (CIP) reliability standards for electric utilities — mandatory with penalties
Transportation DOT / FAA / TSA TSA Pipeline Directives Aviation system security, pipeline cybersecurity directives (post-Colonial), railway security

PRACTITIONER'S NOTE

Organizations operating in multiple sectors face overlapping obligations. A university medical center that accepts payments, conducts federally funded research, and employs students may simultaneously be subject to HIPAA, PCI DSS, FERPA, FISMA, and state laws. The CISO's job is not to satisfy each separately but to design a unified security program that satisfies the most stringent requirement in each category — documented, auditable, and defensible.

Slide 2 — FTC Section 5: The De Facto Consumer Cybersecurity Regulator

Authority Through "Unfair and Deceptive Practices"

The Federal Trade Commission's primary enforcement tool is Section 5 of the FTC Act, which prohibits "unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce." The FTC has no specific statutory cybersecurity authority — but it has used Section 5 creatively to become the primary federal regulator of consumer data security for industries not covered by sector-specific laws.

"Deceptive" Practice

A company makes a material representation, omission, or practice that is likely to mislead consumers who are acting reasonably. Applied to cybersecurity: claiming "bank-level security" when basic controls are absent. Promising your privacy policy is followed when it is not. Publishing a security program that exists only on paper.

Key: The deception must be material to consumers' decisions — they would make different choices if they knew the truth about security practices.

"Unfair" Practice

A practice that causes or is likely to cause substantial injury to consumers that is not reasonably avoidable and is not outweighed by countervailing benefits. Applied to cybersecurity: failure to implement basic, widely-known security measures when handling sensitive consumer data is an "unfair practice" even if no explicit deception occurred.

Key: Does not require a false statement. FTC can pursue companies for security failures even if they made no security claims at all.

Slide 3 — FTC Cybersecurity Enforcement: The Case History
2001
In re Eli Lilly & Company — The First Cybersecurity Case
Eli Lilly's Prozac email list was accidentally exposed, revealing subscribers' mental health status to all list members. FTC settled: first consent decree establishing that inadequate data security can be a deceptive practice. Established the precedent that security failures carry FTC consequences even for pharmaceutical companies outside traditional FTC purview.
2012
FTC v. Wyndham Hotels — Critical Authority Case
Wyndham Hotels suffered three data breaches (2008-2010) exposing 619,000 payment card records, causing $10.6M in fraudulent charges. FTC sued under Section 5. Wyndham challenged FTC's authority to regulate data security. The Third Circuit (2015) affirmed FTC's authority — held that unreasonable cybersecurity practices can constitute unfair practices. This was the pivotal case that cemented FTC's role as consumer cybersecurity regulator.
2019
FTC v. Facebook — $5 Billion Settlement
Following Cambridge Analytica scandal and violations of a 2012 FTC consent order, Facebook paid the largest privacy settlement in FTC history ($5B). Also imposed new corporate governance requirements including a privacy committee, quarterly compliance certifications from CEO, and independent privacy audits. Demonstrated FTC's willingness to use repeat violation settlements to impose structural oversight on Big Tech.
2023
FTC Safeguards Rule — Updated GLBA Rule
FTC's amended Safeguards Rule (implementing GLBA for non-bank financial institutions) expanded covered entities, added specific technical requirements (MFA, encryption, penetration testing, access controls), and required companies to report breaches affecting 500+ customers within 30 days. Demonstrates the FTC's shift from principles-based to more prescriptive requirements.
THE FTC TOOL KIT

FTC enforcement creates consent decrees that function as regulatory mandates — requiring security programs, independent audits, employee training, and board reporting for 20 years. FTC cannot impose civil penalties for first-offense Section 5 violations, but repeat violations (breaking a consent decree) can generate $50,000+ per day in penalties. This structure makes consent decrees extraordinarily powerful compliance drivers — and makes violating them catastrophically expensive.

Slide 4 — HIPAA Security Rule: Healthcare Cybersecurity

Protecting Electronic Protected Health Information (ePHI)

HIPAA's Security Rule (45 CFR Part 164) establishes national standards for protecting electronic protected health information (ePHI). It applies to covered entities (healthcare providers, health plans, clearinghouses) and their business associates — any third party that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits ePHI on behalf of a covered entity.

Administrative Safeguards

Security management process, assigned security responsibility, workforce training, contingency plan, periodic evaluation. The "people and process" pillar — documented policies, risk analysis, workforce sanctions.

Technical Safeguards

Access controls, audit controls, integrity controls, transmission security (encryption). Controls that protect ePHI in information systems — authentication, automatic logoff, encrypted transmission of ePHI over public networks.

Physical Safeguards

Facility access controls, workstation use policies, workstation security, device and media controls. Physical protections for systems containing ePHI — locked server rooms, clean desk policy, secure device disposal.

HIPAA Civil Penalties — Tiered Based on Culpability

Tier 1 $100 Did not know / could not have known — per violation, up to $25K/year
Tier 2 $1K Reasonable cause — per violation, up to $100K/year
Tier 3 $10K Willful neglect, corrected — per violation, up to $250K/year
Tier 4 $50K Willful neglect, uncorrected — per violation, up to $1.9M/year
Slide 5 — Proposed 2024 HIPAA Security Rule Updates

HHS Proposed Overhaul — Most Significant Update Since 2013

In December 2024, HHS/OCR published a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM) for sweeping updates to the HIPAA Security Rule — the most significant proposed changes since the HITECH Act modifications in 2013. The proposal would make many currently "addressable" requirements mandatory.

✓
Mandatory Asset Inventory

Covered entities would be required to maintain a comprehensive inventory of all technology assets that access ePHI — hardware, software, cloud services. Currently optional under existing risk analysis requirements; proposed rule makes it explicit and mandatory.

✓
Enhanced Risk Analysis Requirements

More prescriptive risk analysis requirements specifying what the analysis must include, how often it must be performed (at least annually and after significant changes), and how findings must be documented and tracked to remediation.

✓
Mandatory Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)

MFA would be required for all access to ePHI — moving from "addressable" to "required." With limited exceptions for emergency access procedures, all workforce members would need MFA to access systems containing protected health information.

✓
Mandatory Penetration Testing

Annual penetration testing and vulnerability scanning would be explicitly required — not just implied through the general risk analysis requirement. Healthcare organizations would need to engage qualified testers and remediate identified vulnerabilities within defined timeframes.

✓
72-Hour Incident Response Restoration

Covered entities would be required to restore critical systems within 72 hours of a ransomware or other disruptive incident — pushing healthcare organizations toward more mature business continuity and disaster recovery capabilities aligned with actual clinical patient care needs.

CLINICAL REALITY

Healthcare's regulatory burden is particularly acute because the consequences of cybersecurity failure are not merely financial — they are clinical. The 2024 Change Healthcare ransomware attack disrupted prescription processing for 90% of US pharmacies and was directly linked to delayed patient care. When HIPAA requirements are framed around patient safety rather than compliance, the conversation changes in boardrooms and with legislators. The 2024 NPRM reflects this shift toward clinically-grounded security requirements.

Slide 6 — GLBA Safeguards Rule: Financial Sector Security

Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act — Financial Institution Security Requirements

The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (1999) requires financial institutions to protect customer financial information. The FTC's Safeguards Rule implements GLBA for non-bank financial institutions (mortgage brokers, auto dealers, tax preparers, financial advisors) — entities not primarily regulated by banking regulators. The rule was significantly strengthened in 2023.

Written Information Security Program (WISP)

Financial institutions must develop, implement, and maintain a comprehensive written information security program that contains administrative, technical, and physical safeguards appropriate to the size, complexity, nature, and scope of the institution's activities.

Qualified Individual (QI)

The 2023 update requires designation of a single qualified individual responsible for overseeing the security program — effectively mandating a CISO or equivalent function. This individual must report to the board (or equivalent) at least annually on the security program's status and material risks.

Risk Assessment

Periodic risk assessments that identify reasonably foreseeable internal and external risks to customer information, evaluate safeguards, and design a risk management plan. Must be documented, updated when circumstances change, and used to drive security investment decisions.

Multi-Factor Authentication

The 2023 Safeguards Rule explicitly requires MFA for any individual accessing any information system, with an exception process for alternative controls that must be documented and approved by the qualified individual. MFA went from best practice to legal requirement.

Encryption Requirements

Customer information must be encrypted both in transit (over external networks) and at rest. Encryption must use current cryptographic standards — organizations cannot rely on deprecated algorithms. Applies to all covered information regardless of format.

Incident Response Plan

Written incident response plan with specific components: goals, internal processes for responding, roles and decision-making authority, communication and information sharing plan, identification of vulnerable systems, procedures for eradicating threats, and documentation/reporting requirements.

Service Provider Oversight

Financial institutions must select and retain service providers that maintain appropriate safeguards, require service providers by contract to implement and maintain appropriate safeguards, and periodically monitor the security of those providers — extending the security program into the supply chain.

Breach Notification (500+)

The 2023 update added a 30-day breach notification requirement to the FTC for breaches affecting 500 or more customers — distinct from the underlying GLBA privacy notice requirement. Creates a parallel obligation to SEC reporting for public companies, state AG notifications, and banking regulator notifications.

Slide 7 — SEC Cybersecurity Disclosure Rules (2023)
SECURITIES AND EXCHANGE COMMISSION — EFFECTIVE DECEMBER 2023

Public companies now have binding cybersecurity disclosure obligations to investors

The SEC's new cybersecurity disclosure rules fundamentally change how public companies must treat cybersecurity as a material business risk — requiring both timely incident disclosure and ongoing governance transparency. The rules apply to all SEC reporting companies (domestic and foreign private issuers).

Form 8-K: Material Incident Disclosure

Public companies must disclose a material cybersecurity incident within 4 business days of determining that the incident is material. "Material" follows the traditional securities law standard: information that a reasonable investor would consider important in making an investment decision.

Required disclosures: nature, scope, and timing of the incident; material impact or reasonably likely material impact on the company. A 30-day delay is available if the US Attorney General determines disclosure would pose a substantial risk to national security or public safety.

Annual Report (10-K): Governance Disclosure

Companies must annually disclose: (1) processes for identifying, assessing, and managing material cybersecurity risks; (2) whether and how cybersecurity risks have materially affected or are reasonably likely to affect business strategy, financial condition, or results; (3) board oversight of cybersecurity risk; (4) management's role and expertise in cybersecurity risk management.

Board cyber expertise: companies must disclose whether any board member has cybersecurity expertise — and if so, describe that expertise. This is not a requirement to have expertise, only to disclose its presence or absence.

MATERIALITY JUDGMENT RISK

The SEC rules create a critical decision-making challenge: CISOs and legal teams must make a materiality determination within 4 business days of discovery — often while the incident is still unfolding and full scope is unknown. Getting it wrong in either direction creates liability: too slow triggers SEC enforcement, too fast with inaccurate information triggers securities fraud exposure. SolarWinds Corporation and its CISO were personally charged by the SEC in 2023 for allegedly misleading investors about cybersecurity practices — the first SEC enforcement action against an individual CISO. This changed the risk calculus for every CISO at a public company.

Slide 8 — New York DFS Cybersecurity Regulation (23 NYCRR 500)

The Gold Standard for State Financial Cybersecurity Regulation

The New York Department of Financial Services (NYDFS) Cybersecurity Regulation, first effective March 2017 and significantly amended in November 2023, applies to all financial services companies licensed under New York law — banks, insurance companies, mortgage servicers, and more. It is widely considered the most comprehensive and prescriptive state-level cybersecurity regulation in the US.

Class A Companies — Enhanced Requirements

Companies with 2,000+ employees, $1B+ in gross annual revenue, or $20B+ in year-end total assets face additional "Class A" requirements: independent audit of the cybersecurity program, privileged access management (PAM) controls, automated controls for protecting nonpublic information, and vulnerability scanning with penetration testing annually.

72-Hour Breach Notification to NYDFS

Covered entities must notify NYDFS within 72 hours of determining that a cybersecurity event has occurred — one of the strictest notification timelines in US law. The 72-hour clock applies to NYDFS notification, distinct from consumer notification requirements under New York's SHIELD Act (which has a "most expedient time" standard).

CISO Requirement

All covered entities must designate a qualified Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) responsible for overseeing and implementing the cybersecurity program, reporting to the board at least annually. Small entities with limited staff may use an affiliate or external party as CISO, but accountability must be documented.

Annual Certification

Senior executives must annually certify compliance with the regulation — creating direct personal accountability at the C-suite level. False certifications expose executives to both regulatory penalties and potential personal liability. This accountability structure has driven board-level engagement with cybersecurity across New York's financial sector.

CLASS DISCUSSION

Should New York's DFS regulation become the national standard for all financial institutions?

NYDFS's prescriptive approach — specific technical requirements, CISO mandate, board certification, Class A enhanced standards — contrasts sharply with the principles-based NIST CSF and the flexible GLBA Safeguards Rule. Consider: does prescriptive regulation improve security outcomes, or does it create compliance theater? Who benefits and who is harmed by a uniform national standard modeled on the most stringent state approach? What happens to community banks and credit unions that lack the resources to meet Class A requirements?

Slide 9 — Compliance vs. Security: A Critical Distinction

Meeting Regulatory Requirements Does Not Equal Being Secure

This is perhaps the most important conceptual point in the entire regulatory framework module. Compliance and security are related but distinct goals. Organizations that optimize for compliance checkbox completion often build fragile security programs that satisfy auditors but fail under real-world attack conditions.

Compliance-First Mindset

Satisfies regulatory requirements at minimum cost. Focuses on documentation and audit-readiness. Treats security spending as a cost center. Remediates only to compliance thresholds. Works backward from audit requirements to security controls.

Result: Passes audits. May still get breached. Is still sued.

Security-First Mindset

Uses threat intelligence to prioritize controls. Builds resilience, not just compliance. Treats security as a business enabler. Remediates to risk tolerance, not compliance floor. Works backward from adversary capabilities to necessary controls.

Result: Harder to breach. Compliance is a byproduct, not the goal.

THE TARGET LESSON

Target Corporation was PCI DSS compliant at the time of its 2013 breach — one of the largest retail breaches in history (40 million payment cards). Their security operations center received FireEye malware detection alerts about the attack, which were ignored. Compliance does not prevent breaches. It establishes a floor, creates legal defense arguments, and demonstrates reasonable care — but it cannot substitute for operational security competence, alert triage discipline, and adversary-aware thinking. Teach your future employers the difference.
