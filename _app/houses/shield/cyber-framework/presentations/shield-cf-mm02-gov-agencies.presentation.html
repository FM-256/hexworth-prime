Roles & Responsibilities of Government Agencies

Mapping the US government's cybersecurity ecosystem — who owns what, who responds when, and how agencies coordinate across law enforcement, intelligence, standards, and operations.

FRAMING QUESTION

The US government's cybersecurity structure is intentionally distributed across many agencies. Is this fragmented coordination a strategic strength (specialization, redundancy) or a systemic weakness (jurisdictional gaps, slow response)? Hold this question as you move through the module.

Slide 2 — Discussion: Consolidated vs. Distributed Responsibility
CLASS DISCUSSION

Should the US create a single unified "Cyber Agency" with consolidated authority over all federal cybersecurity functions?

Consider the tradeoffs: A single agency offers unified command, clear accountability, and streamlined response. But consolidation risks creating a single point of failure, losing specialized expertise, and concentrating authority in ways that conflict with constitutional separation of powers. What does history tell us about consolidated vs. distributed security structures?

Current Distributed Model

US cybersecurity authority is distributed across more than a dozen agencies, each with distinct legal authorities, funding streams, and operational cultures. No single "cyber czar" has binding authority over all federal agencies. CISA coordinates but cannot compel.

Compare to: UK's NCSC (National Cyber Security Centre) under GCHQ — a more unified model that many argue is more decisive. Australia's ASD (Australian Signals Directorate) similarly consolidates offensive and defensive cyber under one roof.

Slide 3 — Department of State (DOS)

Cybersecurity's Diplomatic Dimension

The State Department's cybersecurity mission is fundamentally diplomatic rather than operational. DOS leads US international engagement on cyberspace policy and norms.

Open Internet
Free Flow of Information Globally

DOS advocates for an open, interoperable, secure, and reliable internet internationally. Opposes authoritarian internet fragmentation (the "splinternet") and promotes freedom of expression online as a foreign policy priority.

Internet Governance IGF Participation ICANN Policy
Digital Ecosystem
Trusted Technology Infrastructure

DOS works to ensure that critical technology infrastructure in allied nations is built by trusted vendors. A direct response to concerns about Huawei and other state-linked technology suppliers embedding surveillance capabilities into global networks.

Supply Chain 5G Security Trusted Vendors
Partner Capacity Building
Cybersecurity Aid & Training

DOS funds and coordinates cybersecurity capacity building programs in developing nations and allied partners. A country with weak cyber defenses is a vulnerability for the entire global network — attackers use poorly defended nations as staging grounds and proxy infrastructure. DOS addresses this as a foreign policy and national security matter through its Bureau of Cyberspace and Digital Policy (CDP), established 2022.

Bureau of CDP OECD Norms Budapest Convention UN GGE
Slide 4 — Department of Justice & FBI
DOJ / FBI
Department of Justice / Federal Bureau of Investigation

The FBI is the lead federal law enforcement agency for investigating cybercrimes that affect US interests. The Computer Crime and Intellectual Property Section (CCIPS) of DOJ handles prosecution. Together they represent the criminal justice response to cyber threats.

Computer Intrusions

Unauthorized access to computer systems — hacking of government, financial, healthcare, and critical infrastructure systems. FBI's Cyber Division maintains 24/7 rapid response capability and works closely with CISA on major incidents.

Ransomware

FBI tracks ransomware groups, disrupts their infrastructure, and when possible seizes cryptocurrency ransoms (as with Colonial Pipeline's partial $2.3M recovery). The DOJ Ransomware and Digital Extortion Task Force coordinates multi-agency response.

Password Trafficking & Identity Theft

Buying, selling, or trafficking stolen credentials is a federal crime. FBI operates undercover in dark web marketplaces (e.g., operation Disruptor seized $6.5M from 179 defendants in 2020). CFAA Section 1030(a)(6) specifically criminalizes trafficking in passwords.

Internet Fraud & Child Exploitation

The Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) receives 800,000+ complaints annually. ICAC (Internet Crimes Against Children) task force handles online child exploitation — one of the FBI's highest priorities, operating in all 50 states.

PROFESSOR'S NOTE

The FBI operates within US criminal law, requiring probable cause, warrants, and eventual prosecution. This distinguishes it sharply from NSA (intelligence collection) and USCYBERCOM (offensive operations). The same incident may simultaneously involve all three — each operating under completely different legal frameworks. Understanding these lanes prevents confusion in incident analysis.

Slide 5 — National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)

Standards, Not Enforcement

NIST is a non-regulatory agency within the Department of Commerce. It does not have enforcement authority — NIST's power comes from the quality and adoption of its standards, not legal compulsion. However, federal agencies are often required to implement NIST standards, and courts have begun treating NIST frameworks as a reasonable standard of care for private sector defendants.

Standards & Guidelines
Technical Foundations

NIST Special Publications (SP 800-series) cover every aspect of information security — from risk management (SP 800-37) to cryptography (SP 800-131A) to supply chain risk (SP 800-161). These are the gold standard references for federal and private sector security programs.

SP 800-53 SP 800-171 SP 800-37
CSF 2.0 (2024)
Cybersecurity Framework

NIST CSF version 2.0 was released in February 2024, expanding from the original 5 functions to 6 by adding "Govern" as a new function. CSF 2.0 explicitly extends scope from critical infrastructure to all organizations of any size. This is a major evolution — covered in depth in MM5.

Slide 6 — Department of Homeland Security (DHS)

The Domestic Cyber Shield

Created after 9/11 by the Homeland Security Act of 2002, DHS consolidated 22 federal agencies into one department. Its core cybersecurity mission: protect American infrastructure and respond to domestic cyber threats without the intelligence community's foreign-focused authorities.

Counter Terrorism

DHS coordinates federal efforts to prevent terrorist attacks with a growing focus on cyber-enabled terrorism — attacks on power grids, water systems, and financial infrastructure that could cause mass casualty or economic disruption events.

Secure Cyberspace

DHS houses CISA (the operational arm), the Secret Service (financial cyber crimes), and ICE's Homeland Security Investigations (HSI) for cybercrime with transnational elements. Together these provide overlapping coverage of domestic cyber threats.

HIRT Teams — Hunt and Incident Response Teams

CISA's HIRT teams deploy on-site to organizations that have experienced significant intrusions or need proactive threat hunting. Unlike FBI (criminal investigation) or NSA (foreign intelligence), HIRT's mission is purely defensive remediation: evict the adversary, restore operations, harden defenses. These teams respond to requests from any sector — federal, state, local, and critical infrastructure. Think of HIRT as the SWAT team for cyber defense that comes directly to your organization.

Free to Request On-Site Response Hunt Operations Remediation
Slide 7 — CISA: Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency

America's Cyber Defense Agency

CISA was established in 2018 (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency Act) as a stand-alone agency under DHS, elevating what was previously the National Protection and Programs Directorate (NPPD). The elevation signaled that cyber defense of critical infrastructure deserved a dedicated operational agency with its own director and budget authority.

Guidance to State & Local Government

CISA provides free cybersecurity assessments, advisories, and training to state, local, tribal, and territorial (SLTT) governments — who often lack the resources to defend themselves against sophisticated threats. This includes the Multi-State ISAC (MS-ISAC) partnership.

Cyber Exercises

CISA runs Cyber Storm exercises (national-level tabletop and functional exercises) and supports sector-specific exercises. These test coordination between federal, state, and private sector stakeholders — identifying gaps in communication and decision-making before a real incident occurs.

Manages National Cyber Risk

CISA maintains the National Risk Management Center (NRMC), operates the EINSTEIN intrusion detection system on federal networks, and administers the Continuous Diagnostics and Mitigation (CDM) program across federal civilian agencies. It also manages the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog.

Election Security

A newer and politically sensitive mission: CISA designates election infrastructure as critical infrastructure and provides security assistance to state election officials. This includes threat intelligence sharing, vulnerability scanning, and the EINSTEIN-for-elections program — a role that generated significant controversy in 2020-2022.

Slide 8 — National Security Agency (NSA)

Signals Intelligence & Information Assurance

NSA operates under the Department of Defense with Title 50 (intelligence) and Title 10 (military) authorities, depending on the mission. It is simultaneously the nation's premier SIGINT (signals intelligence) collector and its top defensive cybersecurity technical organization — a dual mandate that creates inherent tensions around vulnerability disclosure.

01
Collect foreign signals intelligence (SIGINT) to support national security decision-making
02
Protect national security systems — classified government and military networks
03
Defeat adversary cyberspace operations threatening US national security interests
04
Enable US cryptologic partners — share intelligence with Five Eyes allies (UK, Canada, Australia, NZ)
05
Advance cybersecurity standards — NSA's Cybersecurity Directorate publishes defensive guidance for private sector
06
Partner with government, industry, and academia — NSA Centers of Academic Excellence (CAE) program
07
Support combatant commanders — provide technical intelligence to military operations worldwide
THE EQUITIES PROBLEM

NSA's dual role creates the "equities problem": when NSA discovers a zero-day vulnerability, should it disclose to the vendor (defensive value) or exploit it for intelligence collection (offensive value)? The Vulnerabilities Equities Process (VEP), formalized by EO 13694 and updated in 2017, provides a policy framework — but the WannaCry incident (exploiting NSA's stockpiled EternalBlue) demonstrated the catastrophic downside of the collect-don't-patch approach.

Slide 9 — US Cyber Command (USCYBERCOM)

Military Cyberspace Operations

USCYBERCOM was established in 2009 and elevated to a Unified Combatant Command in 2018 — giving it the same status as CENTCOM and SOCOM. It is co-located with NSA at Fort Meade, Maryland, and its commander is dual-hatted as Director of NSA. This co-location creates powerful synergies and serious civil liberties concerns.

Defend DoDIN

Defend the Department of Defense Information Network — 3+ million users across hundreds of networks globally. 24/7 defensive cyber operations to detect and evict adversaries from military systems.

Support Combatant Commanders

Provide cyber effects and intelligence support to geographic combatant commanders (CENTCOM, INDOPACOM, EUCOM, etc.) integrated with kinetic military operations.

Strengthen Cyber Response

Defend the nation against significant cyber attacks. CYBERCOM's "defend forward" strategy disrupts adversary operations before they reach US networks — a shift from reactive to proactive defense.

DEFEND FORWARD DOCTRINE

"Persistent engagement" and "defend forward" — CYBERCOM's operational doctrine since 2018 — means actively operating in adversary networks to observe, counter, and disrupt malicious cyber activity before it reaches US systems. This is legally authorized under NDAA 2019 Section 1642 and represents a fundamental shift from passive perimeter defense. Critics note it risks escalation; proponents argue it's the only way to deter sophisticated nation-state actors who operate continuously within US networks.

Slide 10 — Regulatory Agencies with Cybersecurity Authority

Sector-Specific Cybersecurity Regulators

Beyond the operational agencies, multiple independent regulatory commissions hold enforcement authority over specific sectors. Their cybersecurity role is distinct — they set compliance requirements, conduct examinations, and impose penalties rather than responding to incidents or collecting intelligence.

Agency Sector Cybersecurity Authority
FTC Consumer / All Sectors Section 5 authority over "unfair or deceptive" practices. De facto consumer cybersecurity regulator — can pursue companies with unreasonable security practices even without a specific cybersecurity statute.
FCC Telecommunications Regulates telecom carrier cybersecurity. Recent CPNI (Customer Proprietary Network Information) rules require carriers to protect subscriber data. Also addressing BGP routing security and submarine cable security.
SEC Financial Markets Crypto assets and cybersecurity enforcement unit targets market manipulation using cyber means. 2023 cybersecurity disclosure rules require material incident reporting (Form 8-K within 4 days) and annual governance disclosures.
HHS / OCR Healthcare HIPAA enforcement — Office for Civil Rights (OCR) investigates healthcare data breaches and can impose civil penalties up to $1.9M per violation category per year. 2024 proposed HIPAA Security Rule updates would add mandatory MFA and penetration testing requirements.
Slide 11 — Presidential Policy Directive 41 (PPD-41)

The Federal Cyber Incident Response Framework

PPD-41, issued by President Obama in 2016, establishes the framework for how the federal government responds to significant cyber incidents. It creates clear organizational lanes, defines key terms, and establishes the National Cyber Incident Response Plan (NCIRP) as the implementing document.

Cyber Incident

An event that actually or potentially jeopardizes the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of an information system or the information the system processes, stores, or transmits. Routine events — millions occur daily on government networks.

Significant Cyber Incident

A cyber incident that is (or group of incidents that together are) likely to result in demonstrable harm to the national security interests, foreign relations, economy, or public confidence of the United States. Triggers full PPD-41 coordinated response. Rare — reserved for events like SolarWinds, Colonial Pipeline scale.

Threat Response

DOJ/FBI lead. Investigative activities — attribute the attack, pursue criminal prosecution, disrupt the adversary. Uses law enforcement authorities and grand jury process.

Asset Response

DHS/CISA lead. Technical assistance to victim organizations — help them understand what happened, recover operations, harden systems against follow-on attacks. Not criminal investigation.

Intelligence Support

ODNI/IC lead. Provide relevant intelligence context — who attacked, why, what other targets may be at risk, what other campaigns are underway. Inform but don't direct other two lines.

WHY THREE LANES MATTER

The three PPD-41 lines of effort reflect the US government's attempt to prevent the inevitable bureaucratic collision between criminal investigators (who need to preserve evidence and build cases), incident responders (who need to remediate quickly), and intelligence analysts (who may want to monitor rather than evict the adversary to collect more intelligence). All three have legitimate priorities that genuinely conflict. PPD-41 creates a framework for managed tension rather than pretending the conflict doesn't exist.

Slide 12 — National Cyber Incident Response Plan (NCIRP)
2009
National Infrastructure Protection Plan (NIPP)
Early framework for critical infrastructure protection, predating a formal cyber incident response plan. Established sector-specific agencies and public-private partnership model.
2014–2015
Office of Personnel Management (OPM) Breach
Chinese intelligence stole SF-86 background investigation files for 21.5 million federal employees and contractors. The chaotic response — multiple agencies with unclear authorities, no unified playbook — directly motivated PPD-41 and the NCIRP.
2016
First NCIRP Publication
The National Cyber Incident Response Plan is first published, providing a national framework for coordinated response to significant cyber incidents. Established roles for federal agencies, private sector, and state/local governments. Directly implements PPD-41's three lines of effort.
2024
NCIRP Update Initiated
DHS initiated a comprehensive update to NCIRP following lessons learned from SolarWinds (2020), Colonial Pipeline (2021), and Volt Typhoon campaigns (2023–2024). Updated plan expected to address cloud incidents, AI-enabled attacks, and infrastructure interdependencies not anticipated in the 2016 version.
EXAM POINT

Remember the NCIRP relationship hierarchy: EO 13636 (2013) mandated critical infrastructure cybersecurity framework development → PPD-41 (2016) established the federal response coordination framework → NCIRP (2016) provided the implementing plan. When the question asks "what provides the national framework for coordinated cyber incident response," the answer is NCIRP. When it asks "what directive established the lines of effort," the answer is PPD-41.
