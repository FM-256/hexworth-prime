MM7 — PRESENTATION

Data Breach Litigation

When a breach occurs, the legal consequences unfold across multiple theories of liability. Understanding how plaintiffs sue, what courts require, and why most cases fail is essential knowledge for cybersecurity professionals advising organizations.

Slide 1 — The Post-Breach Litigation Landscape

Three Primary Legal Battlegrounds

A significant data breach typically triggers litigation on multiple simultaneous fronts. Understanding each theory — its strengths, weaknesses, and typical outcomes — is critical for anyone advising an organization on cyber risk management.

01

Negligence

The most common theory. Plaintiffs argue the organization failed its duty of reasonable care in protecting their data. Requires proving four elements and faces serious standing hurdles.

02

Breach of Contract

Plaintiffs point to privacy policies, terms of service, PCI DSS compliance representations, or insurance contracts as the basis for a contractual promise that was broken.

03

Securities Fraud & Shareholder Actions

Public companies face shareholder class actions when cybersecurity misrepresentations in SEC filings cause stock drops after a disclosed breach. Increasingly common post-SEC Rule 2023.

PROFESSOR'S NOTE

Data breach litigation is one of the fastest-evolving areas of law. Courts issued fewer than 50 data breach opinions per year before 2010. By 2023, hundreds of class action suits are filed annually. The legal framework is being written in real time — which means today's cases set the rules defendants and plaintiffs face tomorrow.

Slide 2 — Negligence: The Four-Element Framework
1

Duty of Care

The defendant owed a legal obligation to protect the plaintiff's data. There is no federal common law of negligence — it is state tort law. Courts have generally assumed a duty exists when an entity voluntarily collects and stores personal data, but the scope of that duty is contested.

Cyber context: No federal statute creates a general duty of data protection. Courts must infer duty from the relationship (customer, patient, employee) and the foreseeability of harm from a breach.
2

Breach of Duty

The defendant failed to exercise the care a reasonably prudent organization would take. A data breach itself is often treated as circumstantial evidence of breach — if you got hacked, perhaps you weren't careful enough.

Cyber context: Plaintiffs point to industry standards (NIST CSF, CIS Controls, sector-specific regulations) to define what "reasonable" security looks like. Failure to implement basic controls (MFA, patching, encryption) is strong evidence of breach.
3

Causation

The breach of duty actually caused the harm claimed. Plaintiffs must show a causal link between the specific security failure and the specific harm they suffered. This is both a cause-in-fact and proximate cause requirement.

Cyber context: Causation can be difficult to establish when harm is downstream fraud. Did the Equifax breach cause this specific credit card fraud — or was the card stolen elsewhere? Tracing data lineage is an evidentiary challenge courts grapple with.
4

Harm / Damages

The plaintiff suffered actual, cognizable damages. This is where most data breach negligence claims fail. Courts apply two major doctrines that block recovery for the most common breach injuries.

Cyber context: The Economic Loss Doctrine and Article III Standing requirements are the primary barriers. Both are addressed in the next slides — they are the reason most data breach negligence claims are dismissed at the pleading stage.
Slide 3 — The Harm Problem: Two Major Barriers

Barrier 1 — The Economic Loss Doctrine

Traditional tort law (negligence) was designed to compensate for physical injury to persons or property. Pure economic loss — financial harm without accompanying physical injury or property damage — is generally not recoverable under negligence in most states.

The problem for data breach plaintiffs: Most breach victims suffer only economic harm. Their data was exposed; they haven't been physically injured. The data on the server was information, not tangible property. Many courts have dismissed data breach negligence claims precisely because the only harm alleged is future risk of identity theft — a pure economic concern.

The exception: Some courts recognize that stolen personal data constitutes harm to "personal property" or that the diminished value of the data itself is property damage. This theory is gaining traction but remains unsettled.

Barrier 2 — Article III Standing

Federal courts require plaintiffs to show a concrete, particularized injury that is either actual or imminent. Speculative future harm — the risk that stolen data might someday be used to commit fraud — has been debated extensively by the circuit courts.

The key question: Is the fear or risk of future identity theft a sufficient injury, or must plaintiffs show actual misuse of their data before they have standing to sue?

STRATEGIC INSIGHT

These two doctrines together mean that data breach victims who have not yet experienced actual fraud face enormous hurdles. The practical advice to practitioners: breach litigation is most viable when plaintiffs can show actual fraudulent charges, actual identity theft events, or time spent remediating actual harm — not merely the risk of future harm.

Slide 4 — Article III Standing: Key Cases
2013 — SUPREME COURT

Clapper v. Amnesty International

Plaintiffs challenged NSA surveillance programs, arguing the mere risk of being surveilled caused them to spend money on encrypted communications.

The Supreme Court held that speculative, future harm — even if plaintiffs spent money in response to that risk — is insufficient for Article III standing. Injury must be "certainly impending" or there must be a "substantial risk" of harm.

Standing Denied — Risk Insufficient
2015 — 7TH CIRCUIT

Remijas v. Neiman Marcus Group

After the Neiman Marcus data breach, some plaintiffs had already experienced fraudulent charges on their payment cards. Others had not but alleged a substantial risk of future harm.

The 7th Circuit found that both groups had standing. For the fraud victims, injury was obvious. For the others, the court found the substantial risk of future identity theft sufficient, distinguishing Clapper because the risk here was not speculative — the data was actually stolen.

Standing Found — Class Certified
2016 — SUPREME COURT

Spokeo v. Robins

A data aggregator published incorrect information about Thomas Robins. He sued under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA), which technically prohibits such inaccuracies.

The Supreme Court held that a "bare procedural violation" of a statute — without any concrete harm from that violation — is insufficient for Article III standing. The plaintiff must show the statutory violation caused real-world harm, not merely a technical violation of the law.

Standing Insufficient — Bare Statutory Violation
2021 — SUPREME COURT

TransUnion v. Ramirez

TransUnion incorrectly flagged 8,000+ people as potential terrorists on their credit reports. Only 1,853 of those had their files shared with third parties. The class included all 8,185.

The Supreme Court held that every member of a certified class must individually show concrete harm — risk of harm alone was insufficient for the 6,332 class members whose files were never shared. Dramatically limits class size in data breach suits.

Class Severely Limited — Every Member Must Show Harm
THE STANDING EVOLUTION

The trajectory from Clapper (2013) to TransUnion (2021) tightens the standing requirements for data breach plaintiffs. The Supreme Court is clearly hostile to purely speculative harm theories. This has driven plaintiffs to allege actual, quantifiable harms — time spent on credit monitoring, costs of identity theft remediation, and lost value of personal data as a commodity.

Slide 5 — Breach of Contract: Contractual Obligations & the Fine Print

When a Promise Becomes a Legal Obligation

Breach of contract claims avoid some of the difficulties of negligence — particularly the Economic Loss Doctrine, which typically does not bar contract claims. Plaintiffs identify a specific promise in a document (privacy policy, terms of service, compliance certification) and argue the breach violated that promise.

  • PCI DSS

    Merchants and processors contractually agree to comply with PCI DSS standards when they accept card payments. A breach that reveals they were not compliant can trigger breach of contract claims from card networks, acquiring banks, and affected cardholders — as well as PCI fines and chargebacks.

  • PRIVACY POLICY

    If a company's published privacy policy promises "we use industry-standard encryption to protect your data" and they were storing passwords in plaintext, courts have found that statement creates an enforceable contractual obligation to affected consumers.

  • INSURANCE

    Cyber insurance policies contain coverage exclusions that frequently become breach of contract disputes. Common contested exclusions: government-sponsored attack clauses (Nation-State Exclusion), war exclusions, and failure-to-maintain-security exclusions. Merck v. ACE (2022) produced a landmark ruling on war exclusion applicability to NotPetya.

  • VENDOR CONTRACTS

    Service agreements between covered entities and vendors frequently contain security warranties and data protection obligations. A vendor breach that violates these terms can result in indemnification claims, termination disputes, and consequential damage claims for downstream breach notification costs.

INSURANCE EXCLUSION NOTE

The cyber insurance market's response to nation-state attacks is a live crisis. After Merck sued over NotPetya (which the US attributed to Russia), insurers broadly invoked "act of war" exclusions. Courts have generally been skeptical, holding that "war" clauses require declared armed conflict between nation-states. But insurers have now rewritten exclusions explicitly naming government-sponsored cyberattacks. The legal landscape for nation-state breach coverage is in flux.

Slide 6 — Securities Fraud & Shareholder Class Actions

Securities Fraud (10b-5 Claims)

  • Company made false or misleading statements about cybersecurity posture in public filings (10-K, proxy statements, earnings calls)

  • The statements were material — a reasonable investor would consider them important

  • Company knew the statements were false (scienter) at the time they were made

  • The false statements artificially inflated the stock price

  • When the breach was disclosed, the stock dropped — investors suffered damages

  • SEC's 2023 Cybersecurity Disclosure Rules now require material incident disclosure within 4 days and annual cybersecurity risk management disclosures

Derivative Actions (Breach of Fiduciary Duty)

  • Shareholders sue on behalf of the corporation (not themselves) against directors and officers for failing their fiduciary duty of oversight

  • Caremark doctrine: directors must maintain adequate information and reporting systems to monitor corporate risk — including cybersecurity

  • Post-breach, plaintiffs allege the board received no meaningful cyber risk reports or refused to act on red flags

  • Rarely succeed — courts apply business judgment rule — but discovery process is extremely damaging and expensive

  • SolarWinds (2023 SEC enforcement) established that even technical officers can face personal liability for misleading disclosures

2018 — SEC ENFORCEMENT

Yahoo / Altaba

Yahoo failed to disclose its 2014 breach (affecting 500 million accounts) for two years. During that period, it represented its security posture favorably in SEC filings and completed the Verizon acquisition with inaccurate disclosures.

SEC charged Yahoo with securities violations. $35 million penalty. The CISO was later charged in a related criminal matter. This case established that concealing a known breach from investors violates securities laws.

$35M SEC Fine — CISO Charged
2023 — SEC ENFORCEMENT

SolarWinds / Timothy Brown

The SEC charged SolarWinds and its CISO Timothy Brown with fraud, alleging that internal communications showed awareness of severe security deficiencies while public statements described robust controls.

The case signals that CISOs personally face securities enforcement liability for cybersecurity misrepresentations — a watershed moment for the profession. The CISO, not just the company, was named as a defendant.

CISO Personally Named — Landmark
CAREER-DEFINING INSIGHT

The SolarWinds case is the most consequential development in cybersecurity law for practitioners since the profession emerged. CISOs have historically operated with relative protection under the corporate veil. The SEC's decision to charge Timothy Brown personally signals that CISOs who sign off on misleading disclosures — or who fail to escalate known risks to the board — face personal legal liability. Documentation of what you knew, when you knew it, and what you recommended is now a career survival skill.
