CFAA Case Analysis — Three Edge Scenarios
Pending

The Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (1986, amended)

The CFAA criminalizes "unauthorized access" or "exceeding authorized access" to protected computers. It was written before the modern internet and its language has been stretched to cover scenarios its drafters never anticipated. Three sections drive most criminal CFAA cases: §1030(a)(2) [unauthorized access], §1030(a)(5) [damage], and §1030(a)(7) [extortion].

Key CFAA Concepts

  • "Unauthorized access" — Access without any permission (outsider attacker, credential theft)
  • "Exceeds authorized access" — Has some legitimate access but goes beyond it (insider going to prohibited files)
  • "Protected computer" — Any computer used in interstate commerce (effectively: any computer connected to the internet)
  • Civil CFAA — Victims can sue under §1030(g) for damages — minimum $5,000 in losses required
  • Criminal CFAA — Requires "intent" to cause damage; felony if damages exceed $5,000 or targets critical infrastructure
Scenario A — The Insider (CFAA § 1030(a)(2))

A hospital IT administrator has legitimate access to patient records to perform system maintenance. He accesses his ex-wife's medical records out of personal curiosity — her records are outside his job function. No data is copied or shared.

Scenario B — Web Scraping (hiQ v. LinkedIn)

A data analytics company scrapes publicly accessible LinkedIn profiles using automated bots. LinkedIn sends a cease-and-desist and blocks their IPs. The company bypasses the blocks and continues. LinkedIn's Terms of Service prohibit automated scraping.

Scenario C — Password Sharing (Pre-Van Buren)

An employee shares their valid corporate login credentials with a colleague who lacks access to a particular system. The colleague uses the credentials to access the system and download files she needs for a project. Both are employed by the company.

Policy Question

Aaron Swartz was prosecuted under CFAA for mass-downloading academic articles from JSTOR using MIT's network — activities that arguably violated ToS but caused no financial damage. He faced 35 years in prison. JSTOR itself declined to press charges. Critics argue the CFAA gives prosecutors excessive discretion. Evaluate: should ToS violations be per se CFAA violations, or should CFAA be limited to cases involving actual computer damage or financial loss?

Van Buren v. United States (2021) — Code vs. Contract
Pending

The Landmark Case That Narrowed the CFAA

In Van Buren v. United States (141 S. Ct. 1648, 2021), the Supreme Court held 6-3 that a person "exceeds authorized access" only when they access computer files, folders, or databases that are off-limits — based on code-based restrictions (gates in the system) — not merely when they access authorized information for an unauthorized purpose. This resolved a circuit split that had made the CFAA unpredictably broad.

Van Buren's Code-Based vs. Contract-Based Standard

  • Code-based restriction (CFAA violation) — A technical barrier prevents access to certain files: separate database, permission gate, access control list. Van Buren accessing the DMV system to look up a plate number for a bribe = CFAA violation because he had no code-based authorization to use the system for that purpose? WRONG — SCOTUS said he DID have code-based access to that system. No violation.
  • Contract-based restriction (NOT a CFAA violation after Van Buren) — Employee agreement, Terms of Service, or company policy prohibiting certain uses of an otherwise-accessible system. Violating a "don't use for personal reasons" policy is no longer a CFAA violation.
  • The test: Did the defendant have code-level authorization to access those specific files? If yes, their purpose/motive is irrelevant to CFAA.

Apply Van Buren to Each Scenario

Scenario 1

An employee has legitimate access to HR database records for all employees. Company policy says "access only records relevant to your job." She looks up her manager's salary out of curiosity. No data is copied.

Scenario 2

A pen tester is hired to test Company A's network. During testing, she discovers a misconfigured server also connected to Company B's network (different client). She accesses Company B's server to demonstrate the vulnerability.

Scenario 3

A police officer has lawful access to a law enforcement database. A private investigator pays him to run license plate searches for non-law-enforcement purposes. The officer has code-level access; departmental policy prohibits commercial use.

Scenario 4

A former employee's access was supposed to be revoked upon termination but due to an IT error, their credentials still work 30 days later. They log in and download their old project files to show a new employer.

Design a Post-Van Buren CFAA

If you were rewriting the CFAA today with the benefit of Van Buren's reasoning, what would "unauthorized access" mean? Would you use a code-based standard, an intent-based standard, a harm-based standard, or a combination? Draft the key operative language for the new statute in 3-5 sentences.

Multi-State Breach Notification — Compliance Matrix
Pending

50 State Laws, Zero Federal Standard (Until CIRCIA)

Every US state has a breach notification law. All 50 differ in: what data triggers notification, how long you have to notify, who you notify (just individuals, or also the AG/regulators), what the notice must contain, and penalties for non-compliance. A company with customers in multiple states must satisfy ALL applicable state laws simultaneously — defaulting to the most stringent.

Massachusetts "As expeditiously as possible" Yes (any number) SSN alone = trigger Must notify AG AND CPA before notifying individuals; encryption safe harbor

Hypothetical Breach: RetailMax Corporation

Incident Facts

RetailMax operates e-commerce websites with customers in all 50 states. On March 1, their database is breached. Stolen data: full names, email addresses, shipping addresses, last 4 digits of credit cards, and purchase history. 485 California residents, 612 New York residents, 1,200 Texas residents, 380 Florida residents, and 290 Massachusetts residents are affected. Total: 2,967 individuals. No SSNs were in the database. Data was not encrypted at rest. Discovery date: March 5. The breach was caused by an unpatched vulnerability.

Compliance Strategy Question

RetailMax's general counsel asks you: "Should we just adopt the strictest state's requirements as our uniform national standard?" Evaluate the legal, operational, and business trade-offs of this approach versus maintaining state-specific notification procedures.

Draft a Breach Notification Letter
Pending

The Notification Letter as Legal Document

A breach notification letter is not just good PR — it is a legally required document with specific mandatory elements under state law, and its contents can be used as evidence in subsequent litigation. Every word is scrutinized. An evasive, minimizing, or incomplete letter creates additional legal exposure.

Required Elements (Most State Laws)

  • Description of the incident — What happened (type of breach), when it occurred, when discovered
  • Categories of information involved — Specific types of PI compromised
  • Steps taken to address the breach — What you've done to contain and remediate
  • What affected individuals should do — Specific protective actions (credit freeze, password change)
  • Contact information — How to reach the company for questions
  • Credit monitoring offer — Required in some states if SSN exposed; best practice generally

Notification Scenario: MediStore Health App

Facts for Your Letter

MediStore is a mobile health app (not a covered HIPAA entity) that helps users track medications. Affected data: names, email addresses, medication lists, and pharmacy information for 45,000 users. The breach occurred when an API endpoint was left publicly accessible without authentication for 22 days (January 8 – January 30). Discovered February 1. No evidence of data being sold or misused yet. MediStore has patched the endpoint, engaged a forensic firm, and is offering 12 months of credit monitoring. Notification deadline is 60 days from discovery.

[COMPANY LETTERHEAD] [DATE] Dear [AFFECTED USER NAME], [PARAGRAPH 1: What happened — Be specific about the incident type, dates, and scope] [PARAGRAPH 2: What information was involved — List the specific data categories] [PARAGRAPH 3: What we are doing — Containment steps, remediation, forensic investigation] [PARAGRAPH 4: What you can do — Specific, actionable protective steps] [PARAGRAPH 5: Credit monitoring offer and how to enroll] [PARAGRAPH 6: Contact information and questions] Sincerely, [NAME, TITLE] MediStore Inc.
