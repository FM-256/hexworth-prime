The Federal Cybersecurity Landscape

No single agency owns cybersecurity. Responsibilities are deliberately distributed across civilian agencies, intelligence community, law enforcement, and the military — each with distinct legal authorities, missions, and constraints. Understanding which agency does what is essential for incident response planning.

Presidential Policy Directive 41 (PPD-41)

PPD-41 (2016) established the US government's policy for responding to significant cyber incidents. It created three parallel "lines of effort" — Threat, Asset, and Intelligence — each led by a different agency simultaneously. This was designed to prevent the paralysis that occurs when agencies argue over who's in charge.

Simulated Breach: NEXUS Financial Network

Incident Facts

A suspected nation-state actor has compromised NEXUS, a clearinghouse processing $2T in daily transactions. Attackers have had access for 90 days. They've exfiltrated settlement data and planted logic bombs in transaction processing code. The actor is believed to be affiliated with a sanctioned state.

Current Status

The FBI received a tip from a foreign partner. CISA has been notified. The Treasury's OFR is monitoring for systemic risk indicators. No public disclosure has been made. The NEXUS board is asking: "Do we go public? Do we pay to find out if we're clean? Who's in charge?"

Decision Point

The FBI wants to keep the investigation covert for 30 more days to build their case. CISA wants immediate disclosure to the financial sector so they can check for similar compromises. The NEXUS board wants to delay public disclosure to prevent a market panic. Who has authority to compel disclosure, and how should this conflict be resolved?

NCIRP Application — Colonial Pipeline Case Study

National Cyber Incident Response Plan (NCIRP)

The NCIRP operationalizes PPD-41 by providing specific roles, responsibilities, and procedures for significant cyber incidents. Colonial Pipeline (May 2021) was a real-world stress test of the NCIRP framework. The 6-day shutdown created the first declared energy emergency in 17 states.

Colonial Pipeline — Key Facts

  • Attack vector: Compromised VPN credentials (likely purchased on dark web); no MFA on the VPN account
  • Attacker: DarkSide ransomware-as-a-service criminal group (Russia-based)
  • Impact: 5,500 miles of pipeline; 45% of East Coast fuel supply; ~$4.4M ransom paid
  • Government response: TSA emergency directives; DOJ recovered $2.3M of ransom; FBI notified Colonial of actor before shutdown decision
  • Key failure: Colonial chose to shut down OT pipelines preemptively — a business decision, not a technical requirement

NCIRP Response Phases — Apply to Colonial

Phase 1: Prepare

What should Colonial have had in place before the attack? Evaluate their actual preparedness based on what was publicly disclosed about their security posture.

Phase 2: Respond

Map the actual government response: when did each agency engage, what actions did they take, and were there gaps in the first 48 hours?

Phase 3: Recover

What enabled Colonial to restore operations? What role did the DOJ's ransom recovery play? What changed in pipeline security policy post-incident?

Phase 4: Adapt

What permanent policy changes resulted from Colonial? Evaluate TSA Pipeline Security Directives 02A and 02B — were they sufficient?

Counterfactual Analysis

If Colonial Pipeline had NOT shut down its OT systems (the pipeline kept flowing while the IT ransomware was contained), how would the incident response have unfolded differently? Would the government response have been the same? Would the policy changes have occurred? What does this tell us about the relationship between business decisions and national security outcomes?

Different Incidents, Different Lead Agencies

The lead federal agency for a cyber incident depends on the nature of the victim, the nature of the threat actor, and the sector involved. A healthcare breach triggers different agencies than a defense contractor breach or an election infrastructure attack. Mapping this matrix is a practical skill for any security professional working with the federal government.

Incident Type Lead Agency Key Supporting Agencies Primary Legal Hook
Federal civilian agency (.gov) breach CISA FBI, ODNI, OMB FISMA; BOD authority
DoD / defense contractor breach CYBERCOM / DIB ISAC NSA, FBI, DCSA DFARS clause; CMMC
Financial sector attack (bank, exchange) Treasury / FS-ISAC FBI, Secret Service, OCC, FDIC, SEC Gramm-Leach-Bliley; SEC Reg S-P
Election infrastructure attack CISA (EI-ISAC) FBI, ODNI, state election offices EAC guidelines; Help America Vote Act
Healthcare / hospital ransomware HHS / HC3 FBI, CISA, Secret Service HIPAA Breach Notification Rule
Ransomware affecting private company (no CI) FBI CISA, Secret Service, private IR firms CFAA; IC3 reporting
Critical infrastructure (power/water/pipeline) CISA + Sector SRMA FBI, NSA, sector regulators (TSA, FERC, EPA) Sector-specific regulations; EO 13636
