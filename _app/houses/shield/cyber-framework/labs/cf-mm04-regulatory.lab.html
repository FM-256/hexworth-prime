The Regulatory Patchwork Problem

US cybersecurity law is sector-specific rather than comprehensive. A company's applicable regulations depend on what industry it's in, what data it handles, its size, whether it's publicly traded, and which states it operates in. A mid-sized company can simultaneously be subject to HIPAA, PCI-DSS, state privacy laws, FTC Act Section 5, and SEC rules — each with different standards and enforcement mechanisms.

FTC v. Wyndham Worldwide — Establishing the FTC's Cyber Authority

FTC v. Wyndham Worldwide Corp. (3d Cir. 2015) confirmed the FTC's authority to regulate cybersecurity under Section 5's prohibition on "unfair" practices. Wyndham was breached three times in two years (2008-2010), exposing 619,000 payment card numbers and causing $10.6M in fraudulent charges. The FTC sued, Wyndham fought back, and the Third Circuit ruled that the FTC could proceed — even without a specific cybersecurity statute.

Wyndham's Specific Security Failures (FTC Allegations)

  • Stored payment card data in plain text with no encryption
  • Used easily guessable default usernames and passwords on property management systems
  • Failed to use readily available security measures (firewalls) on property servers
  • Allowed property management systems to connect to Wyndham's corporate network without appropriate safeguards
  • Failed to follow its own published privacy policy claiming to use "industry-standard practices"
  • Did not implement adequate intrusion detection systems despite prior breaches

Post-Wyndham Standard

After Wyndham, the FTC published guidance on what constitutes "reasonable cybersecurity." The FTC's consent orders now function as de facto cybersecurity regulation for any company subject to FTC jurisdiction. Evaluate: is consent-order-based cybersecurity regulation (fact-specific, reactive, case-by-case) better or worse than prescriptive statutory standards (e.g., specific encryption requirements)? Who benefits from each approach?

The HIPAA Security Rule's Three Safeguard Categories

The HIPAA Security Rule (45 CFR Part 164) requires covered entities and business associates to implement administrative, physical, and technical safeguards for electronic Protected Health Information (ePHI). Safeguards are classified as "required" or "addressable" — addressable does not mean optional; it means implement or document why an equivalent measure is in place.

Scenario: Riverside Community Health System

Organization Profile

A 3-hospital system with 12 outpatient clinics, 4,200 employees, and 280,000 active patients. They have implemented the following: EHR system (Epic) with role-based access, encrypted laptops, a firewall, antivirus software, and annual HIPAA training for all staff. They have NOT implemented: a current risk assessment (last one: 2019), MFA for remote access to the EHR, a formal incident response plan, physical access controls on server rooms (badge readers are "broken"), or Business Associate Agreements with their IT vendor and outsourced billing company.

SEC Cybersecurity Rules — Draft Form 8-K Disclosure
Pending

SEC Cybersecurity Disclosure Rules (Effective December 2023)

Under the SEC's 2023 rules, public companies must disclose "material" cybersecurity incidents on Form 8-K within 4 business days of determining the incident is material. "Materiality" uses the standard securities law definition: information that a reasonable investor would consider important in making an investment decision. Companies also make annual disclosures about their cybersecurity risk management programs on Form 10-K.

The 4-Day Material Incident Disclosure Rule

  • Trigger: Determination of materiality (not discovery) — but you must make the determination promptly
  • Required disclosures: Nature, scope, timing of the incident; material impact or likely impact
  • NOT required: Specific technical details that would impair investigation or remediation
  • Delay permitted: DOJ can request up to 30 days (plus renewals) delay if disclosure would harm national security or public safety
  • Item 1.05: The new 8-K item specifically for cybersecurity incidents

Disclosure Scenario: QuantumPay Inc.

Incident Facts

QuantumPay (NYSE: QPY) is a publicly traded payment processing company with $2.1B annual revenue. On November 15, they discovered that attackers accessed their payment processing systems for 18 days (October 28 – November 14). 3.2 million merchant transaction records were exfiltrated including business banking credentials. No consumer credit card data was taken. Legal, security, and finance teams met on November 17 and determined the incident is material. November 17 + 4 business days = November 23 filing deadline.
