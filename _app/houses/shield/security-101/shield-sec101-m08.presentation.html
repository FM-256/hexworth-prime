Governance and Compliance

Security governance provides the strategic direction, authority, and accountability structure for an organization's security program. Compliance demonstrates that the program meets regulatory and contractual obligations. Neither replaces the other — both are required.

Topics Covered

Security governance structure, NIST CSF and ISO 27001 frameworks, risk management, security policies, auditing, and the relationship between compliance and security.

Why It Matters

Without governance, security is reactive and uncoordinated. Without compliance, organizations face regulatory fines, contract penalties, and reputational damage. Together, they make security sustainable.

Security Governance Structure

Governance defines who is responsible for security decisions, how accountability is enforced, and how security aligns with business objectives. Without clear governance, security investments are undirected.

Board and Executive Level

Board of Directors sets risk appetite. CEO/C-suite has ultimate accountability. CISO owns the security program and reports to CEO or Board (increasingly). Security must be a board-level agenda item, not just an IT concern.

CISO Responsibilities

Define security strategy aligned to business risk. Manage security program budget and resources. Own the Information Security Policy. Report security posture to leadership. Oversee compliance, audit, and incident response programs.

Three Lines of Defense

1st Line: Business units own and manage risk in their operations.

2nd Line: Risk management and security functions provide oversight and frameworks.

3rd Line: Internal audit provides independent assurance.

NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0

The NIST CSF is a voluntary framework providing a common language and systematic methodology for managing cybersecurity risk. CSF 2.0 (2024) adds a sixth core function and expands applicability beyond critical infrastructure.

Govern (NEW in 2.0)

Establish cybersecurity strategy, policies, and accountability. Defines the organizational context, roles, and oversight for the entire security program. The foundation all other functions operate within.

Identify

Asset management, risk assessment, supply chain risk management. Know what you have and what risks apply to it.

Protect

Implement safeguards: IAM, awareness training, data security, platform security, technology controls.

Detect

Anomalies and events, continuous monitoring, detection processes. Find threats before they escalate.

Respond

Incident response planning, communications, analysis, mitigation. Handle incidents with structured process.

Recover

Recover planning, improvements, communications. Restore operations and incorporate lessons learned.

ISO/IEC 27001

ISO 27001 is the international standard for Information Security Management Systems (ISMS). Unlike NIST CSF, ISO 27001 certification is a formal, third-party audited process providing verifiable assurance to stakeholders.

ISMS Concept

An ISMS is a systematic approach to managing sensitive information using people, processes, and technology. ISO 27001 defines the requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an ISMS.

Annex A Controls

ISO 27001:2022 Annex A contains 93 controls organized into 4 themes: Organizational (37), People (8), Physical (14), Technological (34). Organizations select applicable controls based on risk assessment results.

Certification Process

Stage 1 audit: documentation review. Stage 2 audit: evidence review and on-site assessment. Annual surveillance audits. Full recertification every 3 years. Conducted by accredited third-party certification bodies.

ISO 27001 vs NIST CSF

NIST CSF: free, flexible, non-certifiable, US government-preferred, process-oriented. ISO 27001: cost to certify, more prescriptive, international, certifiable (provides third-party assurance), often required by enterprise contracts and EU regulations. Many organizations align to NIST CSF internally and certify to ISO 27001 externally.

Risk Management

Risk is the combination of the likelihood that a threat will exploit a vulnerability and the impact of that exploitation. Risk management is the systematic process of identifying, assessing, prioritizing, and treating risks.

Risk Formula

Risk = Threat x Vulnerability x Impact

Threat: an event or actor that could cause harm. Vulnerability: a weakness that could be exploited. Impact: the damage caused if the threat is realized. Reducing any one component reduces overall risk.

Risk Treatment Options

Accept

Acknowledge the risk and take no action. Appropriate when cost of mitigation exceeds potential impact. Requires documented, authorized risk acceptance from appropriate risk owner.

Mitigate (Reduce)

Implement controls to reduce likelihood or impact. The most common treatment. Examples: patching, MFA, network segmentation.

Transfer

Shift financial impact to a third party. Cyber insurance. Contractual liability transfer (SOW/MSA). Does not reduce the risk itself — only the financial consequence.

Avoid

Eliminate the risk by not engaging in the risky activity. Example: not collecting PII you don't need (eliminates breach exposure). Most effective but often not operationally feasible.

Risk Assessment Methods

Risk assessments formalize the identification and evaluation of risks. They provide the evidence base for security investment decisions and governance reporting.

Quantitative Assessment

Uses financial figures. Requires asset valuation and threat frequency data. More rigorous but time-intensive.

Key Quantitative Terms

AV (Asset Value): Dollar value of the asset.

EF (Exposure Factor): % of asset value lost per incident.

SLE = AV x EF: Single Loss Expectancy.

ARO: Annualized Rate of Occurrence (times/year).

ALE = SLE x ARO: Annualized Loss Expectancy. The annual cost of not treating the risk. Justify control spend against ALE.

Security Audits and Assessments

Audits and assessments measure the gap between stated requirements and actual practice. They provide the objective evidence base for governance reporting and compliance attestation.

Internal Audit

Conducted by internal audit team. Validates that controls are operating as documented. Reports to the board/audit committee. Provides first-party assurance. Annual cycle typical; continuous for critical controls.

External Audit

Conducted by independent third party. Required for ISO 27001 certification, SOC 2 attestation, PCI QSA assessments. Provides third-party assurance valued by customers and regulators. Results often must be shared with stakeholders.

Penetration Testing

Authorized simulated attack to find exploitable vulnerabilities before attackers do. Scope defined in Rules of Engagement (ROE). Types: black box (no prior knowledge), white box (full knowledge), gray box (partial knowledge). Annual minimum; quarterly for high-risk environments.

Vulnerability Assessment vs Pentest

Vulnerability assessment: identifies vulnerabilities (what is there). Penetration test: exploits vulnerabilities to prove real-world impact (what an attacker can do with what is there). Both are needed; they answer different questions.

Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery

Business Continuity Planning (BCP) and Disaster Recovery (DR) ensure an organization can maintain and restore operations following a disruptive event — whether a cyberattack, natural disaster, or infrastructure failure.

BCP vs DR

BCP: Broad plan for continuing business operations during and after a disaster. Covers people, processes, facilities, and technology. Business-process focused.

DR: Specific technical plan for recovering IT systems and data. A subset of BCP. Technology-focused.

RTO and RPO

RTO (Recovery Time Objective): Maximum time systems can be down before unacceptable business impact. Drives infrastructure investment (hot/warm/cold standby).

RPO (Recovery Point Objective): Maximum acceptable data loss measured in time. Drives backup frequency and replication strategy.

Recovery Site Types

Hot site: Fully equipped, running systems. Failover in minutes. Highest cost.

Warm site: Infrastructure in place, not actively running. Failover in hours.

Cold site: Physical space, no equipment. Failover in days/weeks. Lowest cost.

Cloud DR: Spin up infrastructure on-demand. Cost-efficient for many organizations.

Test Your DR Plan

An untested DR plan is not a plan — it is an assumption. Tabletop exercises, functional tests, and full failover tests must be conducted regularly. Ransomware victims who discover their DR plan fails during an active incident face catastrophic consequences.

Third-Party and Supply Chain Risk

Modern organizations rely on hundreds of vendors, suppliers, and service providers. Each represents an extension of your attack surface. Supply chain attacks target this dependency chain.

Third-Party Risk Program

Inventory all vendors. Risk-tier them by data access and criticality. Pre-contract security assessment (questionnaire, SOC 2 review). Contractual security requirements. Ongoing monitoring. Off-boarding and access revocation.

Supply Chain Attacks

Attackers compromise a vendor to reach the vendor's customers. SolarWinds (Orion update backdoor), Kaseya VSA (ransomware via MSP platform), and CCleaner malware insertion demonstrate the scale of supply chain risk.

Software Bill of Materials (SBOM)

A formal inventory of all software components and dependencies in an application. US Executive Order 14028 requires SBOMs for software sold to the federal government. Enables rapid identification of exposure when a component vulnerability is disclosed (e.g., Log4Shell).

Security Culture and Awareness

Technology and process cannot compensate for a workforce that does not understand or value security. Security awareness programs transform the human element from the weakest link to the strongest layer of defense.

Effective Awareness Programs

Role-based training (not one-size-fits-all). Short, engaging content over annual lectures. Simulated phishing with remediation training. Measurable outcomes. Security champions network embedded in business units. Leadership visible security commitment.

Key Training Topics

  • Phishing and social engineering recognition
  • Password hygiene and MFA use
  • Data handling and classification
  • Incident reporting procedures
  • Physical security (tailgating, clean desk)
  • Safe use of AI tools and data sharing

Measuring Effectiveness

Phishing simulation click rates (track over time). Training completion rates. Incident reporting rates (higher = better security culture). Compliance violations per employee. Security culture surveys.

Security Champions

Embed security champions — motivated employees with a security interest — in each business unit and development team. They amplify security culture, provide local expertise, and give security teams a trusted network throughout the organization.

Module 08 — Review Questions

Q1: What is the difference between risk mitigation and risk transfer, and when would you choose transfer over mitigation?

Risk mitigation implements controls to reduce likelihood or impact. Risk transfer shifts the financial consequence to a third party (e.g., cyber insurance) without reducing the technical risk. Choose transfer when the cost of mitigation exceeds the expected financial loss (ALE), or when the risk involves low-frequency, high-impact events where insurance provides more cost-effective coverage than controls.

Q2: What does ALE represent and how is it used in a security investment decision?

ALE (Annualized Loss Expectancy) = SLE x ARO. It represents the expected financial loss per year from a specific risk. If a control costs $50,000/year and reduces ALE by $200,000/year, the investment has a positive ROI and is justified. If the control costs more than the ALE reduction, the investment may not be economically rational.

Q3: What is the key difference between ISO 27001 and NIST CSF, and when would an organization pursue ISO 27001 certification?

NIST CSF is flexible, voluntary, non-certifiable, and US-preferred. ISO 27001 is prescriptive, internationally recognized, and certifiable via third-party audit. Pursue ISO 27001 when customers or regulators require independent assurance (EU markets, enterprise contracts with audit rights, financial sector), or when competitive differentiation from certification justifies the cost.

Q4: Why is a penetration test different from and complementary to a vulnerability assessment?

A vulnerability assessment identifies vulnerabilities — it answers "what is present." A penetration test actively exploits vulnerabilities to demonstrate real-world impact — it answers "what can an attacker actually do." A vulnerability assessment alone cannot show if a found vulnerability is actually exploitable in your environment or what an attacker could access after exploitation.

Q5: Why are SBOM (Software Bill of Materials) important for supply chain risk management?

An SBOM is a complete inventory of every component in a software application. When a critical vulnerability is disclosed in a component (like Log4Shell in Log4j), organizations with SBOMs can immediately query which applications use the affected component. Without an SBOM, the same task requires months of manual investigation across every codebase.
