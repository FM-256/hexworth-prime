Incident Report FW-402. A hospital decommissions 200 workstations and donates them to a local school district. IT "formats" the drives before shipping. A curious 14-year-old student runs a free data recovery tool and recovers 47,000 patient records including diagnoses, Social Security numbers, and insurance information. A format does not destroy data -- it removes the file system index while leaving all data intact on the platters. The hospital faces a $2.8 million HIPAA fine and a class-action lawsuit. Proper data destruction would have cost $4 per drive.

NIST 800-88: Media Sanitization
The definitive federal standard for data sanitization. Defines three levels of sanitization based on the sensitivity of the data and the intended disposition of the media.
Clear
Overwrite all user-addressable storage with a fixed pattern (zeros, ones, or random data). Protects against simple data recovery tools. Suitable when the media stays within the organization. A single-pass overwrite is sufficient for Clear level per current NIST guidance.
Purge
Makes data recovery infeasible using state-of-the-art laboratory techniques. Methods: cryptographic erase (destroy the encryption key for self-encrypting drives), degaussing (for magnetic media), or vendor-specific secure erase commands. Used when media leaves organizational control.
Destroy
Physical destruction of the media so that data recovery is impossible regardless of effort or technology. Methods: shredding, disintegration, incineration, melting. Required for the most sensitive data (classified, critical infrastructure) or when other methods cannot be verified.

Overwriting
Writing new data over the entire drive surface to replace original data. The software-based approach to data sanitization.
Single-Pass vs Multi-Pass
Older guidance (DoD 5220.22-M) specified 3 or 7 passes. Modern NIST 800-88r1 confirms that a single pass of zeros is sufficient for modern HDDs. The density of modern drives makes residual magnetic recovery from a single overwrite infeasible with any known technology.
Tools
DBAN (Darik's Boot and Nuke): free, bootable, multi-platform. Eraser (Windows): scheduled overwrites of files and free space. nwipe: command-line successor to DBAN. Enterprise: Blancco Drive Eraser with tamper-proof certificates of erasure for compliance audits.
Limitations
Overwriting does NOT work reliably on SSDs because of wear-leveling, over-provisioned areas, and TRIM. The drive controller remaps blocks transparently, so the overwrite may not reach all physical storage cells. For SSDs, use cryptographic erase or physical destruction instead.

Degaussing
Exposing magnetic media to a powerful magnetic field that randomizes the magnetic domains, rendering stored data unrecoverable.
How Degaussing Works
A degausser generates a magnetic field strong enough to scramble the magnetic orientation of particles on the drive platters or tape. The data becomes random noise. Modern high-coercivity drives require NSA-listed degaussers rated for the specific media type. Consumer magnets are not strong enough.
Key Limitation
Degaussing permanently destroys the drive -- it erases the servo tracks that the drive head uses to find data sectors. The drive cannot be reused after degaussing. Degaussing does NOT work on SSDs because SSDs store data as electrical charges in NAND flash, not magnetic domains.
When to Degauss
Degaussing is appropriate for magnetic HDDs and backup tapes when: the data is highly sensitive, the media will not be reused, and physical destruction is not available. It is a Purge-level method under NIST 800-88. Always verify with a degauss verification tool or follow with physical destruction.
Cost and Equipment
Professional degaussers range from $1,000 to $30,000 depending on the field strength and throughput. NSA-evaluated degaussers are listed on the NSA/CSS EPL (Evaluated Products List). For organizations with high volumes of magnetic media to retire, a degausser is a cost-effective investment versus per-drive destruction services.

Physical Destruction
When data must be irrecoverable regardless of attacker resources, the media must be physically destroyed beyond any possibility of reconstruction.
Shredding
Industrial shredders reduce hard drives to particles no larger than 2mm. This is the most common physical destruction method for organizations. Mobile shredding trucks can shred on-site so media never leaves the premises. NSA requires particles no larger than 2mm for classified media destruction.
Drilling
Drilling multiple holes through HDD platters physically damages the magnetic surfaces. Quick and inexpensive -- a drill press costs under $200. However, drilling is not suitable for SSDs (data is stored in chips, not platters) and undamaged platter sections may still hold recoverable data. Best as a supplement to other methods.
Incineration
Melting the media in a licensed incinerator at temperatures exceeding 1,000 degrees Celsius. Completely destroys all data storage components. Requires a certified disposal facility with environmental permits. Commonly used for large batches of end-of-life media.
Disintegration
A disintegrator reduces media to particles as small as 1/8 inch or smaller using rotating knife mills. Used for the highest-security requirements. NSA-evaluated disintegrators are listed on the EPL. This is the gold standard for classified media destruction in government and defense.

SSD vs HDD: Destruction Differences
SSDs and HDDs store data fundamentally differently. Sanitization methods that work for one may not work for the other.
Why SSDs Are Different
SSDs use wear-leveling algorithms that remap write operations across NAND flash cells. When you "overwrite" a file, the SSD may write to a new cell and mark the old one for later garbage collection. Over-provisioned space (10-28% of total capacity) is never user-addressable but holds previous data. Standard overwrite tools cannot reach all physical cells.
SSD Sanitization Methods
Cryptographic Erase: self-encrypting drives (SED) destroy the encryption key, making all data unreadable instantly. ATA Secure Erase: vendor-specific command that resets all NAND cells. Physical destruction (shredding) is the most reliable. NIST 800-88 recommends cryptographic erase + verification or physical destruction for SSDs.
HDD Sanitization Methods
All methods work: overwriting (single pass of zeros sufficient), degaussing (permanent, drive becomes inoperable), and physical destruction (shredding, drilling, incineration). HDDs are simpler to sanitize because the data storage model is straightforward -- overwrite the platters and the data is gone.
NVMe and Flash Storage
NVMe SSDs, USB flash drives, SD cards, and eMMC storage in phones all use NAND flash with the same wear-leveling challenges. Do not assume that formatting a USB drive or factory-resetting a phone destroys the data. Use cryptographic erase where supported, or physical destruction for guaranteed sanitization.

Secure Disposal Procedures
Data destruction is a process, not a single action. Proper procedures ensure accountability, verification, and compliance at every step.
Asset Inventory
Before destruction, document every device: serial number, asset tag, make/model, storage type (HDD/SSD), capacity, and data classification. You cannot prove you destroyed something if you cannot prove you had it. An accurate inventory is the foundation of accountable disposal.
Sanitization Execution
Perform the appropriate sanitization method based on media type and data sensitivity. Two authorized personnel should witness the destruction process for high-sensitivity media. In-house destruction provides maximum control. Third-party vendors must be vetted and contracted.
Verification
After overwriting: attempt to recover data using forensic tools to confirm sanitization succeeded. After degaussing: use a magnetic field detector. After shredding: visual inspection of particle size. Verification proves the method worked -- without it, you are assuming rather than confirming.

Chain of Custody
Tracking who has physical control of media from decommissioning through destruction. Unbroken chain of custody proves data never left authorized hands.
Chain Documentation
Every transfer of custody must be documented with: date/time, name of person releasing, name of person receiving, reason for transfer, and signatures of both parties. A broken chain (media left unattended in a hallway, handed to an unlisted person) creates an accountability gap that auditors will flag.
Secure Storage
Media awaiting destruction must be stored in a locked, access-controlled area. Not a shelf in an open office. Not a box by the loading dock. A locked cage or cabinet with limited key holders. Drives waiting to be destroyed still contain live data and must be treated as sensitive assets until destruction is confirmed.
Third-Party Transport
If using a destruction vendor, require bonded and insured transport with GPS-tracked vehicles, locked containers, and background-checked drivers. The vendor should provide real-time tracking from pickup to destruction. Never allow unlocked bins of hard drives to sit on a loading dock for pickup.
Regulatory Requirement
HIPAA, PCI-DSS, GDPR, and most compliance frameworks require documented chain of custody for media disposal. Failure to maintain chain of custody documentation is a compliance violation independent of whether data was actually exposed. The documentation proves due diligence.
Slide 9 of 14
Certificates of Destruction
Formal documentation proving that specific media was destroyed using a specific method on a specific date by an authorized party.
Required Information
Organization name, date of destruction, method used (shred, degauss, overwrite), list of all media destroyed (serial numbers), name and signature of the destruction operator, name and signature of the witness, and the vendor's certification/license information if third-party.
Why Certificates Matter
A certificate of destruction is your legal proof that data was destroyed in compliance with regulations. Without it, if a drive surfaces later with recoverable data, you have no defense. With it, you have documented evidence of due diligence. Auditors and regulators will ask for these certificates by serial number.
Retention
Store certificates of destruction for the same retention period required for the data they represent. If HIPAA requires 6 years of record retention, keep the destruction certificates for at least 6 years. Digital copies should be stored in a secure, backed-up document management system.
Vendor Due Diligence
Before contracting a destruction vendor, verify: NAID AAA certification, insurance coverage, background checks for employees, documented chain of custody procedures, and willingness to provide per-item certificates with serial numbers. A vendor that cannot provide itemized certificates should not be trusted with your media.

Cloud Data Deletion
Deleting data from cloud services is not the same as destroying a physical drive. You are relying on the provider's deletion processes.
The Cloud Deletion Problem
When you delete a file from OneDrive, S3, or Google Drive, you are sending a deletion request to the provider. The data may persist in: backup snapshots, replication copies across data centers, transaction logs, and recycle bins with retention periods. You cannot physically verify that all copies have been destroyed.
Contractual Guarantees
Your cloud service agreement should specify: data deletion timeline after account termination, how backup copies are handled, geographic data residency, and the sanitization method used when drives are decommissioned by the provider. Major providers (AWS, Azure, GCP) publish whitepapers detailing their media sanitization processes.
Crypto-Shredding
Encrypt data before uploading to the cloud using customer-managed keys. When you need to "delete" the data, destroy the encryption key. The encrypted data remains on the provider's storage but is mathematically unrecoverable without the key. This gives you control over destruction regardless of the provider's deletion timeline.
Right to Erasure (GDPR)
GDPR Article 17 gives individuals the right to request erasure of personal data. Organizations must be able to locate and delete all copies of an individual's data across all systems -- including cloud backups. Without a data inventory and retention policy, GDPR erasure requests become operationally impossible.

Data Classification and Disposal
The destruction method must match the sensitivity of the data. Not all data requires the same level of destruction.
Public / Low Sensitivity
Marketing materials, public reports, non-sensitive internal documents. Clear-level sanitization (single-pass overwrite) is sufficient. Media can be reused or recycled after clearing. Standard format + overwrite with any disk utility meets the requirement.
Internal / Moderate
Internal business data, employee information, financial reports. Purge-level sanitization (cryptographic erase, degaussing, or ATA secure erase) is appropriate. Media reuse is acceptable after verified purge. Documentation of the sanitization method and verification is required.
Confidential / High
PII, PHI, payment card data, trade secrets, legal documents. Purge or Destroy depending on whether the media will be reused internally or disposed of. Certificates of destruction required. Chain of custody documented. Third-party destruction vendors must be NAID-certified.
Top Secret / Critical
Classified government data, critical infrastructure information, military systems. Physical Destroy is the only acceptable method. NSA-evaluated equipment required. Two-person integrity (both persons verify destruction). No media reuse under any circumstances.

Paper and Other Media
Data destruction is not limited to hard drives. Paper documents, optical media, magnetic tapes, and mobile devices all require proper disposal.
Paper Documents
Cross-cut shredders (DIN 66399 Level P-4 or higher) for confidential documents. Strip-cut shredders can be reconstructed. Use locked shred bins with scheduled pickup by a bonded vendor. Burn bags for highest-sensitivity environments. Never put sensitive documents in regular recycling bins.
Optical Media (CD/DVD/Blu-ray)
Purpose-built optical media shredders reduce discs to small fragments. Scratching a disc does NOT prevent data recovery -- forensic tools can read through scratches. Industrial disc shredders cost under $500 and are the proper method. For small volumes, dedicated optical media destroyers are available.
Backup Tapes
LTO and other magnetic tape formats contain massive amounts of data (up to 45 TB per cartridge). Degaussing or incineration are the primary methods. Overwriting is possible but time-consuming given the capacity. Tape destruction must be tracked carefully -- a single forgotten tape in a warehouse can contain years of backup data.
USB and Flash Media
USB drives, SD cards, and CompactFlash cards use NAND flash -- same SSD sanitization challenges apply. Physical destruction (shredding or cutting the chip) is the most reliable method. For enterprise volumes, flash media shredders reduce drives to particles. Never donate or sell used USB drives without verified destruction.

Building a Disposal Policy
A written, enforced data disposal policy ensures consistent handling regardless of who performs the destruction.
Policy Components
Data classification scheme (what level of data is on which media). Approved sanitization methods per media type and classification level. Chain of custody requirements. Certification and documentation requirements. Roles and responsibilities. Third-party vendor requirements. Retention periods for destruction records.
Training
All employees must know that "deleting" a file and emptying the recycle bin does not destroy data. IT staff must know the correct sanitization method for each media type. Leadership must understand the legal and financial consequences of improper disposal. Annual refresher training and new-hire onboarding.
Enforcement and Audit
Periodic audits of the disposal process: are chain-of-custody forms complete, are certificates of destruction on file for all decommissioned assets, does the asset inventory reconcile with destruction records. Any missing drive is a potential data breach until proven otherwise.
The $4 That Saves $4 Million
Professional per-drive destruction costs $4-10. The average data breach costs $4.45 million (IBM 2023). The cost-benefit calculation is not ambiguous. Proper data destruction is one of the cheapest security controls available relative to the risk it mitigates.

Week 4 Summary: Data Destruction
1 NIST 800-88 defines three levels: Clear (overwrite), Purge (crypto-erase/degauss), Destroy (physical). Match the method to data sensitivity.
2 Formatting does NOT destroy data. It removes the file index. Recovery tools can rebuild the data from the intact platters in minutes.
3 Overwriting (single-pass zeros) works for HDDs but is unreliable on SSDs due to wear-leveling and over-provisioned space.
4 Degaussing destroys magnetic media (HDD/tape) but does NOT work on SSDs. The degaussed drive is permanently inoperable.
5 Physical destruction (shredding to 2mm, incineration, disintegration) is the most reliable method for all media types.
6 SSD sanitization: use cryptographic erase (self-encrypting drives), ATA Secure Erase, or physical destruction. Standard overwrite is insufficient.
7 Chain of custody must be documented from decommissioning through destruction: who had it, when, where, and why at every step.
8 Certificates of destruction are legally binding compliance evidence. Retain for the same period as the data they represent.
9 Cloud deletion relies on provider processes. Use crypto-shredding (encrypt with customer key, then destroy key) for guaranteed deletion.
10 Destruction costs $4-10 per drive. Average breach costs $4.45M. A written disposal policy with training and auditing is non-negotiable.
