Wireless Fundamentals
Radio frequency signals travel through walls, floors, and parking lots. The network perimeter is invisible.
802.11 Standards
802.11a (5GHz, 54Mbps), 802.11b (2.4GHz, 11Mbps), 802.11g (2.4GHz, 54Mbps), 802.11n/Wi-Fi 4 (dual-band, 600Mbps), 802.11ac/Wi-Fi 5 (5GHz, 6.9Gbps), 802.11ax/Wi-Fi 6.
Frequency Bands
2.4 GHz: longer range, more interference, 3 non-overlapping channels (1, 6, 11). 5 GHz: shorter range, less interference, 23 non-overlapping channels. Wi-Fi 6E adds 6 GHz.
SSID & BSSID
SSID: network name visible to clients. BSSID: MAC address of the access point. Multiple APs can share one SSID for seamless roaming. Hidden SSIDs still broadcast in probe responses.

WEP -- Wired Equivalent Privacy
The first wireless encryption standard. Broken in 2001. Never use it.
How WEP Works
Uses RC4 stream cipher with a 24-bit Initialization Vector (IV). The IV is too short -- it repeats after about 5,000 packets. Once repeated, the key can be mathematically derived.
Why WEP Is Broken
The 24-bit IV creates only 16.7 million possible values. On a busy network, IVs repeat within hours. Tools like aircrack-ng can crack WEP in under 5 minutes with enough captured packets.
WEP Key Sizes Are Misleading
"64-bit WEP" uses a 40-bit key + 24-bit IV. "128-bit WEP" uses a 104-bit key + 24-bit IV. The IV weakness makes key length irrelevant. A 128-bit WEP key is no harder to crack than a 64-bit one because the attack targets the IV, not the key.

WPA -- Wi-Fi Protected Access
A temporary fix for WEP. Better, but still vulnerable.
TKIP (Temporal Key Integrity Protocol)
Replaced WEP's static key with per-packet keys. 48-bit IV instead of 24-bit. Message Integrity Check (MIC) prevents packet tampering. Still uses RC4 underneath.
WPA Vulnerabilities
TKIP has been partially compromised. WPA-PSK is vulnerable to offline dictionary attacks if the passphrase is weak. The 4-way handshake can be captured and cracked offline.
WPA Was a Stopgap
WPA was designed to run on existing WEP hardware with a firmware update. It was never intended as a permanent solution. It improved on WEP significantly but carried forward the RC4 cipher, which limits its long-term security.

WPA2 -- The Current Standard
AES encryption with CCMP. The baseline for any secure wireless network today.
CCMP / AES
Counter Mode with CBC-MAC Protocol. Uses AES-128 encryption -- the same algorithm that protects classified government data. Mathematically secure. No known practical attacks on AES itself.
WPA2-Personal (PSK)
Pre-Shared Key mode. All users share the same passphrase. Simple to configure. Vulnerable to offline dictionary attacks if the passphrase is weak. Use 20+ character passphrases.
WPA2-Enterprise (802.1X)
Each user authenticates individually via RADIUS. Unique encryption keys per session. Supports EAP methods (PEAP, EAP-TLS). The standard for business networks.
KRACK Vulnerability
Key Reinstallation Attack (2017). Exploits the 4-way handshake to reinstall an already-used key. Patched by vendors. Demonstrates that even strong protocols need ongoing maintenance.
Slide 6 of 13
WPA3 -- Next Generation
Addresses WPA2's known weaknesses. Required on all Wi-Fi 6 certified devices.
SAE (Simultaneous Authentication of Equals)
Replaces the PSK 4-way handshake with Dragonfly key exchange. Resistant to offline dictionary attacks even with a weak password. Each session gets a unique key.
Forward Secrecy
If the passphrase is compromised later, previously captured traffic cannot be decrypted. Each session has independent keys. WPA2 does not have forward secrecy.
192-bit Security Suite
WPA3-Enterprise adds an optional 192-bit security mode aligned with CNSA (Commercial National Security Algorithm) requirements. For government and high-security environments.
Enhanced Open (OWE)
Opportunistic Wireless Encryption. Encrypts traffic on open networks (coffee shops, airports) without requiring a password. No more plaintext Wi-Fi on public networks.
Slide 7 of 13
Enterprise Authentication
802.1X + RADIUS + EAP: the enterprise wireless authentication stack.
PEAP (Protected EAP)
Creates a TLS tunnel, then authenticates inside it (usually with MSCHAPv2). Only the server needs a certificate. Most common enterprise EAP method. Supported by all major OSes.
EAP-TLS
Both client and server present certificates. The most secure EAP method. No passwords transmitted. Requires a PKI infrastructure to issue and manage client certificates.
RADIUS Server
Central authentication server. Validates credentials against Active Directory or LDAP. Issues per-user encryption keys. FreeRADIUS (open source) or NPS (Windows) are common deployments.

Wireless Threats
Wireless attacks exploit the open nature of RF signals. Anyone within range can listen.
Evil Twin
Attacker creates a fake AP with the same SSID as a legitimate network. Clients connect automatically. All traffic passes through the attacker's device. Credential harvesting, MITM, DNS manipulation.
Deauthentication Attack
Sends forged deauth frames to disconnect clients from the real AP. Forces clients to reconnect -- potentially to an evil twin. Used to capture the WPA 4-way handshake for offline cracking.
Wardriving
Driving around with a laptop and antenna scanning for wireless networks. Maps SSIDs, encryption types, and signal strength. Identifies vulnerable networks (WEP, open, default passwords).
Rogue Access Point
An unauthorized AP connected to the corporate network. Could be an employee's personal hotspot or a malicious device. Bypasses all network perimeter controls. WIDS detects these.
802.11w -- Management Frame Protection
WPA3 mandates Protected Management Frames (PMF). This prevents deauthentication and disassociation attacks by authenticating management frames. WPA2 supports PMF optionally. Enable it on every AP that supports it.

SSID Management & MAC Filtering
Minor deterrents, not real security. Understand what they do and what they do not do.
Hidden SSID
Disabling SSID broadcast stops the AP from announcing itself. Clients must know the exact SSID to connect. Security value: near zero. SSIDs appear in probe requests and are trivially captured.
MAC Address Filtering
Whitelist of allowed MAC addresses. Only permitted devices can associate. Security value: low. MAC addresses are broadcast in the clear and can be spoofed in seconds with one command.
SSID Naming Best Practices
Do not include the organization name, building number, or department. Generic SSIDs reveal less to wardrivers. Separate SSIDs for guest vs corporate with different VLANs and policies.
Guest Network Isolation
Guest SSID on a separate VLAN with no access to internal resources. Captive portal for authentication. Bandwidth throttling. Content filtering. Guest traffic exits directly to the internet.
Slide 10 of 13
Wireless IDS/IPS
Detecting rogue APs, evil twins, and deauth attacks requires dedicated wireless monitoring.
Rogue AP Detection
WIDS sensors scan all channels for unauthorized access points. Compares detected BSSIDs against an approved list. Alerts on unknown APs connected to the wired network.
Deauth Flood Detection
Monitors for excessive deauthentication frames -- a strong indicator of an active attack. Can trigger automatic countermeasures like blacklisting the source MAC.
Containment Actions
WIPS can actively jam a rogue AP by sending deauth frames to its clients (controversial, legal in enterprise environments). Can also alert security for physical investigation.
Slide 11 of 13
Wireless Site Survey
Before deploying any wireless network, survey the physical space.
Pre-Deployment Survey
Map the physical space. Identify walls, floors, and interference sources (microwaves, Bluetooth). Determine AP placement for coverage without bleeding signal outside the building.
Heat Mapping
Software generates visual coverage maps showing signal strength across the facility. Red = strong, blue = weak. Identifies dead zones and overlap areas that cause co-channel interference.
Channel Planning
Adjacent APs should use non-overlapping channels (1, 6, 11 for 2.4 GHz). Overlap causes co-channel interference that degrades performance. 5 GHz has more channels to work with.
Power Levels
Reduce AP transmit power to minimize signal leakage outside the building. An attacker in the parking lot should not be able to associate. Balance coverage needs vs security exposure.

Wireless Best Practices
A checklist for securing any wireless deployment.
Use WPA2/WPA3 Only
Disable WEP and WPA/TKIP entirely. Use AES/CCMP or SAE. If a device does not support WPA2 minimum, it does not belong on the network.
Enterprise Auth (802.1X)
Use RADIUS-based authentication for corporate networks. Individual credentials, per-user keys, revocable access. PSK is acceptable only for small/home networks.
Segment with VLANs
Guest Wi-Fi on a separate VLAN. IoT devices on a separate VLAN. Corporate workstations on their own VLAN. Each VLAN has its own firewall rules and cannot talk to the others.
Monitor and Audit
Deploy WIDS/WIPS. Log all wireless events. Review logs for rogue APs, deauth floods, and unusual association patterns. Conduct quarterly wireless assessments.
Change Default AP Credentials
Every access point ships with a default admin username and password. "admin/admin" or "admin/password" gives full control. Change it immediately. Disable remote management interfaces. Use HTTPS for AP administration.
Slide 13 of 13  |  Summary
Wireless Security -- Key Takeaways
Wireless signals do not respect walls. Your security must respect that fact.
1 WEP is broken. WPA/TKIP is deprecated. WPA2/AES is the minimum. WPA3/SAE is the target.
2 WPA2-Enterprise (802.1X + RADIUS) provides per-user authentication and unique session keys. PSK is for home use only.
3 WPA3 adds SAE (resistant to offline dictionary attacks), forward secrecy, and Enhanced Open (OWE) for public networks.
4 Evil twin attacks exploit SSID trust. Deauth attacks force reconnection. Wardriving maps vulnerable networks. All three are trivial to execute.
5 Hidden SSIDs and MAC filtering provide minimal security. They are deterrents, not controls. Real security comes from encryption and authentication.
6 WIDS/WIPS detects rogue APs, deauth floods, and evil twins. Deploy sensors and integrate with SIEM for correlation.
7 Site surveys, channel planning, and power level management prevent signal leakage and co-channel interference.
8 Segment wireless traffic with VLANs. Guest, IoT, and corporate devices on separate networks with individual firewall policies.
