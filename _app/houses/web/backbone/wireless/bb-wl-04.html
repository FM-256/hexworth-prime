The Physics of RF Propagation

Radio frequency signals propagate as electromagnetic waves and attenuate with distance according to the inverse square law. In free space, signal power decreases by 6 dB every time distance doubles (because power spreads over a sphere whose area grows with the square of the radius). Real-world indoor environments add absorption by walls, floors, and furniture, reflection creating multipath, and diffraction around obstacles.

Free Space Path Loss (FSPL)

FSPL (dB) = 20*log10(d) + 20*log10(f) + 20*log10(4pi/c) where d is distance in meters and f is frequency in Hz. Simplified: FSPL(dB) = 20*log10(d_m) + 20*log10(f_GHz) + 92.4. At 5 GHz vs 2.4 GHz, the higher frequency adds approximately 6.2 dB of additional path loss for the same distance -- 5 GHz propagates less far in free space but provides more non-overlapping channels.

FSPL Comparison -- 2.4 GHz vs 5 GHz vs 6 GHz at 10 meters
# FSPL = 20*log10(d) + 20*log10(f_GHz) + 92.4

2.4 GHz at 10m:  20*log10(10) + 20*log10(2.4) + 92.4
                 = 20 + 7.6 + 92.4 = 60.0 dB

5.0 GHz at 10m:  20*log10(10) + 20*log10(5.0) + 92.4
                 = 20 + 14.0 + 92.4 = 66.4 dB

6.0 GHz at 10m:  20*log10(10) + 20*log10(6.0) + 92.4
                 = 20 + 15.6 + 92.4 = 68.0 dB

Conclusion: 6 GHz has ~8 dB more path loss than 2.4 GHz at the same distance.
AP transmit power and client sensitivity determine whether this matters.

Signal Metrics: RSSI, SNR, and Noise Floor

RSSI (Received Signal Strength Indicator) Measured in dBm. The absolute received signal power at the receiver. Typical useful range: -30 dBm (excellent, very close) to -85 dBm (minimum for connectivity). Below -90 dBm most APs cannot maintain a connection.
Noise Floor The ambient RF noise level at the receiver, measured in dBm. Typical indoor noise floor: -95 to -100 dBm on 2.4 GHz, -100 to -105 dBm on 5 GHz. Higher interference raises the noise floor, requiring higher RSSI for the same SNR.
SNR (Signal-to-Noise Ratio) SNR (dB) = RSSI (dBm) - Noise Floor (dBm). The metric that actually drives MCS selection. SNR of 25 dB supports MCS 7 (256-QAM). SNR of 35+ dB enables MCS 10/11 (1024-QAM). The AP and client exchange SNR reports via beamforming feedback.
Multipath Fading Reflected signal copies arrive at the receiver with different time delays and phases. Constructive interference can momentarily boost signal; destructive interference creates nulls. OFDM handles multipath by making each sub-carrier symbol period longer than the delay spread.

6 GHz Indoor Power Limits

The FCC (in the US) allows two modes for 6 GHz: Standard Power requires AFC (Automated Frequency Coordination) with a geo-database check before transmitting, permitting higher EIRP. Low Power Indoor (LPI) allows indoor operation without AFC at lower power (max 5 dBm/MHz). Most current Wi-Fi 6E deployments use LPI mode. Standard Power mode requires AFC coordination and is more complex to deploy but provides longer range for outdoor and enterprise campus scenarios.

Channel Planning Fundamentals

Co-channel interference (CCI) occurs when two APs use the same channel within range of each other. Since CSMA/CA requires devices on the same channel to take turns, CCI directly reduces throughput for all clients in the overlapping coverage area. The goal of channel planning is to ensure overlapping APs use different channels while minimizing the number of channels needed to cover the building.

For 2.4 GHz deployments, channels 1, 6, and 11 are the only non-overlapping 20 MHz channels in the Americas. A three-cell repeating pattern (1-6-11-1-6-11) is the maximum non-interfering layout. Any design that uses channels 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, or 10 causes overlapping adjacent-channel interference, which is worse than CCI because it cannot be managed by CSMA/CA.

Practice Questions

  1. Calculate the free space path loss at 5 GHz for a client that is 15 meters from an AP. If the AP transmits at 20 dBm and the client antenna has 0 dBi gain, what is the approximate received signal power? (Assume free space only, no wall losses.)
  2. An AP shows a client connected at RSSI -72 dBm with a noise floor of -98 dBm. What is the SNR? Based on the SNR table in this module, what MCS index range would you expect, and approximately what PHY rate at 80 MHz with 2 spatial streams?
  3. A 2.4 GHz deployment in a 3-floor office building uses a repeating 1-6-11 channel plan on each floor. However, APs on floors 1 and 3 are visible to each other through the thin concrete flooring. What is the interference situation, and how would you modify the channel plan to account for inter-floor interference?
  4. Why does the 6 GHz band have less interference than 5 GHz despite being at a higher frequency that attenuates more? What specific regulatory and device characteristics contribute to this clean RF environment?

Key Takeaways

  • FSPL increases with frequency: 6 GHz has ~8 dB more path loss than 2.4 GHz at the same distance
  • SNR -- not RSSI -- drives MCS selection; SNR = RSSI minus noise floor
  • 2.4 GHz has only 3 non-overlapping channels (1, 6, 11); 5 GHz has 25; 6 GHz has 59
  • CCI (same channel) can be managed by CSMA/CA; ACI (adjacent channel overlap) cannot -- avoid ACI always
  • Multipath is handled by OFDM's guard interval; MIMO exploits multipath as independent signal paths
  • 6 GHz provides the cleanest RF environment due to no legacy devices and AFC power management
  • Minimum useful SNR for association is approximately 10 dB; high-order modulation requires 35+ dB SNR
