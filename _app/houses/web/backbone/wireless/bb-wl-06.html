Mesh Architecture Fundamentals

Wireless mesh extends network coverage to locations where running Ethernet cabling is impractical or cost-prohibitive: outdoor campuses, warehouses with concrete floors, historical buildings, and temporary deployments. In a mesh network, APs form a self-healing multi-hop topology where each node uses its wireless radios to carry both client traffic and backhaul traffic to the rest of the network.

The Cisco Mesh architecture defines two roles: the Root AP (RAP) has a wired Ethernet uplink to the distribution network and connects to the WLC via CAPWAP in the normal fashion. Mesh APs (MAPs) connect to the network wirelessly through other MAPs or RAPs, building a hierarchical tree topology rooted at the RAPs.

Root Access Point (RAP) Has a wired connection to the campus network. Serves as the gateway between the mesh wireless network and the wired infrastructure. All mesh traffic ultimately flows through RAPs. Multiple RAPs provide redundancy and load distribution.
Mesh Access Point (MAP) No wired uplink. Connects wirelessly to a RAP or another MAP (parent node). Serves clients on its access radio while maintaining the backhaul link to its parent. MAPs can also act as parents for other MAPs, forming a multi-hop chain.

Mesh Hop Count and Throughput

Each mesh hop halves effective throughput in single-radio mesh (because the same channel is used for both backhaul reception and client access). In dual-radio mesh where backhaul uses a dedicated radio/band, hop efficiency is much better. As a rule of thumb: limit mesh chains to 3 hops maximum for acceptable client throughput. At hop 4, effective throughput for clients at the chain end can drop below 10% of the RAP's uplink capacity.

Backhaul Band Selection

The choice of backhaul band significantly impacts mesh performance. Mesh APs with multiple radios can dedicate one radio to backhaul and another to client access, eliminating the half-duplex sharing penalty.

Backhaul ConfigurationCapacityInterference RiskClient Impact
Same-band backhaul (2.4 GHz) Low (half-duplex sharing) High -- backhaul competes with clients Severe throughput reduction per hop
5 GHz backhaul, 2.4 GHz client Medium Low -- separate bands Acceptable for basic applications
5 GHz backhaul, 5 GHz client (diff ch) High Low if proper channel separation Good -- backhaul and access on separate channels
6 GHz backhaul (Wi-Fi 6E mesh) Very high Minimal -- 6 GHz has ample channels Excellent -- high-speed backhaul, minimal CCI

Cisco's preferred mesh configuration uses the 5 GHz radio for backhaul and the 2.4 GHz radio for client access in dual-radio APs. In tri-band APs (2.4/5/6 GHz), the 6 GHz radio is ideal for backhaul: it has the most spectrum, the fewest competing devices, and the highest achievable throughput for the dedicated backhaul link between APs in close proximity.

Outdoor RF Propagation

Outdoor wireless deployments face different propagation challenges than indoor deployments. Without walls and floors to limit range, outdoor APs must contend with Fresnel zone obstruction, terrain, vegetation, and weather-related attenuation.

Fresnel Zone An ellipsoid volume around the direct line of sight between two antennas. Obstructions within the first Fresnel zone cause diffraction losses. For minimal loss, 60% of the first Fresnel zone must be clear. At 5 GHz over 500m, the first Fresnel zone radius is approximately 3.9 meters at the midpoint.
Vegetation Attenuation Dense foliage attenuates 5 GHz signals by 10-30 dB per 100 meters of vegetation. 2.4 GHz is less affected (~3-10 dB per 100m). Seasonal variation matters: deciduous trees in leaf vs bare can change path loss by 5-10 dB on the same link. Plan for worst-case (full foliage).
Rain Fade At frequencies below 6 GHz, rain causes minimal attenuation (less than 0.01 dB/km even in heavy rain). Rain fade is significant primarily above 10 GHz (microwave backhaul bands). For 802.11a/n/ac/ax outdoor links, rain is not a primary design concern.
Multipath in Outdoor Ground reflections and building reflections create constructive/destructive interference patterns outdoors. Antenna height and orientation affect the multipath signature. MIMO exploits multipath when multiple reflections exist; near-line-of-sight links with few reflections may not benefit from spatial multiplexing.

Bridge Mode vs Mesh Mode

Bridge mode creates a point-to-point or point-to-multipoint wireless link to extend the wired network across a gap. The radio is dedicated entirely to the bridge link -- no client association. Mesh mode allows the AP to simultaneously serve clients and carry backhaul traffic over the same or dedicated radio.

Directional Antennas for Outdoor Bridges

Outdoor bridge links use directional antennas (Yagi, parabolic dish, or sector panel) with gains of 14-30 dBi. A 30 dBi parabolic dish concentrates all transmit power into a very narrow beam (~2 degrees). This gain extends range and provides interference immunity from off-axis directions. Both ends of the link must be precisely aligned. Even 1 degree of misalignment on a 30 dBi antenna can cause 3-6 dB of link budget loss.

Practice Questions

  1. A warehouse has concrete floors and steel racks making Ethernet cabling impractical. You deploy 1 RAP and 4 MAPs in a linear chain (RAP-MAP1-MAP2-MAP3-MAP4). Each hop is on the same 5 GHz channel (backhaul and client access sharing). If the RAP uplink provides 300 Mbps, estimate the approximate available client throughput at MAP4. What infrastructure change would most improve this?
  2. Calculate the first Fresnel zone radius at the midpoint of a 1 km outdoor 5 GHz link. Is a 2-meter-tall fence at the midpoint a problem? Show your calculation using the formula: r = sqrt(lambda * d1 * d2 / (d1 + d2)) where d1 = d2 = 500m and lambda = c/f.
  3. Your outdoor mesh deployment over a wooded campus works well in January but shows intermittent connectivity in July. Name the RF phenomenon causing this and explain what link budget margin should be designed in to accommodate it.
  4. Compare the appropriate antenna types and configurations for: (a) a 2 km point-to-point bridge between two buildings, and (b) a mesh AP serving clients in a 360-degree outdoor parking lot.

Key Takeaways

  • RAPs have wired uplinks; MAPs are wireless-only and form a self-healing tree topology toward RAPs
  • Same-band backhaul halves throughput per hop; dedicated backhaul radio (tri-band) eliminates this penalty
  • Mesh hop count should be limited to 3 maximum for reasonable end-client throughput
  • Fresnel zone clearance (60% of first zone) is critical for low-loss outdoor point-to-point links
  • Vegetation is the primary outdoor attenuation variable; plan for full-foliage worst case
  • Bridge mode dedicates the radio for fixed infrastructure links; mesh mode serves both clients and backhaul
  • Directional antennas (14-30 dBi) extend outdoor bridge range and provide interference rejection
