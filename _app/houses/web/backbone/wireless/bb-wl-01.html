Why the Standard Keeps Evolving

IEEE 802.11 is not a single protocol -- it is a family of amendments to a base standard first ratified in 1997. Each amendment targets a specific engineering problem: more spectrum, better modulation, smarter channel access, or lower latency. Understanding the generational arc explains why a Wi-Fi 6 AP in a conference room performs so differently from the Wi-Fi 4 AP it replaced.

The Wi-Fi Alliance markets these amendments under consumer-friendly names (Wi-Fi 4, 5, 6, 7) but engineers still reference the IEEE amendment codes. Both naming schemes appear on certification exams and in vendor documentation, so fluency with both is required.

PHY vs MAC Layer Improvements

Each 802.11 amendment modifies either the Physical layer (modulation, coding, channel bonding, antennas) or the MAC layer (frame aggregation, power save, access control), or both. PHY changes determine raw bit rate. MAC changes determine how efficiently that capacity is shared among multiple clients.

PHY Layer Technology Deep Dive

DSSS (Direct Sequence Spread Spectrum) Used by 802.11b. Spreads the signal across the full channel using a chipping code. Robust against narrowband interference but spectrally inefficient. Max 11 Mbps at 22 MHz channel width.
OFDM (Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing) Used from 802.11a onward. Splits the channel into 52 subcarriers, each carrying a portion of the data stream. Resistant to multipath fading. Enabled QAM modulation for higher spectral efficiency.
MIMO (Multiple Input, Multiple Output) Introduced in 802.11n. Uses multiple antennas to transmit simultaneous spatial streams over the same channel. Doubles or quadruples throughput without additional spectrum. Requires matching streams at both ends.
OFDMA (Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiple Access) Introduced in 802.11ax. Subdivides OFDM channels into Resource Units (RUs) and assigns different RUs to different clients simultaneously. Transforms WLAN from time-division to frequency-division shared access.

The transition from OFDM to OFDMA is the most significant architectural change in Wi-Fi history. Before 802.11ax, only one client could transmit per time slot regardless of channel width. OFDMA allows an AP to serve 37 clients simultaneously on a single 80 MHz channel by assigning RUs of 26, 52, 106, or 242 subcarriers per client based on their needs.

QAM Modulation and MCS Index

Quadrature Amplitude Modulation (QAM) encodes multiple bits per symbol by varying both amplitude and phase. Higher QAM orders require better signal-to-noise ratio but deliver more bits per hertz of spectrum.

The Modulation and Coding Scheme (MCS) index combines modulation order, coding rate, and spatial streams into a single index. Vendors and APs report MCS index in management frames and association statistics. MCS 11 in 802.11ax at 80 MHz with 1 spatial stream delivers 1201 Mbps PHY rate.

MAC-Layer Efficiency: Aggregation and CSMA/CA

Raw PHY rates are never achieved in practice because the 802.11 MAC uses Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance (CSMA/CA). Every frame transmission requires:

  1. DIFS (Distributed Inter-Frame Space) wait -- 34 microseconds at 5 GHz
  2. Random backoff window -- 0 to 15 slot times initially
  3. Frame transmission
  4. SIFS wait then ACK from receiver -- 16 microseconds at 5 GHz

A-MPDU (Aggregate MAC Protocol Data Unit), introduced in 802.11n, bundles multiple frames into a single transmission, amortizing the overhead across many frames. Wi-Fi 5 extended maximum A-MPDU size to 1MB. Without aggregation, effective throughput is typically 40-50% of PHY rate. With optimal aggregation, throughput efficiency rises to 65-75%.

Backward Compatibility Tax

When an 802.11b client associates to a mixed-mode 802.11g/n/ac AP, the AP must use Protection mechanisms (CTS-to-self or RTS/CTS) for all OFDM frames. This adds overhead for every transmission on that BSS, degrading throughput for all other clients by 20-40%. Eliminating legacy clients or configuring minimum data rates prevents this penalty.

Channel Planning Across Generations

Channel planning philosophy changed significantly across generations. The 2.4 GHz band offers only 3 non-overlapping 20 MHz channels (1, 6, 11 in North America), creating a fundamental capacity ceiling. The 5 GHz band opened 24+ non-overlapping channels, enabling proper enterprise deployments. Wi-Fi 6E added the 6 GHz band (5.925-7.125 GHz) with 59 additional 20 MHz channels and 7 non-overlapping 160 MHz channels -- a spectrum windfall that eliminated the co-channel interference problems endemic to 5 GHz high-density deployments.

Key Takeaways

  • 802.11 is a family of amendments; each generation addresses a specific capacity or efficiency problem
  • DSSS (802.11b) was replaced by OFDM (802.11a/g) -- a fundamental PHY shift enabling 64-QAM
  • MIMO (802.11n) multiplied capacity by adding spatial streams; channel bonding doubled and quadrupled width
  • 802.11ac (Wi-Fi 5) moved exclusively to 5 GHz, added 256-QAM and downlink MU-MIMO
  • 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6/6E) introduced OFDMA -- the biggest MAC revolution since OFDM at the PHY level
  • 802.11be (Wi-Fi 7) adds MLO and 320 MHz channels; covered in detail in the next module
  • Higher QAM requires higher SNR; 4096-QAM demands near-perfect signal conditions (~40 dB SNR)
  • Backward compatibility costs throughput -- minimum data rate policies eliminate legacy protection overhead
