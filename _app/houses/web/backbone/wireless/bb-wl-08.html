The Wireless Threat Landscape

Wireless networks expose the network perimeter to the air. Unlike wired access where an attacker must physically connect to a port, a malicious actor with a wireless adapter can target network assets from a parking lot, adjacent building, or even a passing vehicle. Wireless Intrusion Detection Systems (WIDS) and Wireless Intrusion Prevention Systems (WIPS) monitor the RF environment for unauthorized devices and attack signatures, alerting on threats and in some deployments automatically responding to them.

WIDS (Detection Only) Passively monitors 802.11 management and data frames for anomalies, unauthorized devices, and attack signatures. Generates alerts for security personnel to investigate. No automated response -- avoids legal and operational risks of active countermeasures.
WIPS (Detection + Prevention) Performs all WIDS functions plus actively responds to detected threats. Primary response is containment via 802.11 deauthentication frame flooding targeting the rogue AP or client. Raises significant legal questions -- see warning below.

Rogue AP Classification

Not all unknown APs are threats. A rogue AP classification system categorizes detected wireless devices by their threat level to avoid alert fatigue from neighboring business SSIDs and employee hotspots.

ClassificationDefinitionThreat LevelTypical Response
Authorized AP Managed AP in the WLC database; known BSSID, known configuration None No action
External AP (Neighbor) Detected AP not in the corporate network; seen by multiple monitors but not on wired network; likely a neighbor's legitimate AP Low Log and monitor; do not contain
Rogue AP (Wired) Unknown AP whose BSSID or connected clients can be detected on the corporate wired network -- plugged into an internal switch port High Locate switch port via MAC table; disable port; physical removal
Evil Twin / Honeypot AP AP advertising the same SSID as a legitimate corporate network; designed to capture credentials Critical WIPS containment (where legal); immediate incident response
Misconfigured AP Authorized AP broadcasting wrong SSID, open security, or on wrong VLAN due to configuration error Medium Remediate configuration; generate alert

Determining "On the Wired Network"

The WLC or WIPS server determines if a rogue AP is on the corporate wired network by checking whether the MAC addresses of clients associated to the rogue AP appear in the corporate Layer 2 tables (ARP cache, switch MAC address tables). If a client associated to an unknown AP appears in the switch MAC table on a corporate VLAN, the rogue AP is bridging into the corporate network -- the highest-risk rogue scenario.

Rogue AP Localization

WIDS uses RSSI reports from multiple monitor mode APs to triangulate the physical location of a rogue device. The RSSI values from three or more sensors with known positions allow the system to calculate the rogue AP's approximate location using trilateration. Cisco's WIPS triangulation typically achieves 5-10 meter accuracy in indoor environments with adequate sensor density.

The floor map in the WLC management interface overlays rogue AP locations, color-coded by threat level, enabling a security team to physically locate and remove unauthorized devices. RSSI-based location accuracy degrades significantly if sensors are too far apart, if walls create severe attenuation in one direction, or if the rogue is near a reflective metal surface.

WIDS Attack Signatures

AttackSignatureFrame Type ObservedImpact
Deauthentication Flood High rate of deauth frames (type 00, subtype 1100) from single source to broadcast or targeted client; spoofed AP BSSID Management: Deauthentication Disconnects clients; enables capture of 4-way handshake on reconnect
Disassociation Flood High rate of disassociation frames; similar to deauth flood but uses different frame subtype Management: Disassociation Same as deauth; also forces re-association probe capture
PMKID Capture Attempt Single EAPOL frame solicitation without completing 4-way handshake; multiple attempts to different APs Data: EAPOL Key frame (Message 1 only) Offline WPA2-PSK password cracking attempt
Probe Flood / Fuzzing Extremely high rate of probe request frames; often with randomized source MACs and empty SSID Management: Probe Request Fingerprinting, network mapping, AP resource exhaustion
Evil Twin Detection AP advertising identical SSID to a known corporate network but with different BSSID, different channel, or detected from an anomalous physical direction Management: Beacon, Probe Response Credential harvesting, MitM attack on clients that auto-connect

WIPS Containment and Legal Risk

WIPS containment works by sending spoofed 802.11 deauthentication frames with the rogue AP's BSSID as the source address to its connected clients. This is technically effective but legally problematic in many jurisdictions. The US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, the UK Computer Misuse Act, and similar legislation may classify wireless containment as unauthorized interference with a computer network -- even when targeting unauthorized rogues. Organizations should obtain legal counsel before enabling automatic containment. The safest posture: detect and alert automatically; contain only on explicit operator approval.

Practice Questions

  1. Your WLC WIPS dashboard shows an unknown AP with SSID "CorpWiFi-Guest" (your organization's guest SSID). Three monitor APs report RSSI values of -62 dBm, -71 dBm, and -68 dBm. The MAC address of the rogue AP does not appear in any corporate switch MAC address table. Classify this device (authorized/external/rogue on wired/evil twin) and describe your investigation and response steps.
  2. A WIDS alert fires: "Deauthentication flood detected from source MAC aa:bb:cc:dd:ee:01 targeting BSSID 00:11:22:33:44:55." The source MAC is not in the corporate AP inventory. Describe the attack goal, how to locate the attacker physically, and what monitoring you would enable to capture the downstream attack steps.
  3. Compare an integrated WIPS deployment (Wi-Fi APs time-slice between serving clients and scanning) versus an overlay WIPS deployment (dedicated monitor-mode APs). For each, describe the tradeoffs in detection coverage, cost, and impact on client service.
  4. 802.11w (Management Frame Protection, MFP) was introduced to defend against deauthentication flood attacks. Explain how 802.11w protects management frames, which WPA generation requires it, and what the client device must support to benefit from it.

Key Takeaways

  • WIDS detects and alerts; WIPS adds active containment via deauthentication spoofing (legally complex)
  • Rogue classification: authorized, external neighbor, rogue on wired network, evil twin -- severity escalates in that order
  • Wired network correlation (MAC in switch tables) is the definitive indicator that a rogue is bridging into corporate infrastructure
  • Deauth flood signature: high-rate deauthentication frames from a non-AP BSSID targeting broadcast or specific clients
  • PMKID attack requires only a single EAPOL message-1 capture -- single-frame anomaly, not a full handshake
  • 802.11w Management Frame Protection cryptographically protects deauth/disassoc frames; required for WPA3
  • WIPS containment legality varies by jurisdiction -- consult legal counsel before enabling automatic containment
