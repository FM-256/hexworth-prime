What 802.11be Targets

802.11be (Wi-Fi 7) was ratified in 2024 and targets Extremely High Throughput (EHT) for applications that Wi-Fi 6 cannot adequately serve: 8K video streaming, AR/VR with sub-5ms latency requirements, dense enterprise environments, and high-speed backhaul between mesh nodes. Peak PHY rate reaches 46 Gbps across three bands with 16 spatial streams -- a theoretical headline number that requires ideal conditions across all three bands simultaneously.

More practically, Wi-Fi 7 addresses the latency and congestion problems that remain in Wi-Fi 6 deployments. Multi-Link Operation is the defining architectural change: a client can maintain simultaneous active connections across multiple bands and aggregate or load-balance across them in real time, something Wi-Fi 6 band steering cannot do.

Multi-Link Operation (MLO)

In all previous 802.11 amendments, a client associates to a single BSS on a single band at a time. Band steering is a vendor-specific mechanism that encourages clients to move between bands, but the client is only active on one at a time. MLO changes this fundamentally.

With MLO, a single association can span multiple bands simultaneously. A Wi-Fi 7 client with an MLO connection might be simultaneously connected on 5 GHz channel 36 and 6 GHz channel 37. The MAC layer can then:

  • Transmit frames on whichever link is least congested (opportunistic switching)
  • Split a single data stream across both links for aggregate throughput
  • Send duplicate copies of latency-sensitive frames on both links (STR mode) for reliability
  • Buffer on one link while the other is in power-save state
STR MLO (Simultaneous Transmit and Receive) Device can simultaneously transmit on one link and receive on another. Requires sufficient hardware isolation between radios. Provides full aggregate throughput from both links. Highest performance, highest hardware cost.
Non-STR MLO (NSTR) Device cannot simultaneously transmit and receive across links due to inter-radio interference. Must coordinate transmission windows. Still provides latency benefits and load balancing but not full simultaneous aggregate throughput.

MLO vs Dual-Band: The Key Difference

A Wi-Fi 6E dual-band AP can serve 5 GHz and 6 GHz clients separately, but each client has one connection. MLO gives a single client an aggregated connection that uses both bands simultaneously under a single MAC address. The AP manages the combined stream as one logical link. Packet ordering, sequence numbers, and block ACKs all operate across the combined multi-link.

320 MHz Channels

802.11be doubles the maximum channel width from 160 MHz to 320 MHz. A single 320 MHz channel in the 6 GHz band delivers approximately double the throughput of a 160 MHz channel at the same MCS and spatial stream count -- a single spatial stream at MCS 13 on 320 MHz delivers 2882 Mbps PHY rate.

The critical constraint: 320 MHz channels are only available in the 6 GHz band. The 5 GHz band has insufficient contiguous spectrum for 320 MHz without overlapping licensed bands, and the 2.4 GHz band has only 83.5 MHz total. The United States allocated 1200 MHz of unlicensed 6 GHz spectrum, enabling two non-overlapping 320 MHz channels in the lower portion alone.

Preamble Puncturing

Wide channels (80, 160, 320 MHz) require that all sub-channels be clear of other transmissions before the primary channel can transmit. In congested 5 GHz spectrum, a neighboring BSS or a radar detection event on a secondary sub-channel forces the AP to drop down to a narrower channel width, reducing throughput even though the primary sub-channel is free.

Preamble puncturing allows an 802.11be transmitter to use a wide channel while marking specific 20 MHz sub-channels as punctured (excluded) due to interference. The PPDU preamble contains a puncturing bitmap indicating which sub-channels are active and which are punctured. Receivers ignore the punctured sub-channels. A 320 MHz channel can have up to 4 contiguous or non-contiguous sub-channels punctured, still operating effectively as a wider channel with some spectrum excluded.

DFS and Preamble Puncturing

Dynamic Frequency Selection (DFS) is mandatory for 5 GHz channels 52-144 to avoid interference with radar systems. When radar is detected, current 802.11ac/ax implementations must vacate the entire channel width. Wi-Fi 7's preamble puncturing allows the AP to puncture the affected sub-channels and continue operating on the remaining portions of the bond -- a significant improvement for high-density enterprise 5 GHz deployments near airports or military radar.

4096-QAM (MCS 12 and MCS 13)

802.11be adds two new MCS indexes above 1024-QAM: MCS 12 (4096-QAM, 3/4 coding) and MCS 13 (4096-QAM, 5/6 coding). Each symbol encodes 12 bits versus 10 for 1024-QAM, a 20% increase in spectral efficiency. The required SNR is approximately 40 dB -- achievable in controlled environments but essentially limited to distances of 5-8 meters from the AP in typical office RF conditions.

4096-QAM is most relevant for mesh backhaul links between APs where physical proximity can be engineered, and for fixed wireless access scenarios where the client antenna can be precisely aimed and mounted. For typical roaming laptop users, the useful MCS range remains MCS 7-11 regardless of the theoretical maximum.

Practice Questions

  1. A Wi-Fi 7 AP supports STR MLO on 5 GHz (80 MHz, MCS 9) and 6 GHz (160 MHz, MCS 11). A client is simultaneously using both links. Calculate the approximate combined PHY rate assuming 2 spatial streams on each link.
  2. Your enterprise 5 GHz deployment on channel 100 (DFS) keeps dropping to 40 MHz whenever the AP detects a radar signal on the secondary sub-channel of the 80 MHz bond. How does Wi-Fi 7 preamble puncturing address this specific problem? What configuration would you set?
  3. Explain why 320 MHz channels are only available in the 6 GHz band and not the 5 GHz band. Calculate how many non-overlapping 320 MHz channels exist in the US 6 GHz allocation (5.925-7.125 GHz = 1200 MHz).
  4. A client device supports Wi-Fi 7 NSTR MLO. In what scenarios would NSTR MLO still provide a latency benefit even though it cannot simultaneously transmit and receive on both links?

Key Takeaways

  • 802.11be (Wi-Fi 7) targets Extremely High Throughput with peak 46 Gbps across all bands/streams
  • MLO is the defining feature: a single client maintains simultaneous active links across multiple bands
  • STR MLO allows true simultaneous transmit/receive; NSTR MLO provides latency and load-balancing benefits
  • 320 MHz channels exist only in the 6 GHz band; the US has 3 non-overlapping 320 MHz channels available
  • Preamble puncturing allows wide channels to operate with specific 20 MHz sub-channels excluded for interference
  • 4096-QAM requires ~40 dB SNR -- useful for mesh backhaul and fixed wireless, not mobile clients
  • Wi-Fi 7 requires Wi-Fi 6E infrastructure (6 GHz APs) to access the most impactful features
