Learning Objectives

Survey Methodology Types

A wireless site survey determines where APs should be placed, what channels they should use, and what transmit power levels will achieve adequate coverage and capacity. Three distinct survey types serve different stages of the deployment lifecycle.

Passive Survey Laptop listens on each channel without associating. Captures beacon frames, RSSI, noise floor, and channel utilization. Identifies existing APs (rogue or neighbor) and interference sources. No test traffic generated.
Active Survey Test device associates to APs and measures throughput, retry rates, and roaming behavior. Requires a deployed (or temporary) AP. Validates actual application performance rather than just RF coverage.
Predictive Survey Software models RF propagation using floor plan uploads, wall materials, and AP models. Produces heat maps before any hardware is deployed. Used for pre-sale scoping and initial AP count estimation.
Post-Deployment Validation Active survey performed after full installation. Confirms coverage, roaming, capacity, and interference targets are met. Deliverable includes heat maps and a formal RF validation report.

Industry Practice

Best practice is predictive survey first (to estimate AP count and get budget approval), followed by a physical site walk (to observe actual obstructions), then active post-deployment validation. Skipping post-deployment validation is the leading cause of coverage complaints after go-live.

Heat Maps & Coverage Metrics

A heat map overlays signal strength or other RF metrics on a floor plan image. Survey tools (Ekahau, Hamina, AirMagnet) collect GPS-tagged readings as the surveyor walks the floor and interpolate between measurement points.

Metric Typical Target What It Reveals
RSSI (Signal Strength) -67 dBm or stronger Basic coverage reach; -70 dBm is marginal for VoIP/video
SNR 25 dB minimum, 30+ dB preferred Usable MCS rates; low SNR forces low modulation, degrading throughput
Co-Channel Interference (CCI) Less than -85 dBm from neighbors Neighbors on same channel cause retries; visible as high retry rates
Channel Utilization Under 50% at peak Above 70% causes significant queuing and latency spikes
Roaming Transition Time Under 50 ms (with 802.11r) VoIP calls drop if roaming exceeds ~150 ms
Secondary Coverage (2nd AP -85 dBm) Present throughout Ensures roaming candidate always available; no dead-zone single-AP areas

The -67 dBm RSSI target is an industry convention derived from VoIP requirements. Data-only deployments can tolerate -70 to -72 dBm. High-density venues with many APs deliberately reduce power to keep cells small — the target RSSI for those environments is often -65 dBm minimum with a hard cell edge at -67 dBm.

Capacity Planning

Coverage-based design (place APs so signal reaches everywhere) was the norm through Wi-Fi 4. Wi-Fi 5 and Wi-Fi 6 deployments are capacity-driven: APs are placed closer together, at lower power, to divide clients across more cells.

Capacity Planning Worksheet
# Inputs
total_clients       = 800          # peak concurrent devices
target_throughput   = 10 Mbps      # per client (bidirectional)
air_time_efficiency = 0.5          # 50% — realistic for CSMA/CA with mixed clients

# Capacity per AP (802.11ax, 5 GHz, 80 MHz, HE80)
raw_phy_rate_mbps   = 1200         # ~MCS9 2x2 MIMO per AP
usable_throughput   = raw_phy_rate_mbps * air_time_efficiency
                    = 600 Mbps

# Clients per AP (capacity limit)
clients_per_ap_cap  = usable_throughput / target_throughput
                    = 600 / 10 = 60 clients per AP

# Coverage-driven clients per AP (historical guideline)
clients_per_ap_cov  = 25-30        # rule of thumb for VoIP-capable density

# AP count
ap_count_capacity   = total_clients / clients_per_ap_cap
                    = 800 / 60 = 14 APs (minimum for capacity)

ap_count_voip       = total_clients / clients_per_ap_cov
                    = 800 / 25 = 32 APs (for VoIP SLA)

# Decision: take the higher count — 32 APs for this deployment

Dual-Band Client Steering

Modern APs advertise 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz SSIDs. Without band steering, legacy clients park on 2.4 GHz and consume airtime inefficiently. Cisco's 802.11v BSS Transition Management, combined with Optimal Roaming (ORRM) on WLC, nudges capable clients to 5 GHz. Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7 add 6 GHz — steer newer clients there to free up 5 GHz for legacy devices.

AP Placement Strategies

Placement decisions depend on environment type, ceiling height, structural materials, and the desired cell radius. The goal is controlled overlap — typically 15-20% cell overlap for seamless roaming without excessive CCI.

Environment Placement Approach Key Considerations
Open Office Grid pattern, ceiling-mount, 15-20 m spacing (5 GHz) Low attenuation; power reduction critical to limit cell radius
Cubicle / Partitioned Denser grid, 10-15 m spacing; adjust per partition material Metal partitions add 10-15 dB attenuation; model each section separately
Warehouse / Industrial High-ceiling mount (6-10 m); directional antennas toward floor Metal shelving causes severe multipath; 5 GHz preferred over 2.4 GHz
Auditorium / Conference Multiple lower-power APs; avoid single high-power AP High client density; capacity drives placement, not coverage
Hallway / Corridor AP every 15-20 m; wall-mount preferred over ceiling in narrow corridors Waveguide effect can extend coverage further than expected
Multi-Floor Building Stagger APs floor-to-floor to reduce inter-floor CCI Concrete floors provide 20-30 dB isolation; wood/carpet floors much less

Transmit Power & Cell Sizing

  • Cisco best practice: RRM auto-power targets -65 dBm cell edge; typically results in 8-14 dBm AP Tx power in dense deployments
  • Client transmit power is fixed (typically 15-20 dBm for phones); if AP Tx is too high, clients hear AP but AP cannot hear client — asymmetric link
  • Rule: AP Tx power should not exceed client Tx power by more than 3-6 dB
  • Dynamic Transmit Power Control (DTPC) shares AP power level with clients via beacons so they can reduce their own power

Channel Planning & CCI Mitigation

Co-Channel Interference (CCI) occurs when two APs on the same channel are both heard by a client. Because 802.11 is a shared medium (CSMA/CA), every AP on the same channel must defer to all others — effectively halving throughput for each additional co-channel AP within range.

2.4 GHz Channels Only 3 non-overlapping 20 MHz channels: 1, 6, 11 (North America). With high AP density, 2.4 GHz becomes a CCI nightmare. Use 2.4 GHz for legacy IoT and minimum-coverage scenarios only.
5 GHz Channels Up to 25 non-overlapping 20 MHz channels (with DFS). 5 non-overlapping 80 MHz channels. Use 20 MHz or 40 MHz in high-density deployments to maximize non-overlapping channel count and reduce CCI.
6 GHz Channels 59 non-overlapping 20 MHz channels. 7 non-overlapping 80 MHz channels. No legacy 802.11 devices, no DFS radar concerns. Use 80 MHz or 160 MHz channels freely. Preferred band for Wi-Fi 6E/7 deployments.
RRM / Auto-RF Cisco's Radio Resource Management (RRM) dynamically adjusts channels and power. RRM runs every 600 seconds by default; can be triggered manually. TPC (Transmit Power Control) and DCA (Dynamic Channel Assignment) work together.
Cisco WLC — RRM Configuration Verification
# View current RRM settings per RF group
show advanced 802.11a channel
show advanced 802.11a txpower

# Output includes:
#   Channel Assignment Method: Automatic
#   Channel Update Interval:   600 seconds
#   Channel Assignment Leader: [WLC IP]
#   DCA Sensitivity:           Medium (5 dB)

# View AP channel assignments
show ap summary            # lists all APs with channel/power
show ap channel all        # channel per AP detail

# Force RRM recalculation (use during off-hours)
config 802.11a channel global once

# Lock AP to specific channel (override RRM)
config ap channel 802.11a

Survey Tools & Deliverables

Commercial survey tools automate heat map generation, report production, and AP placement optimization. The choice of tool depends on budget, existing vendor ecosystem, and whether predictive or active surveys (or both) are required.

Tool Type Strengths Limitations
Ekahau AI Pro Predictive + Active Industry standard; AI-assisted placement; integrates with Cisco/Aruba High cost; requires Ekahau Sidekick for accurate active surveys
Hamina Wireless Predictive Browser-based; faster for small deployments; collaborative Less mature active survey capability vs Ekahau
AirMagnet Survey Pro Active + Passive Detailed protocol analysis; compliance reporting (802.11k/r/v) Older interface; less active development than Ekahau
Cisco Prime / Cisco PI Post-deployment Deep integration with Cisco WLC; client location heatmaps Cisco ecosystem only; limited predictive capability
inSSIDer / WiFi Analyzer Passive (free) Quick channel utilization check; free; good for spot troubleshooting No floor plan import; no heat maps; not for formal surveys

A professional post-deployment survey deliverable includes: floor plan with AP locations overlaid, RSSI heat map (minimum -67 dBm coverage), SNR heat map (minimum 25 dB), channel utilization heat map, roaming event log (showing roam triggers and transition times), and a written executive summary with any remediation recommendations.

Practice Exercise

A 40,000 sq ft office floor with metal stud walls, 10-foot ceilings, and 300 peak concurrent users (mix of phones and laptops) requires Wi-Fi 6 deployment. Estimate the AP count using both coverage-driven (30 clients/AP) and capacity-driven (60 clients/AP) methods. Which drives the design? What channel width would you specify for 5 GHz and why?

Key Takeaways

  • Passive surveys capture existing RF; active surveys measure real performance; predictive surveys plan before deployment
  • RSSI target of -67 dBm is the VoIP/video floor; -72 dBm may suffice for data-only
  • Capacity planning at 25-30 clients/AP for VoIP; 50-60 clients/AP for data — always design to the stricter requirement
  • AP Tx power must stay within 3-6 dB of client Tx power to avoid asymmetric links
  • 2.4 GHz has 3 non-overlapping channels; 5 GHz has up to 25; 6 GHz has 59 — channel richness drives band preference in dense environments
  • CCI forces CSMA/CA deferral — every co-channel AP within range halves effective airtime
  • Post-deployment validation is mandatory; predictive models do not account for furniture, people, or equipment installed after the survey
