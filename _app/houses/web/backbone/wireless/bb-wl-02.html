The Density Problem Wi-Fi 6 Solves

802.11ax was not designed to deliver higher peak speeds to a single device -- 802.11ac already achieved that. It was designed to deliver adequate speeds to many devices simultaneously. A stadium with 50,000 people, a convention hall floor, a university lecture theater: these environments break Wi-Fi 5 because CSMA/CA allows only one client to transmit per time slot regardless of how few subcarriers that client actually needs.

The fundamental shift in 802.11ax is from Time Division Multiple Access (effectively what CSMA/CA is) to Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiple Access. With OFDMA, the AP subdivides each channel into Resource Units and assigns different RUs to different clients in the same time slot. Clients with small amounts of data get small RUs. Clients with large transfers get large RUs. The channel is fully utilized in both dimensions simultaneously.

Wi-Fi 6 vs Wi-Fi 6E

Wi-Fi 6 operates on 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands using 802.11ax. Wi-Fi 6E extends the same amendment into the newly opened 6 GHz band (5.925 to 7.125 GHz in the US). The 6 GHz band has no legacy devices, no co-channel interference from older networks, and 7 non-overlapping 160 MHz channels. Wi-Fi 6E APs supporting 6 GHz are tri-band devices and require WPA3 for the 6 GHz radio.

OFDMA: Resource Units and Scheduling

802.11ax divides a 20 MHz channel into 256 subcarriers (compared to 64 in 802.11ac). These subcarriers are grouped into Resource Units of fixed sizes. The AP's scheduler assigns RUs to clients based on their current traffic demand, reported channel conditions, and QoS class.

The AP triggers OFDMA transmissions using a Multi-User PPDU frame with an HE-MU preamble that tells each client which RU it is assigned for the upcoming PPDU. Downlink OFDMA (DL-OFDMA) is where the AP transmits to multiple clients simultaneously. Uplink OFDMA (UL-OFDMA) requires the AP to send a Trigger Frame first, coordinating simultaneous uplink transmissions from multiple clients -- a fundamentally different mechanism that 802.11ac's downlink-only MU-MIMO never addressed.

MU-MIMO: Uplink Now Included

802.11ac introduced downlink MU-MIMO, allowing the AP to transmit to up to 4 clients simultaneously using spatial multiplexing via beamforming. 802.11ax extends this to 8 spatial streams and adds uplink MU-MIMO for the first time.

Downlink MU-MIMO (DL-MU-MIMO) AP transmits simultaneously to multiple clients on the same channel using different spatial streams. Requires explicit beamforming sounding (NDP + NDPA). Up to 8 streams in Wi-Fi 6 vs 4 in Wi-Fi 5.
Uplink MU-MIMO (UL-MU-MIMO) New in 802.11ax. Multiple clients transmit simultaneously to the AP on different spatial streams. Requires the AP to send a Trigger Frame coordinating the simultaneous transmission. Eliminates the "receive bottleneck" for dense uplink traffic (video uploads, conferencing).

MU-MIMO vs OFDMA: Which Wins?

MU-MIMO works best when clients are spatially separated and have similar high-throughput demands. OFDMA works best for mixed traffic: many small-packet clients alongside a few high-throughput ones. In practice, Wi-Fi 6 APs dynamically select between MU-MIMO and OFDMA based on the current client mix. They can also combine them: UL-OFDMA with MU-MIMO for maximum aggregate throughput.

BSS Coloring

In dense deployments, APs on the same channel can hear each other's transmissions and must back off via CSMA/CA even when those transmissions are from a completely different BSS and will not collide with local transmissions. This is called inter-BSS interference and it severely limits channel reuse in high-density buildings.

BSS Coloring adds a 6-bit color identifier to every 802.11ax PPDU preamble. Receiving stations check the color of incoming frames. If the color matches their BSS, it is a relevant transmission and CSMA/CA applies normally. If the color does not match (inter-BSS frame), the station can apply Spatial Reuse -- optionally transmitting at reduced power if the RSSI of the inter-BSS frame is below a configurable threshold, effectively treating that channel activity as noise rather than a reason to defer.

Target Wake Time (TWT)

IoT devices and mobile clients spend most of their time sleeping to preserve battery life. Before Wi-Fi 6, power save was handled by legacy mechanisms (PS-Poll, U-APSD) that required clients to wake periodically and poll the AP for buffered frames, often wasting energy waking up when there was nothing to receive.

TWT allows an AP to negotiate a specific wake schedule with each individual client. The AP and client agree on a wake interval (from milliseconds to minutes), a wake duration, and the start time. The client sleeps deeply between scheduled wake periods. For a temperature sensor that reports every 5 minutes, TWT can extend battery life from months to years. For a video call client, TWT negotiates frequent short wake intervals for low-latency frame exchange.

Individual TWT (iTWT) AP negotiates a unique schedule with each client independently. Maximum flexibility for heterogeneous IoT deployments. The client initiates by sending a TWT Request frame. AP responds with TWT Response.
Broadcast TWT AP announces a shared TWT schedule in beacon frames that any client can follow. Suitable for synchronized low-latency applications where all clients should wake simultaneously. Simpler than per-client negotiation.

1024-QAM and Spatial Reuse

802.11ax adds 1024-QAM (MCS 10 and MCS 11) to the modulation options. A single 80 MHz stream at MCS 11 achieves 1201 Mbps PHY rate, compared to 867 Mbps at 256-QAM (MCS 9) in 802.11ac. The gain is approximately 25%. However, 1024-QAM requires SNR of approximately 35 dB -- achievable only for clients within roughly 10 meters of the AP with a clear line of sight. For most clients at enterprise distances, MCS 7-9 is more realistic.

Practice Questions

  1. An 802.11ax AP is managing 40 IoT sensors that each transmit a 50-byte payload every 30 seconds. Why is OFDMA superior to legacy CSMA/CA for this traffic pattern? Which RU size would be most appropriate for each sensor?
  2. Explain the difference between Spatial Reuse and BSS Coloring. Are they the same feature or does one enable the other? Describe what happens when a Wi-Fi 6 client receives a BSS-colored frame from a neighboring BSS at -82 dBm.
  3. A client connected at 802.11ax, MCS 11, 2 spatial streams, 160 MHz channel width is advertised as achieving approximately 2400 Mbps PHY rate. The actual measured TCP throughput is 780 Mbps. Name four factors that explain the gap between PHY rate and application throughput.
  4. Your organization has deployed Wi-Fi 6 APs but most clients are still 802.11ac devices. Will those clients benefit from the new APs? Which specific Wi-Fi 6 features are backward compatible and which are not?

Key Takeaways

  • 802.11ax targets density efficiency, not peak speed -- its value is in high-client-count environments
  • OFDMA subdivides channels into Resource Units, enabling simultaneous multi-client access in the same time slot
  • UL-OFDMA and UL-MU-MIMO are new in 802.11ax; 802.11ac only had downlink MU-MIMO
  • BSS Coloring reduces CSMA/CA defer events for inter-BSS traffic, improving channel reuse efficiency
  • TWT schedules individual client wake times, reducing battery consumption for IoT by an order of magnitude
  • 1024-QAM adds ~25% throughput gain over 256-QAM but requires ~35 dB SNR -- close proximity only
  • Wi-Fi 6E adds 6 GHz: 59 new 20 MHz channels, no legacy interference, but requires WPA3
