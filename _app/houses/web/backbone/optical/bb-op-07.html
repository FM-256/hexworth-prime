Why Submarine Cables Matter

Over 99% of international internet traffic travels on submarine fiber optic cables -- not satellites. The 400+ submarine cable systems deployed worldwide carry the entirety of transatlantic, transpacific, and inter-ocean communications. A single modern submarine cable can carry 250+ Tbps across the Pacific Ocean. Satellites are used for the geographic coverage gaps where cables cannot reach economically, and for latency-tolerant applications.

Submarine cables represent some of the most demanding optical engineering challenges: amplifier chains spanning 6,000-20,000 km, repeaters that must operate reliably for 25 years on the ocean floor without access for maintenance, and fault detection and repair operations that cost millions of dollars per incident and require specialized cable ships.

Scale: Modern Submarine Cable

The MAREA cable (Microsoft/Facebook/Telxius, 2017) spans 6,600 km across the Atlantic with 8 fiber pairs and initial design capacity of 160 Tbps. The 2Africa cable (Meta, 2022+) is 45,000 km long, connecting Europe, the Middle East, and Africa. The Dunant cable (Google, 2021) crosses the Atlantic with a single fiber pair capable of 250 Tbps using space division multiplexing.

Submarine Cable Structure

A submarine cable is not simply a bundle of fibers -- it is a highly engineered composite structure designed to survive deep ocean pressure, anchors, trawling, and burial in shallow coastal zones.

Deep-water cable (beyond 1000m depth) is unarmored or lightly armored -- the deep ocean floor is geologically stable and there are no human activities that threaten cables at these depths. Shallow-water cable near coasts receives heavy armor (double armor, rock armor) because anchors, trawls, and construction activities cause the majority of submarine cable breaks.

Submarine Repeaters and Power Feeding

Submarine repeaters are hermetically sealed pressure housings containing EDFAs (and in modern systems, Raman pump lasers) that amplify the optical signals at regular intervals -- typically every 40-80 km for deep-water systems. The repeaters have no battery and cannot be accessed for maintenance once deployed. All electrical power is supplied from shore through the copper conductor in the cable itself.

Power Feeding Equipment (PFE) Located at each cable landing station. Generates the high-voltage DC (8,000-15,000 V) that travels down the copper conductor to power the repeater chain. Modern trans-Pacific cables may have PFEs at both ends, each supplying half the total current path.
Power Budget Constraint The total resistance of the copper conductor (proportional to cable length) limits current delivery. Longer cables require higher voltage from the PFE to maintain adequate current at distant repeaters. This limits the span length of a single powered segment and drives the need for mid-cable branching units (BUs).

EDFA Chains and Raman Amplification

Standard terrestrial DWDM systems use lumped EDFA amplifiers every 80-120 km. Submarine systems face two additional challenges: the amplifiers must operate for 25 years without service, and the OSNR budget must survive spans of thousands of kilometers with dozens or hundreds of amplifiers in cascade.

EDFA Submarine Repeaters C-band and L-band EDFAs operating in fixed-gain mode are the workhorses of submarine amplification. Pump lasers at 980 nm or 1480 nm power the erbium-doped fiber. Redundant pump lasers are included in every repeater for the 25-year reliability requirement.
Raman Amplification Distributed amplification using the fiber itself as the gain medium. High-power Raman pump lasers (typically 1450-1480 nm) launched from the repeater propagate backward through the fiber span, providing gain distributed along the entire span rather than only at repeater sites. This improves OSNR by reducing the peak ASE accumulation per span.

Space Division Multiplexing (SDM)

Next-generation submarine cables are pursuing SDM to multiply capacity beyond what is achievable with more wavelengths per fiber. SDM approaches include multiple fiber pairs per cable (up to 16 pairs in some designs), multi-core fibers (MCF with 4-12 cores per fiber), and few-mode fibers. Google's Dunant cable uses 12 fiber pairs. A 250 Tbps capacity per pair at 12 pairs = 3 Pbps total system capacity on a single cable.

Terrestrial Long-Haul Architecture

Terrestrial long-haul systems (cross-country, continental backbone) use the same DWDM + EDFA foundation as submarine systems but with key differences: spans are shorter (typically 80-120 km between amplifier sites), regenerators are used at 2000-3000 km intervals to convert back to electrical signals and clean up optical impairments, and the system can be maintained by field technicians.

Practice Questions

  1. A transatlantic submarine cable is 7,000 km long with EDFA repeaters every 70 km. How many repeaters are in the chain? If each EDFA adds 5 dB of noise figure worth of OSNR degradation, estimate the total OSNR degradation from all repeaters. Why is this a simplified estimate, and what additional factors affect actual OSNR at the far end?
  2. Explain why the copper power conductor in a submarine cable must operate at very high voltage (8,000-15,000 V) rather than lower voltage with higher current. What electrical engineering principle drives this choice, and what safety engineering does it require at cable landing stations?
  3. A submarine cable system uses Raman amplification in combination with EDFA repeaters. Explain how distributed Raman gain improves OSNR compared to EDFA-only amplification, and why this matters specifically for submarine systems where OSNR budget is critical.
  4. Why do submarine cable systems typically use DP-QPSK modulation rather than higher-order modulation (DP-16QAM or DP-64QAM) used in shorter terrestrial systems? Under what specific conditions could a submarine cable use higher-order modulation on shorter segments?

Key Takeaways

  • Over 99% of international internet traffic travels on submarine fiber cables, not satellites
  • Submarine cables are layered structures with fiber, copper power conductor, armor, and polyethylene jacket
  • Deep-water cable is unarmored; shallow-water cable uses heavy armor against anchors and fishing gear
  • Power Feeding Equipment (PFE) at landing stations provides DC power to repeaters at 8,000-15,000 V
  • Submarine repeaters contain redundant EDFA pump lasers and must operate for 25 years without service
  • Raman amplification distributes gain along the fiber span, improving OSNR vs lumped EDFA amplification
  • Space Division Multiplexing (multiple fiber pairs, multi-core fiber) is the capacity frontier for next-gen cables
