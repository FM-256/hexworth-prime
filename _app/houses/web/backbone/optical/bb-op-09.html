Learning Objectives

  • Explain the silicon photonics platform and its manufacturing advantages

The Integration Imperative

Traditional optical networking uses discrete components: a laser chip, a modulator chip, a photodetector chip, and a DSP chip, each manufactured separately and connected by wire bonds or short fiber jumpers. This discrete approach worked well through 100 Gbps per lane, but at 400G per lane and beyond, electrical interconnects between chips become the bottleneck — they consume excessive power and limit bandwidth due to parasitic capacitance and signal loss.

Photonic integration places multiple optical functions on a single semiconductor substrate, reducing the number of electrical chip-to-chip interfaces. This improves power efficiency, reduces form-factor size, lowers cost through wafer-scale manufacturing, and ultimately enables the 1.6 Tbps and 3.2 Tbps per port rates required by AI/ML training clusters and hyperscale data centers.

The Bandwidth-Power Wall

A 51.2 Tbps switch ASIC requires 128 x 400G optical ports. Driving those ports through traditional QSFP-DD pluggables on a PCB edge consumes roughly 25-30 W per port in electrical SerDes alone — before the optics. Co-packaged optics moves the optical engine next to the switching ASIC, cutting that per-port SerDes power by 5-10x. At 128 ports, that is 1.5-2 kW of power savings per switch — a massive operational cost reduction in a hyperscale environment.

Silicon Photonics Platform

Silicon photonics (SiPh) fabricates optical waveguides, modulators, and photodetectors using standard CMOS semiconductor processes (180 nm to 45 nm nodes). Silicon's high refractive index (3.45 vs silica's 1.45) allows tight optical confinement in waveguides only 200-500 nm wide — enabling dense component packing on a chip smaller than a thumbnail.

Component Silicon Implementation Performance / Limitation
Waveguide Silicon-on-insulator (SOI) rib or strip waveguide Loss: 1-3 dB/cm; tight bend radius (5 um); handles 1310 nm and 1550 nm bands
Modulator Mach-Zehnder Modulator (MZM) using carrier depletion in p-n junction Bandwidth: 40+ GHz; requires longer device length (2-3 mm) vs InP
Photodetector Germanium-on-silicon (Ge-on-Si) avalanche photodetector Responsivity: 0.8-1.0 A/W; bandwidth 50+ GHz; full CMOS compatible
Laser Cannot lase in silicon (indirect bandgap) — laser co-packaged or hybrid bonded III-V (InP or GaAs) laser die bonded to SiPh chip via flip-chip or wafer bonding
Multiplexer / Demux Arrayed Waveguide Grating (AWG) or Echelle grating Multiplexes 4-16 wavelengths on chip; insertion loss 1-3 dB per channel
Optical Coupler Grating coupler (vertical) or edge coupler (horizontal) to fiber Grating coupler: 2-3 dB loss, polarization dependent; edge: lower loss, harder to assemble

Key silicon photonics suppliers for networking include Intel (Broadwell SiPh), Cisco (acquired Acacia), Marvell (acquired Inphi), and Coherent (acquired II-VI and Lumentum assets). TSMC, GlobalFoundries, and IHP offer silicon photonics foundry PDKs (process design kits) enabling fabless PIC design.

Indium Phosphide (InP) Platform

Indium Phosphide has a direct bandgap that allows lasing and amplification directly on the semiconductor. This makes InP the dominant platform for long-haul and metro coherent transceivers where laser integration and optical gain are required on-chip.

InP Laser Integration Distributed Feedback (DFB) lasers, Electro-Absorption Modulated Lasers (EML), and Vertical Cavity Surface Emitting Lasers (VCSEL) can be fabricated directly in InP — eliminating the hybrid bonding step required in SiPh.
InP Modulator Speed InP electro-absorption modulators achieve 100+ GHz bandwidth with shorter device length (0.1-0.2 mm vs 2-3 mm for SiMZM). Critical for 100G per lane signaling rates. Used in 400ZR and OpenZR+ pluggables.
InP Cost Disadvantage InP wafers are 3-4 inches vs silicon's 12-inch wafers. Per-unit cost is 5-10x higher than SiPh for equivalent integration density. InP also cannot reuse CMOS fabs, limiting capacity scaling.
Hybrid InP/SiPh Emerging approach: InP gain section bonded to SiPh waveguide circuit. Intel uses this in their SiPh products. Combines SiPh passive routing and modulation efficiency with InP gain and speed.

Co-Packaged Optics (CPO)

Co-packaged optics places the optical engine (PIC + DSP) in the same package as the switching ASIC, connected by a very short electrical interposer (a few millimeters) rather than a PCB trace spanning 10-15 cm. This eliminates the dominant electrical loss in pluggable architectures.

Pluggable vs Co-Packaged Signal Path Comparison
# Pluggable transceiver path (QSFP-DD on Tomahawk 4 at 12.8 Tbps)
Switch ASIC  --[PCB trace 10 cm, ~5 dB loss]-->  QSFP-DD cage
QSFP-DD cage --[connector + cage]-->               Pluggable module (Optical engine)

Electrical SerDes power per port: ~3-4 W at 400G
PCB re-timer required above 53.125 Gbaud per lane

# Co-packaged optics path (hyperscale switch 25.6 Tbps)
Switch ASIC  --[interposer 3-5 mm, <0.5 dB loss]--> Optical Engine PIC
Optical Engine PIC --> fiber array (direct fiber attach to switch faceplate)

Electrical interface power per port: ~0.5-1 W at 400G
No re-timer required; SerDes lanes run at native ASIC speed
No QSFP cage or PCB routing needed

# Aggregate comparison (128 x 400G switch)
Pluggable total SerDes power:  128 x 4 W = 512 W (electrical only)
CPO total SerDes power:        128 x 1 W = 128 W
Savings: ~384 W per switch (significant in a 10,000-switch hyperscale pod)

The tradeoff with CPO is repairability. In a pluggable architecture, a failed transceiver is replaced in seconds. In CPO, the optical engine is integrated into the switch package — a failure may require returning the entire switch. Vendors are addressing this with "near-packaged optics" (NPO) and "on-board optics" (OBO) as intermediate steps that reduce electrical path length without fully integrating into the ASIC package.

Architecture Electrical Path Length Power Efficiency Repairability Deployment Status
QSFP Pluggable 8-15 cm PCB + connector Baseline Full (hot-swap) Deployed (ubiquitous)
On-Board Optics (OBO) 3-6 cm PCB 30-40% better Board removal required Early deployment (2024-2025)
Near-Packaged Optics (NPO) 10-20 mm interposer 50-60% better Module replaceable Sampling / Early adoption
Co-Packaged Optics (CPO) 3-5 mm interposer 70-80% better Switch return required Hyperscale pilot (2025-2026)

PIC Platform Comparison

No single photonic platform dominates all applications. Platform selection depends on integration density requirements, wavelength band, need for on-chip gain, cost targets, and fab availability.

Platform Best For Limitations Commercial Example
Silicon Photonics (SiPh) Datacom (400G-1.6T), dense passive routing, photonic switches No on-chip laser; weaker modulation efficiency vs InP Intel CPO, Cisco Silicon One, Marvell Braveheart
Indium Phosphide (InP) Long-haul coherent (400ZR+), high-gain amplification, lasers Small wafer, high cost, limited CMOS integration Coherent CFP2-DCO, JDSU/Lumentum coherent modules
Silicon Nitride (SiN) Ultra-low-loss waveguides, sensing, visible/NIR wavelengths No electro-optic modulation; passive only without additional materials LioniX International, Ligentec (sensing + telecom filter integration)
Lithium Niobate (LiNbO3) Ultra-high-speed modulation (100+ GHz), microwave photonics Large device footprint; CMOS integration still maturing Hyperlight, POETS (thin-film LN modulators)
Polymer / III-V on Silicon Hybrid integration: best of silicon routing + III-V gain/speed Complex bonding process; yield challenges at wafer scale Rockley Photonics (health monitoring + datacom hybrid)

Thin-Film Lithium Niobate (TFLN)

Thin-film lithium niobate on insulator represents the most significant emerging modulator platform. TFLN achieves both the electro-optic coefficient advantage of bulk LiNbO3 (28 pm/V vs silicon's near-zero) and CMOS-compatible device sizes (sub-1 cm modulator lengths). Companies like Hyperlight, HyperLight, and POETS demonstrated 100+ GHz bandwidth TFLN modulators. At 200G per lane and beyond, TFLN may displace silicon MZMs in short-reach interconnects.

On-Chip Optical Interconnects

On-chip optical interconnects use photonic waveguides to replace electrical metal traces for chip-to-chip or core-to-core communication within a single die or multi-chip module. This is driven by the same electrical bandwidth-power wall: at 10+ Tbps aggregate bandwidth across a processor package, copper traces cannot deliver the bandwidth with acceptable power and latency.

Optical Mesh NoC Network-on-Chip using optical waveguides and microring resonators as wavelength-selective switches. WDM allows multiple parallel communication channels on a single waveguide. Active research at MIT, Stanford; not yet in production silicon.
Silicon Photonic Interposer A silicon substrate with embedded waveguides that routes optical signals between chiplets in a 2.5D or 3D package. IMEC and CEA-Leti demonstrated silicon photonic interposers with 100+ channels. Commercial feasibility by 2027-2028.
Microring Resonator Switch A ring resonator couples light from a bus waveguide when its resonance wavelength is thermally or electrically tuned. Used as on-chip wavelength-selective routers. Power consumption: ~1 mW per ring at 10 Gbps. Key building block for photonic switching fabrics.
VCSEL Array Interconnects Vertical Cavity Surface Emitting Laser arrays emit perpendicular to the chip surface, enabling 2D arrays for parallel optical links. Used in current 400G parallel optics (PSM4) and emerging short-reach intra-cluster interconnects in AI/ML pods.
400G Parallel Optical Link — Lane Architecture
# 400G PSM4 (Parallel Single Mode, 4 lanes)
4 x 100G lanes on 4 separate fibers (SMF)
Wavelength: 1310 nm (same wavelength all lanes)
Distance:   up to 500 m
Fiber count: 8 (4 Tx + 4 Rx in an MPO-12 connector)
Use case:   intra-data-center switch-to-switch

# 400G DR4 (Dense Single Mode, 4 wavelengths, 1 fiber pair)
4 x 100G lanes wavelength-multiplexed onto 1 SMF pair
Wavelengths: ~1295, 1300, 1304, 1309 nm (CWDM spacing)
Distance:   up to 500 m
Use case:   intra-data-center with fiber count reduction

# 400G FR4 (Far Reach, 4 wavelengths)
4 x 100G lanes on 1 SMF pair (same as DR4)
Wavelengths: 1271, 1291, 1311, 1331 nm (CWDM-4 grid)
Distance:   up to 2 km
Use case:   campus / inter-building

# 800G OSFP (Octal SFP, 8 x 100G lanes)
8 lanes per module using 2 MPO-12 connectors or WDM
PIC-based module with on-chip 8-channel MUX/DEMUX
Next generation: 1.6T using 8 x 200G lanes (PAM8 or coherent)

Key Takeaways

  • Silicon photonics uses CMOS fabs for low-cost, dense integration but requires hybrid-bonded III-V lasers since silicon cannot lase
  • InP allows on-chip lasing and fast electro-absorption modulation but suffers from small wafer size and high cost — dominant in coherent long-haul modules
  • Co-packaged optics eliminates the 10-15 cm PCB electrical path between switch ASIC and optical transceiver, saving 300-400+ W per 128-port switch
  • The CPO repairability tradeoff drives intermediate architectures: on-board optics and near-packaged optics as stepping stones
  • Thin-film lithium niobate is the most promising emerging modulator platform for 200G/lane and beyond due to 100+ GHz bandwidth
  • 400G parallel optics (PSM4/DR4/FR4) use PIC-based modules with on-chip AWG multiplexers to pack 4 lanes onto a single fiber pair

Practice Questions

Q1

Why can't silicon be used as a laser material directly, and what solutions do silicon photonics vendors use to provide an on-chip light source?

Q2

A hyperscale operator runs 10,000 switches, each with 128 x 400G ports. Co-packaged optics reduces per-port SerDes power from 4 W to 1 W. Calculate the annual power savings assuming 24/7 operation at $0.08/kWh.

Q3

Compare PSM4 and FR4 as 400G module options for a link between two switches 800 m apart in the same campus. Which option is preferred and why?

Q4

What is a microring resonator and how does it enable wavelength-selective switching in an on-chip optical network?
