From Static Mux/Demux to ROADM

Early DWDM systems used fixed optical multiplexers and demultiplexers: every wavelength had a fixed physical port, and adding or rerouting a wavelength required a technician to physically repatch fiber. A metropolitan network with 20 sites and 80 wavelengths required 1,600 potential fiber patches for full wavelength flexibility -- an operational nightmare.

Reconfigurable Optical Add/Drop Multiplexers (ROADMs) solve this by enabling remote, software-controlled wavelength switching. An operator can add a new wavelength from a data center, route it through intermediate metro nodes, and drop it at the destination -- all through software, without any technician touching fiber. ROADMs are the fundamental enabling technology for on-demand optical bandwidth provisioning.

Wavelength-Selective Switch (WSS)

The Wavelength-Selective Switch is the key optical component inside a ROADM. A WSS uses liquid crystal on silicon (LCoS) or micro-electro-mechanical system (MEMS) technology to independently steer individual wavelengths from an input fiber to any of multiple output ports -- or to block a wavelength entirely.

WSS in the Express Path Wavelengths passing through the node (not dropped) are switched by the WSS from the input line fiber to the output line fiber. The WSS can also equalize the power of each wavelength independently -- critical for maintaining OSNR consistency across the wavelength comb.
WSS in the Add/Drop Path Local wavelengths are dropped from the express fiber to local transponders (drop WSS), or added from local transponders to the express fiber (add WSS). A full degree-N ROADM has one add WSS and one drop WSS per fiber direction plus an express WSS.

ROADM Degree

A ROADM's "degree" refers to the number of fiber directions it connects. A degree-2 ROADM connects two fiber directions (east-west on a ring). A degree-4 ROADM connects four directions (a mesh node connecting to four neighbors). Higher degree requires more WSS modules. Modern metro ROADM nodes commonly support degree-4 to degree-8, with degree-20+ nodes deployed at major interconnection points.

CDC: Colorless, Directionless, Contentionless

ROADM architectures are characterized by three independent properties that determine operational flexibility. Together, a CDC ROADM provides maximum operational agility -- any transponder, at any port, can be assigned any wavelength to any direction, without physical intervention or wavelength blocking.

PropertyDefinitionHardware RequirementBenefit
Colorless A transponder port can be assigned any ITU wavelength without being physically connected to a wavelength-specific port Tunable lasers in transponders; WSS-based add/drop instead of fixed MUX/DEMUX filters Transponders are interchangeable; no inventory of wavelength-specific hardware required
Directionless A wavelength can be routed to any fiber direction from any add/drop port without physical repatching Multi-directional add/drop WSS that can route to any degree Network reconfiguration without truck rolls; wavelengths can be rerouted remotely
Contentionless Multiple transponders using the same wavelength can coexist on the same add/drop module without blocking each other Wavelength blocker or multi-cast switch; requires MIMO-capable add/drop architecture No wavelength blocking constraints; full flexibility across all transponder/wavelength combinations

Flex-Grid: Beyond Fixed Channel Plans

The traditional fixed ITU-T G.694.1 grid assigns channels at 50 GHz or 100 GHz intervals regardless of the actual bandwidth occupied by the signal. A 100G DP-QPSK signal occupies approximately 32 GHz of optical spectrum but sits in a 50 GHz slot -- wasting 18 GHz of spectrum on either side. This waste compounds when the network operates many channels.

Flex-grid (also called elastic optical networking) replaces fixed channel slots with variable-width optical spectrum units called "spectrum slots" of 12.5 GHz granularity. A channel can be allocated exactly as many 12.5 GHz slots as its signal actually occupies. A 32 GHz wide 100G signal gets three 12.5 GHz slots (37.5 GHz total). A 400G DP-16QAM signal occupying 75 GHz gets six slots.

Fixed Grid Efficiency 50 GHz fixed grid: 100G DP-QPSK uses approximately 32 GHz but occupies a full 50 GHz slot. Spectral efficiency: 64%. 80 channels possible in C-band. Simple to manage and plan.
Flex-Grid Efficiency 12.5 GHz granularity: 100G DP-QPSK in 3 slots (37.5 GHz); 400G DP-16QAM in 6 slots (75 GHz). Higher spectral efficiency overall. Enables spectrum-efficient mix of 100G, 200G, and 400G channels on the same fiber.

Flex-grid requires WSS hardware capable of 12.5 GHz granularity -- not all deployed ROADMs support this. Network operators must plan spectrum fragmentation: as channels are added and removed over time, spectrum gaps that are too narrow for any useful signal may accumulate. Defragmentation algorithms that shift channel center frequencies to consolidate free spectrum are an active area of optical network management.

Optical Control Plane

Modern ROADM networks use GMPLS (Generalized Multiprotocol Label Switching) to automate wavelength provisioning. GMPLS extends IP routing protocols to include optical resources: wavelengths, spectrum slots, and optical amplifier states. OSPF-TE (Traffic Engineering) extension (or IS-IS-TE) advertises available spectrum on each fiber link.

Path Computation Elements (PCEs) take wavelength connection requests and compute valid end-to-end optical paths considering OSNR budget, polarization constraints, nonlinear effects, and flex-grid slot availability. RSVP-TE or PCEP signals the computed path to each ROADM node along the route, which configures its WSS accordingly -- all in seconds, without manual intervention.

Practice Questions

  1. A metro network has degree-4 ROADM nodes (North, South, East, West fiber directions). A new 100G wavelength service is needed between Site A and Site C, transiting Site B. Without CDC capability, describe what physical interventions are required at Site B to add this service. With a CDC ROADM at Site B, describe the operational difference.
  2. Explain the difference between colorless and contentionless ROADM properties. What specific hardware failure mode can occur in a colorless-but-not-contentionless ROADM when two transponders in the same add/drop module are assigned the same wavelength, even for different fiber directions?
  3. On a flex-grid system with 12.5 GHz slot granularity, how many spectrum slots does a 400G DP-16QAM signal require if its baud rate is 64 Gbaud and it uses a spectral shaping filter with 30% excess bandwidth? Calculate the actual optical bandwidth occupied and the number of 12.5 GHz slots needed.
  4. A GMPLS-controlled ROADM network receives a request for a new 100G wavelength path. List the steps the PCE performs to compute and signal the path, and explain what OSNR budget parameter the PCE must verify before committing the path.

Key Takeaways

  • ROADMs enable software-controlled wavelength add/drop without physical fiber repatching
  • WSS (liquid crystal on silicon or MEMS) is the key photonic component that steers individual wavelengths
  • CDC = Colorless + Directionless + Contentionless -- maximum operational flexibility for wavelength provisioning
  • Flex-grid replaces fixed 50 GHz channels with 12.5 GHz granularity spectrum slots for higher spectral efficiency
  • Spectrum fragmentation is a flex-grid operational challenge requiring defragmentation management
  • GMPLS control plane automates wavelength provisioning; PCE computes paths considering OSNR and spectrum constraints
  • ROADM degree = number of fiber directions; metro nodes typically degree 2-4; core interconnect nodes degree 8-20
