The Principle: One Fiber, Many Colors

Dense Wavelength Division Multiplexing transmits multiple independent optical signals simultaneously over a single fiber by using different wavelengths (colors) of light for each signal. Because each wavelength carries its own independent data stream, 80 wavelengths on a single fiber pair delivers 80 times the capacity of a single-wavelength system -- without laying any additional fiber.

DWDM systems operate in the 1550 nm window (the C-band: 1530-1565 nm, or L-band: 1565-1625 nm) where silica fiber has minimum attenuation (~0.2 dB/km) and where Erbium-Doped Fiber Amplifiers (EDFAs) provide gain. The combination of low attenuation and optical amplification enables terrestrial spans of 80-120 km between amplifiers and transoceanic systems of thousands of kilometers.

WDM vs CWDM vs DWDM

WDM (original): 2 channels at 1310 nm and 1550 nm. CWDM (Coarse WDM): up to 18 channels with 20 nm spacing, low cost, cannot be amplified by EDFA. DWDM: 40 to 96+ channels with 50 GHz or 100 GHz spacing, full EDFA support, used for all long-haul and metro capacity systems.

ITU-T G.694.1: The DWDM Grid

ITU-T G.694.1 defines the fixed frequency grid for DWDM channels. The reference frequency is 193.1 THz (approximately 1552.52 nm). All DWDM channels are defined at integer multiples of the channel spacing from this reference.

Common DWDM channels in the C-band by ITU designation (50 GHz grid): C17 (191.7 THz / 1563.86 nm) through C61 (196.1 THz / 1529.55 nm). The channel number is derived from frequency: channel N corresponds to 193.1 + N*0.05 THz for 50 GHz spacing. Engineers must match the transceiver's ITU channel to the ROADM port assignment or the wavelength will be blocked.

DWDM System Architecture

Transponder / Muxponder Converts client-side short-reach optics (grey optics) to a specific ITU DWDM wavelength (coloured optics) on the line side. A muxponder aggregates multiple lower-rate client signals (e.g., 10x10G) into one high-rate wavelength (100G).
Optical Multiplexer (MUX) Combines multiple wavelengths from individual transponders onto a single fiber for transmission. Uses thin-film filters or arrayed waveguide gratings (AWG). The reverse device at the destination is the DEMUX.
EDFA (Erbium-Doped Fiber Amplifier) Amplifies all wavelengths simultaneously in the optical domain without converting to electrical signals. Pumped by a 980 nm or 1480 nm laser that excites erbium ions, which release energy at 1550 nm as stimulated emission.
Optical Supervisory Channel (OSC) A separate wavelength (typically 1510 nm or 1625 nm, outside the EDFA gain band) used for management plane communication between DWDM nodes. Carries OSNR alarms, amplifier status, and provisioning data even when the traffic wavelengths are down.

OSNR: Optical Signal-to-Noise Ratio

Each EDFA adds Amplified Spontaneous Emission (ASE) noise across the full gain bandwidth. Unlike electrical amplifiers, optical amplifiers cannot filter between signal and noise once they are mixed. OSNR accumulates along the span chain: every amplifier stage degrades OSNR by its noise figure.

The OSNR budget determines the maximum number of amplified spans for a given bit rate and modulation format. Higher-order modulation (200G, 400G) requires higher OSNR to close the link. A 100G DP-QPSK coherent signal typically requires OSNR greater than 15 dB measured in a 0.1 nm bandwidth. A 400G DP-16QAM signal requires OSNR greater than 24 dB under the same conditions.

OSNR vs Dispersion: Two Independent Failure Modes

A DWDM link can fail because of insufficient OSNR (too many amplifiers, too much noise) or because of accumulated chromatic dispersion (pulse broadening from wavelength-dependent propagation). These are independent problems requiring different mitigation. Legacy systems used dispersion-compensating fiber modules inserted at amplifier sites. Modern coherent transceivers use DSP-based digital equalization and handle both simultaneously in the electrical domain after detection.

CWDM: The Simpler Sibling

Coarse WDM uses 20 nm channel spacing (versus 0.4-0.8 nm for DWDM) over a wavelength range from 1270 nm to 1610 nm. This wider spacing allows lower-cost, uncooled lasers that do not require precise temperature control. CWDM supports up to 18 channels (8 channels are commonly used in metro applications).

The critical limitation: CWDM channels span both the 1383 nm water absorption peak and the 1550 nm EDFA gain band, making EDFA amplification impossible without filtering out large portions of the spectrum. CWDM is therefore limited to unamplified spans -- typically under 80 km. For any long-haul application requiring amplification, DWDM is the only viable technology.

Practice Questions

  1. A DWDM system has 80 channels at 100G each on a 50 GHz grid. Calculate the total capacity of the system in Tbps. If you upgrade each wavelength from 100G to 400G using the same 80 channels, what is the new capacity?
  2. An EDFA has a gain of 20 dB and a noise figure of 6 dB. The input signal has an OSNR of 38 dB. After passing through this single amplifier, estimate the approximate OSNR degradation. (Use the formula: OSNR_out = 10*log10(1/(1/OSNR_in_linear + NF_linear/G_linear)) as a guide.)
  3. A network engineer wants to deploy a CWDM system for a 120 km dark fiber link between two data centers. What technical problem will they encounter, and what technology should they use instead?
  4. Why does a 400G coherent wavelength require higher OSNR than a 100G wavelength on the same DWDM system? What happens to a 400G signal when OSNR drops below its required threshold?

Key Takeaways

  • DWDM multiplies fiber capacity by carrying 40-96+ independent wavelengths on a single fiber pair
  • ITU-T G.694.1 defines the fixed grid; 50 GHz spacing is the current standard supporting 80 C-band channels
  • EDFAs amplify all wavelengths simultaneously in the optical domain -- the key enabler of long-haul DWDM
  • Each EDFA adds ASE noise; OSNR budget determines how many amplified spans a system can support
  • CWDM (20 nm spacing) cannot be EDFA-amplified and is limited to unamplified short-haul applications
  • Transponders convert grey optics to coloured (ITU-assigned) wavelengths for DWDM line side
  • Higher-order modulation (400G, 800G) requires significantly better OSNR than 100G
