The Merchant Silicon Revolution

For decades, enterprise networking was dominated by vertically integrated vendors -- Cisco, Juniper -- who designed proprietary ASICs, built switches around them, and charged premium prices for the combination. A 48-port 10G switch could cost $30,000 or more. The ASIC design cycle was 3-5 years. Customers were locked in.

Starting around 2012, companies like Broadcom, Mellanox (now NVIDIA), and Intel began selling high-performance switching ASICs directly to any hardware manufacturer willing to buy them. These merchant silicon chips matched or exceeded the performance of proprietary ASICs at a fraction of the cost. A Broadcom Trident chip powering a $3,000 open networking switch performs identically to a $25,000 Cisco switch using the same silicon.

The consequence was disaggregation: hardware, software (NOS), and support could be purchased from different vendors. Open networking was born. Today, hyperscalers build entire fabrics from merchant silicon switches running open-source NOS software like SONiC.

Broadcom Dominance Broadcom controls approximately 70% of the merchant silicon market. Their Tomahawk, Trident, and Jericho product lines power most DC switches sold today.
NVIDIA/Mellanox Spectrum Spectrum ASICs target latency-sensitive workloads and HPC. Spectrum-4 supports 51.2 Tbps throughput with advanced buffer management.
Intel Tofino Programmable forwarding plane via P4. Operators write custom packet-processing programs. Dominant in research and telemetry-heavy deployments.
Marvell Prestera/Teralynx Strong in edge and DCI applications. Teralynx targets 400G/800G spine applications with deep buffer support.

Forwarding Modes: Store-and-Forward vs. Cut-Through

How a switch handles a packet as it arrives fundamentally affects latency characteristics. The two primary forwarding modes represent different tradeoffs between latency and error detection.

Forwarding Mode Comparison
STORE-AND-FORWARD
  Frame arrives --> Buffered completely --> FCS checked --> Forwarded
  Latency: ~variable, depends on frame size
  64-byte frame  @ 10G: ~67 ns
  1518-byte frame @ 10G: ~1.2 us
  Error handling: Corrupt frames dropped before forwarding
  Use case: Any environment where FCS error detection matters

CUT-THROUGH
  Frame arrives --> Destination MAC read (first 14 bytes) --> Forwarded immediately
  Latency: Fixed, ~250-400 ns regardless of frame size
  Error handling: Corrupt frames may be forwarded; switch counts FCS errors
  Use case: HPC, financial trading, latency-sensitive east-west traffic

ADAPTIVE CUT-THROUGH (default on most DC switches)
  Starts in cut-through mode
  If FCS error rate on a port exceeds threshold --> switches to store-and-forward
  When error rate drops --> reverts to cut-through
  Best of both: normal latency when links are clean, error protection when degraded

Cut-Through and FCS Propagation

In cut-through mode, a switch begins forwarding before it has received the entire frame, including the FCS (Frame Check Sequence) at the end. If a frame arrives with a bit error in the payload, the switch has already forwarded the corrupt frame before detecting the error. The bad frame is delivered and only rejected at the destination. This is why cut-through is not used on links that might have physical layer issues.

Buffer Architectures

Buffering is what happens when a packet arrives faster than it can be sent out. Buffer size, location, and management strategy have profound effects on data center performance -- especially under many-to-one (incast) congestion patterns.

ArchitectureBuffer LocationBest ForDrawback
Shared bufferSingle pool shared by all portsGeneral purpose, unpredictable trafficOne elephant flow can starve all others
Output queuedPer output portPredictable traffic patternsSpeedup required for non-blocking; expensive at scale
VOQ (Virtual Output Queue)Per input, per destinationHigh-radix switches, eliminating HOL blockingScheduling complexity; requires central arbiter
Dynamic thresholdShared, with dynamic allocationMixed elephant/mice flowsComplex tuning; algorithm-dependent fairness

Head-of-Line (HOL) blocking is a critical problem in output-queued switches: if a packet at the front of an input queue is blocked waiting for a busy output port, all packets behind it are blocked too -- even if their destination output ports are idle. VOQ solves this by maintaining separate queues per destination at the input, so a blocked packet for output port A does not block a ready packet for output port B.

Shallow vs. Deep Buffer Switches

Shallow buffer switches (Tomahawk, Tofino) have 16-64 MB of on-chip SRAM. They rely on ECMP spreading traffic across many paths to prevent congestion rather than absorbing bursts. Deep buffer switches (Jericho, Spectrum-3) use external HBM (High Bandwidth Memory) or DRAM to provide gigabytes of buffer. Choose based on traffic pattern: predictable east-west workloads tolerate shallow buffers; edge and WAN-facing switches facing bursty WAN traffic need deep buffers.

TCAM: The Feature Budget

TCAM (Ternary Content-Addressable Memory) is the specialized memory that stores forwarding entries -- route tables, ACLs, QoS policies, and VXLAN mappings. TCAM can match on any combination of bit values with a "don't care" (X) mask per bit, which is what makes it possible to match on IP prefixes with variable-length masks in a single clock cycle.

TCAM is expensive -- it consumes roughly 10x the die area and 10x the power of equivalent SRAM. Every switch has a finite TCAM budget. When you enable VXLAN, EVPN, ACLs, QoS policies, and ECMP, each feature consumes TCAM entries. TCAM exhaustion is a real operational problem: adding a security policy can push a switch over its TCAM limit and cause the switch to stop accepting new routes.

TCAM Planning Guidelines

  • Plan TCAM allocation before deploying -- changing regions requires a reboot (service impact)
  • VXLAN VTEP table consumes TCAM -- plan for peak active VTEP count plus 20% headroom
  • ACL rules double-consume TCAM (ingress + egress in some ASIC architectures)
  • BGP route tables on leaf switches should be filtered aggressively -- leaves only need host routes, not full internet BGP table
  • Monitor with SNMP OIDs or streaming telemetry before you hit 80% utilization

Key Takeaways

  • Merchant silicon (Broadcom, NVIDIA, Intel) broke vendor lock-in -- identical performance at dramatically lower cost enables open networking
  • Trident is the leaf workhorse (feature-rich, 12.8 Tbps); Tomahawk is the spine workhorse (bandwidth-first, 51.2 Tbps)
  • Cut-through forwarding gives fixed low latency; store-and-forward gives FCS error detection -- adaptive cut-through gives both
  • VOQ eliminates HOL blocking by maintaining separate input queues per destination
  • TCAM is finite and shared across routing, ACLs, VXLAN, and QoS -- plan your allocation before deploying features
  • Port speed evolution is predictable: plan your fabric for 400G leaf uplinks and 800G spine ports for 2025+ designs
