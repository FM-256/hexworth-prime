Learning Objectives

  • Determine when an enterprise should run BGP vs. use static default routes
  • Design single-homed, dual-homed, and multihomed BGP connectivity
  • Distinguish transit from peering relationships and their policy implications
  • Configure provider-independent addressing and explain its operational value
  • Design an enterprise BGP policy for inbound and outbound traffic engineering

When Does an Enterprise Need BGP?

Most enterprises do not need to run BGP. A static default route pointing to your ISP is simpler, more stable, and fully adequate for single-homed sites. BGP becomes appropriate when the operational complexity it adds is outweighed by the benefits it provides.

Run BGP When:

  • Multihomed to two or more ISPs and need policy control over path selection
  • Owning provider-independent (PI) address space that must be advertised
  • Needing granular inbound traffic engineering across multiple links
  • Operating as a transit or hosting provider yourself
  • SLA requirements demand sub-second failover across redundant circuits

Static Routes Are Better When:

  • Single ISP connection -- BGP adds no value over a static default
  • Provider-assigned (PA) address space -- ISP handles advertisement
  • No traffic engineering requirements -- simpler is better
  • Network team lacks BGP operational expertise
  • Cost of ASN and PI space is not justified by requirements

Multihoming Design Patterns

Multihoming means connecting to more than one ISP. The goal is usually redundancy (failover if one ISP fails) and sometimes load balancing or performance optimization. BGP enables all three objectives simultaneously, with different design choices for each.

Single-Homed (No BGP)

One ISP, one link. Use a static default. Simple, no BGP required. No redundancy.

Dual-Homed (Same ISP)

Two links to the same ISP. Usually same ASN -- use MED or AS path to prefer primary link.

Multihomed (Different ISPs)

Two or more different ISPs. Requires public ASN and PI address space for full control. Local preference and AS path prepending for engineering.

Partial vs. Full Table

Accept a full BGP table (~900K prefixes) for maximum flexibility, or just a default route from each ISP for simplicity. Full table uses significant memory and CPU.

Transit vs. Peering

Understanding the commercial and technical distinction between transit and peering is fundamental to enterprise BGP design.

Transit

A paid relationship where a provider (upstream) carries your traffic to the rest of the internet. The provider accepts your routes and advertises them globally. You accept a full table or default route from the provider. You pay per Mbps or per Gb transferred. Most enterprises buy transit from one or more ISPs.

Peering

A settlement-free relationship between networks of similar size/type where each agrees to carry the other's traffic directly. Typically occurs at Internet Exchange Points (IXPs). You exchange only your own routes and your customers' routes -- not transit routes. No money changes hands. Large enterprises and CDNs use peering to reduce transit costs and improve latency.

From a BGP policy perspective, this distinction matters: routes learned from a transit provider can be re-advertised to all peers (they have paid you to carry their traffic). Routes learned from a peer should only be used for that peer's traffic -- not re-advertised to other peers or transit providers, which would make you an accidental transit AS.

Provider-Independent Addressing

Provider-assigned (PA) address space is allocated by your ISP from their block. It is tied to that ISP -- if you switch providers, you lose those addresses. Provider-independent (PI) space is assigned directly to you by your RIR (ARIN, RIPE, etc.) and remains yours regardless of provider.

For multihomed enterprises running BGP, PI space is essential. Your ISPs advertise your PI block on your behalf (with your AS as origin), and you maintain your addresses regardless of ISP changes. The minimum PI allocation for IPv4 from ARIN is currently /24.

Enterprise BGP -- Dual-ISP Design with PI Space
! Enterprise: AS 65200, PI prefix 203.0.113.0/24
! ISP-A: AS 64500 via 10.0.0.2 (primary)
! ISP-B: AS 64501 via 10.0.1.2 (backup)

! Anchor aggregate for PI space
ip route 203.0.113.0 255.255.255.0 Null0 254

router bgp 65200
 bgp router-id 1.1.1.1
 bgp log-neighbor-changes

 neighbor 10.0.0.2 remote-as 64500
 neighbor 10.0.0.2 description ISP-A-Primary
 neighbor 10.0.1.2 remote-as 64501
 neighbor 10.0.1.2 description ISP-B-Backup

 address-family ipv4 unicast
  network 203.0.113.0 mask 255.255.255.0

  ! Prefer ISP-A for outbound (exit) traffic
  neighbor 10.0.0.2 activate
  neighbor 10.0.0.2 route-map ISP-A-IN in
  neighbor 10.0.0.2 prefix-list OWN-PREFIXES out

  neighbor 10.0.1.2 activate
  neighbor 10.0.1.2 route-map ISP-B-IN in
  neighbor 10.0.1.2 prefix-list OWN-PREFIXES out
  ! Prepend to make ISP-B less attractive for inbound
  neighbor 10.0.1.2 route-map ISP-B-OUT out

! Outbound policy: prefer ISP-A by setting higher local pref
route-map ISP-A-IN permit 10
 set local-preference 200

route-map ISP-B-IN permit 10
 set local-preference 100

! Inbound policy: prepend our AS when advertising via ISP-B
route-map ISP-B-OUT permit 10
 set as-path prepend 65200 65200

! Only advertise our own PI space to both ISPs
ip prefix-list OWN-PREFIXES seq 10 permit 203.0.113.0/24

Receiving a Full vs. Partial BGP Table

Enterprises have three options for what to accept from their ISPs, each with different resource implications and traffic engineering capabilities:

BGP Table Options and Their Trade-offs
! Option 1: Default route only (lowest complexity)
! ISP sends only 0.0.0.0/0 -- you have no visibility into specific paths
! Works fine for single-homed; suboptimal for multihomed
neighbor 10.0.0.2 default-originate

! Option 2: Partial table (customer routes only)
! ISP sends your downstream customers' prefixes for direct paths
! Reduces table size, still enables some traffic engineering
! Typically: full table minus transit-only routes
neighbor 10.0.0.2 route-map PARTIAL-TABLE in

! Option 3: Full BGP table (~900,000 IPv4 prefixes as of 2026)
! Maximum flexibility -- you choose the best path to every destination
! Requires significant router memory (4GB+ for modern tables)
! Most enterprises with multiple ISPs use this for traffic optimization
! (no restriction -- just activate the neighbor)
neighbor 10.0.0.2 activate

Full Table Memory Requirements

As of 2026, the global BGP routing table contains approximately 900,000 IPv4 prefixes and 170,000+ IPv6 prefixes. Each prefix entry requires roughly 200-300 bytes of memory. A router accepting a full table from two providers needs at minimum 1-2GB of dedicated BGP memory. Check platform limits before enabling full table acceptance.

Design Exercise

A healthcare company (AS 65400) owns PI space 198.51.100.0/22. They have two data centers: DC1 connects to ISP-A (AS 64500) and DC2 connects to ISP-B (AS 64501). Requirements:

  1. Design the BGP topology. Should they run iBGP between DC1 and DC2? Why or why not? What connectivity is needed between the data centers?
  2. Primary outbound traffic should exit through ISP-A. Write the route-map configuration to achieve this across both data centers.
  3. Inbound traffic for the web server subnet 198.51.100.0/24 should prefer ISP-A's link at DC1. Traffic for the database subnet 198.51.101.0/24 should prefer ISP-B's link at DC2. Write the AS path prepending configuration.
  4. What is the minimum configuration to prevent the company from becoming an accidental transit AS (forwarding internet routes between ISP-A and ISP-B)?

Key Takeaways

  • BGP is justified for enterprises that are multihomed, own PI space, or need granular traffic engineering
  • Single-homed sites get nothing from BGP -- a static default route is simpler and more stable
  • Transit is paid, carries all internet routes; peering is settlement-free, exchanges only own routes
  • PI address space is essential for multihomed BGP -- PA space is tied to the ISP that allocated it
  • Local preference controls outbound traffic (which exit you use); AS path prepending influences inbound
  • Strict outbound prefix lists prevent the enterprise from becoming an accidental transit AS
  • Full BGP table requires significant router memory -- verify platform limits before enabling
