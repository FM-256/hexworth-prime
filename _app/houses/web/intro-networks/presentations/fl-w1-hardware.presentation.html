The Hub — Layer 1
The simplest (and dumbest) networking device. It repeats everything it receives to every port.
Why Hubs Are Obsolete
Hubs create a single collision domain — if two devices send at the same time, the signals collide and both must retransmit. They also send every frame to every port, wasting bandwidth and creating security risks. Switches replaced hubs in virtually all modern networks.
Slide 3 of 13
The Switch — Layer 2
The backbone of every LAN. A switch learns MAC addresses and forwards frames only to the correct port.
Managed Switch
Configurable via CLI or web interface. Supports VLANs, port mirroring, QoS, SNMP monitoring, PoE management. Used in enterprise networks. More expensive.
Unmanaged Switch
Plug and play. No configuration options. Learns MACs and forwards frames automatically. Perfect for small offices and home networks. Cheap and simple.
Slide 4 of 13
The Router — Layer 3
Routers connect different networks and make forwarding decisions based on IP addresses.
Key Functions
Inter-network routing: Connects LANs to each other and to the internet. NAT: Translates private IPs to a public IP for internet access. Firewall (basic): Most consumer routers include a stateful firewall. DHCP server: Assigns IPs to devices on the local network.
Slide 5 of 13
Wireless Access Point (AP)
Bridges the wireless and wired worlds. Clients connect via Wi-Fi; the AP connects to the switch via Ethernet.
An AP is not a router. A standalone AP only bridges wireless clients to the wired network. Consumer "wireless routers" combine an AP, switch, router, and DHCP server in one box — but enterprise environments use separate, dedicated APs connected to managed switches (often powered by PoE).
Slide 6 of 13
The Firewall
The gatekeeper. Inspects traffic and enforces rules about what gets in and out.
Hardware FirewallDedicated appliance (Cisco ASA, Palo Alto, Fortinet). Sits inline at the network perimeter. Inspects all traffic entering/leaving.
Software FirewallRuns on the host OS (Windows Defender Firewall, iptables). Protects individual machines. Often used alongside a hardware firewall.
NGFWNext-Generation Firewall. Deep packet inspection, application awareness, IPS, and threat intelligence in one appliance.
Slide 7 of 13
The Modem
Modulator/demodulator. Converts digital signals from your network to the signal type your ISP uses.
Cable modem: DOCSIS standard, uses coaxial cable. DSL modem: Uses phone lines (twisted pair). Fiber ONT: Optical Network Terminal, converts fiber optic light signals to Ethernet. Many ISPs now provide a gateway — a modem + router + AP in one device.
Slide 8 of 13
Patch Panel & Media Converter
Infrastructure components that organize and adapt your physical connections.
Patch Panel
A passive panel with rows of RJ-45 ports. Permanent cables from wall jacks terminate at the back; short patch cables connect the front to the switch. Provides organization, labeling, and easy moves/adds/changes without re-running cables. No electronics — purely physical.
Media Converter
Converts one cable type to another — most commonly fiber optic to copper Ethernet (or vice versa). Used when a long fiber run from an ISP or building backbone needs to connect to a copper switch. Small, powered device.
Slide 9 of 13
Power over Ethernet (PoE)
One cable for data AND power. No separate power adapter needed.
802.3af (PoE)15.4W per port. Enough for VoIP phones, basic APs, simple cameras.
802.3at (PoE+)25.5W per port. Supports PTZ cameras, high-power APs, thin clients.
802.3bt (PoE++)Up to 90W per port. Laptops, digital signage, LED lighting.
Why PoE matters: Install an AP on a ceiling without running a separate power cable. Deploy security cameras anywhere with just one Ethernet drop. Fewer cables, simpler infrastructure, centralized power management.
Slide 10 of 13
NIC — Network Interface Card
The hardware that connects a device to the network. Every networked device has at least one.
Types of NICs
Wired (Ethernet): RJ-45 port, built into most motherboards. Speeds: 100 Mbps, 1 Gbps, 10 Gbps. Wireless: Built-in Wi-Fi adapter in laptops/phones, or add-on PCIe/USB for desktops. Fiber: SFP/SFP+ modules on servers and high-end equipment for 10/25/100 Gbps connections.
Slide 11 of 13
More Devices: Bridges, Repeaters, Load Balancers
Supporting cast members that solve specific networking problems.
Bridge
Layer 2
Connects two network segments at Layer 2. Learns MAC addresses on each side and only forwards frames that need to cross. Essentially a 2-port switch. Rarely deployed as standalone hardware today.
Repeater
Layer 1
Regenerates a weakened signal to extend cable distance. Works at the electrical/physical level. Ethernet cables max out at 100m — a repeater resets that limit. Wi-Fi extenders are wireless repeaters.
Load Balancer
Layer 4-7
Distributes incoming traffic across multiple servers so no single server is overwhelmed. Methods: round-robin, least connections, IP hash. Essential for high-availability web applications.
Slide 12 of 13
Device Comparison at a Glance
Which device works at which layer, and what does it use to make decisions?
Hub / Repeater
Layer 1 — Physical
Operates on electrical signals. No intelligence. Broadcasts everything.
Switch / Bridge
Layer 2 — Data Link
Uses MAC addresses. Forwards frames to specific ports. Creates separate collision domains.
Router
Layer 3 — Network
Uses IP addresses. Routes packets between different networks. Maintains a routing table.
Firewall (NGFW)
Layers 3-7
Inspects packets, sessions, and application data. Enforces security policy. Blocks threats.
Access Point
Layers 1-2
Bridges wireless clients to the wired network. Operates at the physical (radio) and data link layers.
Load Balancer
Layers 4-7
Distributes traffic by port (L4) or URL/header (L7). Ensures high availability.

Networking Hardware: Solved
Key Takeaways
1 Hubs (L1) repeat to all ports. Switches (L2) forward to specific ports using MAC addresses. Switches replaced hubs.
2 Routers (L3) connect different networks using IP addresses. They provide NAT, DHCP, and basic firewall functions.
3 Firewalls enforce security rules. Hardware firewalls sit at the perimeter; NGFWs inspect at Layers 3-7.
4 Access Points bridge wireless to wired. They are not routers — enterprise APs are dedicated devices powered by PoE.
5 PoE sends power over Ethernet: 802.3af (15W), 802.3at (25W), 802.3bt (90W). Used for APs, cameras, phones.
6 Patch panels organize cable runs. Media converters bridge fiber to copper. Load balancers distribute traffic across servers.
Back in that server room: the rack holds switches (L2) at the core, a router (L3) connecting to the ISP, a firewall (L3-7) at the perimeter, a patch panel organizing every cable run, and PoE-powered APs on the ceiling. Every device has a purpose. Now you can name them all.
