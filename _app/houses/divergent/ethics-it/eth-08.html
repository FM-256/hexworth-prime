Review 01 / 03 -- Chapter 4: Privacy

Chapter 4 maps the legal and ethical landscape of privacy in IT. The key conceptual tools are the privacy type framework, the federal statute matrix, and the design principles that distinguish legal compliance from genuine privacy protection.

Law Sector Key Obligation
HIPAA Healthcare Secure protected health information; breach notification; covered entities and business associates bound through BAA
COPPA Children (under 13) Verifiable parental consent before collecting personal information from children
FERPA Education Student educational records are private; consent required for most disclosures
ECPA Electronic communications Prohibits warrantless interception; governs employer monitoring and law enforcement access
GDPR EU personal data (global reach) Data minimization, purpose limitation, right to erasure, consent, breach notification within 72 hours
PATRIOT Act National security Expands government surveillance authority; national security letters with gag orders
Key Case: Carpenter v. United States (2018)

The Supreme Court ruled that accessing seven or more days of cell-site location information requires a warrant. The Court rejected applying the third-party doctrine to comprehensive digital location tracking -- such data reveals "the privacies of life" in ways that analog-era precedents did not contemplate. This is the clearest modern statement that digital surveillance requires Fourth Amendment analysis, not just the old third-party doctrine.

Privacy by Design: Four Principles

Data minimization -- collect only what is necessary. Purpose limitation -- use data only for the purpose disclosed at collection. Retention limits -- delete when no longer needed. Meaningful consent -- users understand what they are agreeing to. These are not just GDPR obligations; they are the ethical floor for any system that handles personal data.

The U.S. has no omnibus privacy law. Sector-specific statutes leave significant gaps. GDPR fills some of those gaps for EU-facing products. Privacy by design fills the rest -- not because it is required, but because the alternative is to build systems that treat people's personal data as an asset to be extracted.

Review 02 / 03 -- Chapter 5: Freedom of Expression

Chapter 5 maps the constitutional and statutory framework governing online speech and the ethical questions that legal analysis leaves unresolved. The three key structures are the First Amendment (government action only), Section 230 (platform liability immunity), and the DMCA (copyright takedowns and their abuse).

Concept What It Does Limit
First Amendment Prohibits government from restricting most speech Does not apply to private actors; unprotected categories include obscenity, defamation, true threats, incitement
Section 230 Immunizes platforms from liability for user-generated content; permits good-faith moderation without losing immunity Does not protect against federal criminal law, IP claims, or sex trafficking liability (FOSTA-SESTA)
DMCA Section 512 Provides notice-and-takedown process for copyright infringement claims Counter-notice mechanism imposes costs on the speaker; false takedown notices carry low consequences for the requester
Defamation False statement of fact + fault (actual malice for public figures; negligence for private individuals) + damages Truth is an absolute defense; opinions generally protected; Section 230 insulates platforms from third-party claims
Key Case: Reno v. ACLU (1997)

The Supreme Court struck down the CDA's indecency provisions (unanimous). The internet receives the highest level of First Amendment protection -- equivalent to print media, not broadcast. Users choose what to access; they are not passive recipients. This holding established the framework under which the modern internet operates, with platforms (not governments) as the primary governors of online speech.

Remove

Prevents harm to victims of harassment or misinformation. Risk: over-removal chills legitimate speech.

Label

Warns users without removing speech. Risk: labels may increase engagement with contested content.

No Action

Maximizes free expression. Risk: harmful content persists and may be algorithmically amplified.

The legal framework gives platforms enormous power and almost no legal obligation to exercise that power responsibly. The ethical framework asks what responsible exercise of that power looks like. Those are different questions, and only the second one matters for professional practice.

Review 03 / 03 -- Chapter 6: Intellectual Property

Chapter 6 maps the four IP protection mechanisms and their application in software development. Key distinctions: copyright protects expression (not ideas), patents protect inventions (requires registration), trade secrets protect confidential information (requires secrecy, not registration), trademarks protect brand identity (common law rights plus optional registration).

Type What It Protects Duration Critical Limit
Copyright Original expression fixed in tangible form (code, docs, UI) Life + 70 years; work-for-hire 95 years from publication Does not protect ideas, facts, or functional elements -- only the specific expression
Patent Novel, non-obvious, useful inventions and processes 20 years from filing Requires full USPTO examination; software patents contested under Alice Corp. limitations
Trade Secret Confidential information with economic value Indefinite while secrecy maintained Lost if disclosed or if owner fails to take reasonable protective measures
Trademark Source identifiers -- names, logos, product identities Indefinite with continued use and renewal Can become generic (escalator, thermos) and lose protection through widespread descriptive use
Open Source License Type Core Obligation
MIT / Apache 2.0 Permissive Attribution required; no copyleft; commercial use and closed-source derivatives permitted
GPL v3 Copyleft Derivative works must be GPL v3; source code must be available to recipients
AGPL v3 Strong copyleft Network use (SaaS) triggers copyleft; effectively prohibits closed commercial SaaS without a commercial license
Key Case: Waymo v. Uber

Levandowski downloaded 14,000 confidential files before leaving Google to found a competing company later acquired by Uber. Uber settled for ~$245M in equity. Levandowski received 18 months federal prison for trade secret theft. The key distinction: the knowledge in his head was legal to use; the files were not. Trade secret theft does not require unauthorized access -- it requires misappropriation of confidential information obtained in a position of trust.

IP law is a system of temporary monopolies designed to incentivize creation. Copyright, patents, and trade secret protection all assume that at some point the information becomes public. The ethical question is whether the person using, sharing, or building on others' work is doing so honestly and in accordance with the terms under which that work was made available.
