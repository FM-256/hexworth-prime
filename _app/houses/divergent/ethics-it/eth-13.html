01 / 05 -- Contingent Workers, H-1B Visas, and Workforce Structure Ethics

Contingent workers are employees hired on a temporary, contract, or part-time basis without the full benefits and protections extended to permanent employees. In IT, contingent labor is widespread: contract developers, short-term consultants, outsourced support staff, and gig-economy platforms that supply on-demand technical work.

The ethical issue is not whether contingent work is inherently wrong. It is whether organizations use contingent labor structures to avoid obligations they would otherwise have to workers doing the same work. When permanent employees and contractors sit side by side doing identical work and the contractors receive no benefits, no job security, and reduced pay, the organization is using classification to externalize costs onto individuals who did not choose that arrangement.

The H-1B visa program allows US employers to hire foreign workers in specialty occupations. The program's stated purpose is to address genuine skill shortages. Its ethical controversy centers on two documented patterns: some employers use H-1B workers to reduce labor costs rather than fill genuine gaps, and some have required US workers to train their H-1B replacements as a condition of receiving severance. Both practices are legal in most circumstances. Neither is defensible under any major professional ethics code.

The contract that classifies a worker as an "independent contractor" does not change what the worker does. It changes who pays the payroll tax and who bears the risk if the work slows down. The law permits this. Ethics asks whether the organization chose this structure to serve the worker or to serve the spreadsheet.

02 / 05 -- Offshore Outsourcing: Ethical Dimensions

Offshore outsourcing transfers IT work -- software development, data processing, technical support -- to organizations in other countries, typically where labor costs are lower. It is one of the most debated practices in IT management ethics because it simultaneously raises questions about domestic employment, global labor standards, and the obligations companies have to workers in both countries.

Stakeholder Potential Benefit Potential Harm
US organization Reduced labor costs, access to global talent, 24-hour coverage Quality control risks, intellectual property exposure, reputational risk
US workers Some positions created in oversight, project management, and strategic roles Job displacement, skills atrophy in offshore domains, wage pressure
Offshore workers Employment, skill development, higher wages relative to local alternatives Labor standards may be lower than US equivalents; potential exploitation
Society Lower cost technology products; global economic development Concentration of IT unemployment in specific domestic communities; tax base erosion

The ethical obligation of an IT manager considering offshore outsourcing is not to refuse to outsource. It is to conduct the decision transparently, consider all stakeholders rather than only the cost line, treat displaced workers fairly, and ensure that offshore operations meet ethical labor standards rather than exploiting the gap between domestic and foreign regulation.

The decision to outsource is a business decision. The decision to outsource to a vendor that violates labor standards because the price is lower is an ethical decision. They are not the same thing.

03 / 05 -- The Gig Economy and Platform Labor Ethics

The gig economy encompasses work arrangements where individuals perform discrete tasks or projects mediated by digital platforms, typically classified as independent contractors. In IT, this includes on-demand development platforms (Upwork, Toptal), freelance marketplaces, and crowdsourced testing or annotation services.

The central ethical question is worker classification. Platform operators argue that gig workers are independent contractors who choose their engagements, set their hours, and operate as businesses. Critics argue that the classification is a legal fiction designed to avoid benefit obligations and that many gig workers are economically dependent on a single platform in ways functionally indistinguishable from employment.

California's AB5 (2019) and subsequent legal battles over worker classification illustrate that the ethical debate has migrated into law. IT managers who rely heavily on gig labor carry an obligation to ensure that the classification accurately reflects the working relationship and is not used primarily to avoid obligations to workers.

When a platform controls what work a worker can take, how much they are paid, whether they keep their account, and when they are deactivated -- but calls them a contractor because it does not provide benefits -- the classification describes a desired business outcome, not an actual employment relationship.

04 / 05 -- Whistle-blowing: Obligation, Risk, and Retaliation

Whistle-blowing occurs when an employee reports illegal, unethical, or unsafe organizational conduct to someone in a position to take corrective action. The report may be made internally (to management or compliance) or externally (to regulators, law enforcement, or the press).

Professional ethics codes generally support whistle-blowing when internal remedies have been exhausted or are unavailable, and when the harm being disclosed is significant. The ACM Code states that members should "report violations of this Code to appropriate authorities." The IEEE Code requires members to "report unethical or illegal practices to appropriate authorities."

Reference Case: Frances Haugen (Facebook / Meta, 2021)

What she disclosed: Haugen, a former Facebook data scientist, copied internal research documents before resigning and provided them to the Wall Street Journal and the US Securities and Exchange Commission. The documents showed that Facebook's internal research had identified significant harms from its platforms -- including Instagram's impact on teenage mental health -- that were not disclosed to the public or regulators.

Ethical justification: Haugen argued that internal channels had failed -- that she had raised concerns through proper channels and they were dismissed. She concluded that external disclosure was necessary to force the corrective action that internal escalation could not produce.

The tension: Haugen violated her employment agreement and confidentiality obligations. Whether that violation was ethically justified depends on whether you believe the harm she was disclosing was serious enough to override those obligations. Most ethics frameworks provide a framework for that analysis rather than a simple answer.

Federal and state whistle-blower protections vary significantly. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act (SOX) provides protection for whistle-blowers at publicly traded companies who report securities fraud. The False Claims Act provides protection and financial incentives for reporting fraud against the federal government. Retaliation against whistle-blowers is common despite legal protections.

Most whistle-blowers did not plan to become whistle-blowers. They tried internal channels first. When those failed, they faced a choice between professional loyalty and professional responsibility. The codes say which comes first.

05 / 05 -- Green Computing and E-Waste

Green computing refers to the design, manufacture, use, and disposal of computing technology in ways that minimize environmental impact. The ethical obligation for IT organizations encompasses the full lifecycle of technology: energy consumption in data centers, hardware procurement standards, device refresh cycles, and end-of-life disposal.

Electronic waste (e-waste) is one of the fastest growing waste streams globally. Discarded computers, servers, mobile devices, and peripherals contain toxic materials including lead, mercury, cadmium, and beryllium. Improper disposal contaminates soil and groundwater. Much of the world's e-waste is shipped to developing countries where informal recycling operations expose workers and communities to these toxins without protective measures.

IT organizations face a specific ethical obligation: the technology lifecycle that begins with a server purchase in a US data center may end with a child in Ghana hand-sorting circuit boards in an open burn site. That chain of consequence does not disappear because the disposal contract was awarded to a third party.

Responsible practices include certified e-waste recycling (R2 or e-Stewards certification), extended device lifecycles that reduce refresh frequency, procurement of EPEAT-certified hardware, and data center energy efficiency standards (PUE metrics). Each is a practice choice with measurable environmental impact.

The IT organization that replaces its entire server fleet every three years for performance reasons and ships the old hardware to an uncertified recycler has made a choice. It has chosen not to account for the cost of that choice because the cost lands on someone else.
