Case Study: Amazon Workplace Monitoring

Case Facts: Amazon Warehouse Monitoring

Amazon's warehouse operations use extensive monitoring systems: handheld scanners that track worker productivity in real time, AI-powered cameras that monitor worker movements, algorithmic performance management that automatically generates disciplinary actions when productivity falls below thresholds, and wristband devices (patented, not universally deployed) that can track hand movements.

Workers are required to meet productivity quotas that the algorithms track and report. The system is automated to the degree that some disciplinary actions are generated without human review of the circumstances.

Utilitarian Monitoring enables efficiency gains that reduce costs for consumers. But harm to workers (injury rates, stress, turnover) must be included in the calculation. The net welfare calculus is contested.
Deontological Does the monitoring treat workers as means to efficiency rather than as ends in themselves? Automated discipline without human review removes judgment from the process in a way Kant would analyze as a duty violation.
Fairness Behind the veil of ignorance: would a rational person choose a monitoring system this intensive if they did not know whether they would be the employer or the worker? The veil likely produces a different answer.
Common Good What does this system do to the conditions of dignified work as a social institution? If replicated across the economy, what is the cumulative effect on working conditions?

Case Study: Carpenter v. United States (2018)

Case Facts: Carpenter v. United States

Timothy Carpenter was convicted of robbery based partly on cell-site location information (CSLI) obtained by the FBI from his cell phone carrier without a warrant, using only a court order under the Stored Communications Act. The government argued the third-party doctrine applied: data voluntarily shared with a third party (the carrier) carries no Fourth Amendment protection.

The Supreme Court ruled 5-4 that obtaining CSLI without a warrant violated the Fourth Amendment. Chief Justice Roberts wrote that the comprehensive nature of CSLI -- which can reveal a person's movements over months -- creates a unique privacy threat that the third-party doctrine was not designed to address.

What Would You Do?

You are a data architect at a telecommunications company. Law enforcement presents a court order (not a warrant) requesting six months of CSLI for 500 customers connected to an investigation. The SCA permits compliance with a court order. The data request would expose the daily movements of 500 people, most of whom are not suspects. What does the Carpenter decision mean for your compliance decision? What does your professional ethics code require beyond the minimum legal standard?

Privacy by Design

PrincipleDescriptionImplementation
Proactive, not reactivePrivacy protections are built in before systems are deployed, not added after privacy failures occurPrivacy impact assessments before system launch; threat modeling includes privacy threats
Privacy as the defaultThe default setting for any system should be maximum privacy; users must opt in to share more, not opt out to share lessDefault data collection minimum; granular consent; clear opt-in mechanisms
Data minimizationCollect only the data necessary for the stated purpose; do not retain it beyond what is neededData retention policies; purpose limitation documentation; regular data audits
End-to-end securityPrivacy protections span the full data lifecycle from collection through disposalEncryption at rest and in transit; secure disposal; access control throughout lifecycle
Warehouse Lore

Privacy by design is not a compliance checkbox. It is an architectural philosophy. A system that was not designed with privacy in mind cannot be made private by adding disclosures afterward. The disclosures explain what the system does. They do not change what the system does.
