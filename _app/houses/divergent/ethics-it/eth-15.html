Review // Week 1 -- Ch. 1-3
Foundation

Ethics, Professionalism, Cybersecurity

  • Ethics is a code of behavior defined by a group. It is distinct from morality (internal) and law (external). Something can be legal but unethical, or ethical but illegal.
  • The VW emissions scandal (2009-2015): defeat device software was maintained by multiple engineers over years through incremental decisions that prioritized institutional loyalty over professional responsibility.
  • IT professionals have obligations to five relationships: employer, client, supplier, user, and society. When these conflict, society comes last in daily practice but first in ethical priority.
  • Reasonable assurance in cybersecurity is a proportionality standard, not a guarantee -- controls must be appropriate to the risk level, not exhaustive of every possible control.
  • The Sony Pictures hack (2014) exposed 47,000 employee records including SSNs and medical data. The ethical failure was not the breach -- it was security controls that were not proportionate to the sensitivity of the data held.
Review // Week 2 -- Ch. 4-6
Rights

Privacy, Expression, Property

  • The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable government searches. HIPAA, COPPA, and FERPA create specific privacy obligations for healthcare, children's data, and educational records. The ECPA and PATRIOT Act govern electronic surveillance.
  • Section 230 of the CDA (1996) protects platforms from liability for third-party content. Its ethical legitimacy is contested as platforms scale to billions of users with algorithmic amplification.
  • Copyright protects expression (70+ years). Patents protect inventions (20 years). Trade secrets protect confidential business information indefinitely if protected. These are distinct rights with different obligations.
  • Reverse engineering for interoperability is generally permitted under US law. Competitive intelligence becomes corporate espionage when it involves unauthorized access, deception, or theft.
  • The Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) prohibits circumvention of technological protection measures and provides a safe harbor for platforms that respond promptly to takedown notices.
Review // Week 3 -- Ch. 7-8
Impact

Software Quality, AI, Society

  • The Therac-25 (1985-1987): removing hardware safety interlocks without adequate software validation caused lethal radiation overdoses. Engineers dismissed early warnings. The lesson: safety-critical software requires independent verification, not assumption of inherited safety.
  • The 2018 Uber autonomous vehicle fatality: detection suppression implemented to reduce false-positive phantom braking also degraded true-positive response. The engineering tradeoff was made before anyone died and was foreseeable.
  • IBM Watson for Oncology was trained primarily on hypothetical patient scenarios and recommended "unsafe and incorrect" treatments. It was marketed as evidence-based. This was not prominently disclosed to hospital customers.
  • The productivity paradox (Solow, 1987): IT investment does not automatically produce productivity gains. Gains require complementary organizational change. Workers displaced by automation bear costs they did not choose to accept.
  • AI displacement increasingly targets routine cognitive work (legal review, radiology, loan processing) -- categories previously considered automation-resistant. This creates novel ethical obligations for developers and employers.
Review // Week 4 -- Ch. 9-10 + Appendices
Society

Social Media, Organizations, Governance

  • Cambridge Analytica obtained Facebook profile data on 87 million users through friend-network API access. The data was used for psychographic political targeting. Facebook knew of the breach for over two years before disclosing it to users. The FTC fine was $5 billion.
  • Engagement algorithms optimize for interaction. False and emotionally provocative content generates more interaction than accurate content. Claiming algorithms are neutral is technically illiterate -- algorithms embed the values of whoever designed the objective function.
  • Contingent workers and H-1B visa exploitation: using worker classification to avoid employment obligations, or requiring US workers to train their replacements, are legal in most circumstances but indefensible under professional ethics codes.
  • Frances Haugen's disclosure of internal Facebook research documents is a reference case for whistle-blowing analysis: internal channels failed, harm was significant, external disclosure may have been justified despite violating confidentiality obligations.
  • All four major codes (ACM, IEEE, PMI, AITP) agree on one structural priority: public welfare supersedes obligations to employers and clients when they conflict. This is not aspirational -- it is the stated hierarchy in each code.
