Review: Ethics, Professionalism, and Law (Ch. 1-2)

Ethics is a code of behavior defined by the group to which an individual belongs. It is distinct from morality (internal, self-enforced) and law (external, government-enforced). The law is a minimum standard. Professional codes often require more.

IT professionals carry obligations that extend beyond their employment relationship: to clients, suppliers, users, and society. When obligations conflict, society's interest takes ethical priority -- even when it comes last in daily practice.

The four major ethical frameworks (stockholder, stakeholder, utilitarian, deontological) provide different analytical lenses. The transparency test is a practical heuristic: if you would not be comfortable with your decision reported publicly, it requires further ethical analysis before proceeding.

Review: Cybersecurity Ethics (Ch. 3)

Reasonable assurance is a proportionality standard: controls must be appropriate to the assets, threats, and obligations involved. It is not a guarantee. Failing to implement controls proportionate to the sensitivity of the data held is an ethical failure.

Incident response ethics prioritizes affected parties over institutional protection. Disclosure obligations run to users whose data was exposed -- delay and concealment are second ethical failures layered on the original breach.

Week 1 Case Comparison

VW Emissions Scandal Defeat device software. Institutional normalization of ethical compromise over years. Multiple engineers. No single rogue actor -- incremental organizational drift. The decision to deceive regulators was maintained across six years and multiple engineering teams.
SAP FCPA Violation IT systems maintained the payment classification structure that concealed bribery. IT professionals processed transactions they knew or should have known were improperly classified. Professional code obligation: refuse instructions requiring falsification.
Sony Pictures Breach 47,000 employee SSNs and medical records exposed. Security controls not proportionate to data sensitivity. Prior warnings unaddressed. The ethical failure preceded the breach -- it was the failure to maintain reasonable assurance, not the attacker's action.
