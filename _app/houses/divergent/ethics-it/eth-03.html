The Four Core Security Tradeoffs

Cybersecurity ethics is not primarily about preventing attacks. It is about navigating the inherent tradeoffs between competing values that security decisions require.

Privacy vs. Safety Monitoring systems that improve security reduce privacy. The ethical question is proportionality -- does the safety gain justify the privacy cost?
Security vs. Usability Controls that reduce risk often reduce usability. Overly restrictive controls that users route around are not effective security -- they are security theater that creates false confidence.
Disclosure vs. Concealment When a breach or vulnerability is discovered, the ethical obligation is disclosure -- to affected parties, regulators, and sometimes the public. Delay or concealment is a second ethical failure layered on the first.
Access Control vs. Availability Restricting access prevents unauthorized use but can impede legitimate operations. In healthcare, overly restrictive access controls have contributed to patient harm when clinicians could not access needed information.

Incident Types and Perpetrator Categories

Perpetrator categories in cybersecurity ethics matter because they affect the ethical analysis of organizational responsibility:

Script Kiddies Low-skill attackers using existing tools. Organizations have a clear obligation to defend against known, easily executable attacks. Failing to patch known vulnerabilities is ethically indefensible.
Criminal Organizations Profit-motivated attackers with significant resources. "Reasonable assurance" requires controls proportionate to the threat level -- not every possible control, but those appropriate to the risk.
Nation-State Actors State-sponsored attackers with advanced persistent threat (APT) capabilities. Against sophisticated nation-state adversaries, even well-resourced organizations may not be able to guarantee defense -- changing the ethical standard appropriately.

Reasonable Assurance: The Proportionality Standard

"Reasonable assurance" is the ethical and legal standard that governs cybersecurity obligations. It does not require eliminating all risk -- which is not achievable. It requires implementing controls that are proportionate to: the sensitivity of the data held, the credible threat landscape, and the organization's resources and obligations.

ElementDescription
Asset valueThe higher the sensitivity or value of the data, the more rigorous the required controls. PII, financial data, and health records require more than publicly available information.
Threat landscapeControls must be calibrated to realistic threats. Defending a small credit union against the same threat model as a major bank is neither required nor proportionate.
Known vulnerabilitiesFailing to remediate known, patchable vulnerabilities when a breach results is ethically indefensible -- regardless of attacker sophistication. This is the negligence standard in cybersecurity.
Incident responseA proportionate response plan is part of reasonable assurance. The absence of an IR plan when significant data is held is itself an ethical failure.

Incident Response Ethics

IR PhaseEthical Obligation
PreparationMaintain documented IR plan; train staff; establish relationships with law enforcement and legal counsel before an incident occurs
Detection and AnalysisInvestigate promptly and thoroughly; do not suppress findings that are inconvenient; preserve evidence chain of custody for potential legal proceedings
ContainmentAct to limit harm to affected parties, not primarily to protect the organization's reputation; containment decisions should prioritize user harm, not institutional optics
NotificationNotify affected parties promptly and completely; legal minimums are a floor, not an ethical ceiling; delay to assess legal exposure at the cost of harming affected parties is ethically indefensible
Recovery and ReviewImplement genuine improvements, not optics; post-incident review should identify what failed ethically as well as technically

Case Study: Sony Pictures Breach (2014)

Case Facts: Sony Pictures Entertainment Breach

In November 2014, attackers (attributed to North Korean state-sponsored actors) breached Sony Pictures Entertainment and exfiltrated approximately 100 terabytes of data including unreleased films, internal emails, executive compensation data, and the personal data of approximately 47,000 current and former employees including Social Security numbers and medical records.

Sony had been warned about security vulnerabilities prior to the breach. The breach exposed that Sony's security controls were not proportionate to the sensitivity of the data it held. The employee personal data -- SSNs, medical records, salary data -- was particularly exposed because it was not segregated or protected with controls appropriate to its sensitivity.

FailureEthical Analysis
Security controls not proportionate to data heldSony held medical records and SSNs for 47,000 people. Reasonable assurance required controls proportionate to that sensitivity. The controls in place were not adequate to that standard.
Prior warnings not adequately addressedPre-breach warnings about security vulnerabilities that were not remediated constitute a failure of the reasonable assurance standard. Known, unpatched vulnerabilities in a system holding sensitive PII is an ethical failure regardless of attacker sophistication.
Duty to employees47,000 people trusted Sony with their most sensitive personal data as a condition of employment. They did not choose to take that risk. Sony had an obligation to protect that data proportionate to its sensitivity. This obligation ran to the user relationship -- the employees -- not only to the employer's interests.
What Would You Do?

You are the CISO at a mid-sized entertainment company. You have an incident response plan. You become aware that the company holds the SSNs and medical data of 40,000 former employees in an unencrypted database that has not been patched in 18 months. The data is there because no one ever got around to deleting it after the HR system migration. What is your ethical obligation, and what does "reasonable assurance" require you to do before an incident occurs?
