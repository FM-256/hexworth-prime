Work-for-Hire Doctrine

Software created by an employee within the scope of their employment is owned by the employer under the work-for-hire doctrine. This applies to code written during work hours on company equipment for work purposes. Code written entirely on personal time using personal equipment for purposes unrelated to employment may belong to the employee -- but employment agreements frequently include broad IP assignment clauses that may override this.

Case Study: Waymo v. Uber and Trade Secret Theft

Case Facts: Waymo v. Uber (2017-2018)

Anthony Levandowski, a former Google/Waymo autonomous vehicle engineer, downloaded approximately 14,000 confidential files before leaving to found his own company (Otto), which was subsequently acquired by Uber. Waymo alleged the files included trade secrets related to its LiDAR sensor designs. Levandowski pleaded the Fifth Amendment in the civil case. In 2020, he pleaded guilty to criminal trade secret theft charges. Uber settled with Waymo for approximately $245 million in equity.

The Taking Levandowski downloaded 14,000 files before departure. This was not inadvertent. The scale of the download makes any "I forgot to delete them" defense implausible.
The Transfer The files were used in founding a competing company. The competitive advantage sought was built on IP that belonged to his employer. This is the core trade secret violation.
Uber's Exposure Uber acquired Otto knowing Levandowski had left Google. The question of what Uber knew and when it knew it determined whether Uber had liability for receiving stolen trade secrets.
IT Professional Obligation The professional obligation was clear at the point of download. An IT professional who downloads employer IP before departure for use at a competitor has violated their professional obligations -- regardless of whether the law catches up to them.

Cybersquatting and Domain Name Ethics

Cybersquatting is the practice of registering domain names that incorporate another party's trademark with the intent to profit from the trademark owner's goodwill. The Anti-Cybersquatting Consumer Protection Act (ACPA) provides a cause of action against bad-faith domain registration.

The World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) Uniform Domain Name Dispute Resolution Policy (UDRP) provides an arbitration mechanism for domain disputes. To prevail, the complainant must show: the domain is identical or confusingly similar to a trademark, the respondent has no legitimate interest, and the domain was registered and used in bad faith.

What Would You Do?

A startup you advise has developed a successful product under a name that turns out to be a registered trademark in the EU (but not in the US). Warehouse Lore

The line between competitive intelligence and corporate espionage is not ambiguous in most cases. It is determined by the method used, not the information obtained. The same piece of competitor information obtained by reading a public document and obtained by hacking a competitor's system creates completely different ethical and legal situations. The information is identical. The act is not.
