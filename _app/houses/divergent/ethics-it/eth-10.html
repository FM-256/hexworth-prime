01 / 05 -- IT and Productivity: The Productivity Paradox

Technology is assumed to increase productivity. The evidence is more complicated. The productivity paradox, identified by economist Robert Solow in 1987, observed that massive investments in computing technology during the 1970s and 1980s did not produce corresponding gains in measured productivity. Solow's observation: "You can see the computer age everywhere except in the productivity statistics."

The paradox was partially resolved by the late 1990s, when productivity gains from IT investments became measurable -- primarily through business process redesign, network effects, and supply chain optimization. The lesson was not that IT does not improve productivity. It was that IT investments require organizational and process changes to produce gains. Technology alone does not create value.

This matters ethically because organizations often justify significant disruption -- layoffs, restructuring, displacement of workers -- on the promise of productivity gains that may not materialize without complementary organizational investment. The workers displaced carry the cost of a bet they did not make.

The quarterly earnings report does not capture the career of a 47-year-old data entry clerk whose job was automated. The productivity gain is real. The cost is externalized.

02 / 05 -- AI, Machine Learning, and Workforce Displacement

Automation has always displaced workers. The ethical question is not whether displacement happens -- it always has -- but whether the pace and scale of AI-driven displacement creates obligations that did not exist with earlier automation waves.

Earlier automation primarily replaced routine manual labor. AI and machine learning are increasingly capable of replacing cognitive work: legal document review, radiology readings, financial analysis, customer service, and software testing. This targets workers who previously considered themselves automation-resistant.

Category Automation Risk Level Example Roles
Routine manual High (already automated) Assembly line workers, data entry, toll collectors
Routine cognitive High (rapidly automating) Paralegals, radiology technicians, loan officers
Non-routine cognitive Medium (augmentation likely) Physicians, attorneys, software engineers, managers
Social/relational Low (difficult to automate) Social workers, teachers, therapists, caregivers

The ethical obligations of technology companies and employers are contested. Minimum positions include adequate notice, retraining investment, and transition support. Stronger positions argue for an automation tax to fund universal reskilling programs. The debate is active and unresolved. IT professionals who build these systems are participants whether they choose to engage or not.

The engineer who builds an automated legal document review system is not responsible for law firm layoffs. But they are part of a chain of decisions. Pretending the chain does not exist is its own kind of choice.

03 / 05 -- Healthcare IT: Promise and Ethical Hazards

Information technology in healthcare has produced measurable improvements: electronic health records reduce medication errors, telemedicine expands access in underserved areas, AI-assisted diagnostics identify patterns humans miss. The ethical complexity lies in where these benefits are distributed, how patient data is used, and what happens when automated systems fail.

EHR Benefits

Electronic Health Records reduce duplicated testing, medication errors, and improve care coordination across providers. Patient outcomes improve in organizations with mature EHR implementations.

EHR Risks

EHR systems are high-value targets for ransomware. Breaches expose some of the most sensitive personal information that exists. Interoperability failures fragment care across systems.

Algorithmic Bias

ML diagnostic tools trained on non-representative data produce biased outputs. Studies have shown AI diagnostic systems perform significantly worse on dark-skinned patients when trained primarily on light-skinned patient data.

Data Monetization

De-identified patient data is commercially valuable. Patients who contributed their data to research datasets often have no visibility into how that data generates profit for health technology companies.

The same dataset that trains a life-saving diagnostic AI may also be sold to an insurance company to identify high-risk policyholders. The patient signed a consent form that did not describe either use.

04 / 05 -- The Digital Divide

The digital divide refers to the gap between individuals and communities who have access to information and communication technology and those who do not. The divide operates along multiple axes simultaneously: economic, geographic, generational, and educational.

When government services, employment applications, healthcare scheduling, and financial products move online, the digital divide becomes a divide in access to essential services. Citizens without broadband access, devices, or digital literacy are not merely inconvenienced -- they are structurally excluded from participation in modern civic and economic life.

The ethical implications for IT professionals are real. Systems designed without accessibility in mind, products that assume constant connectivity, and applications that require high-end hardware all exacerbate the divide. Inclusive design is not charitable -- it is a professional obligation under codes like ACM and IEEE, which require IT professionals to contribute to the public good.

The app that works beautifully on a 1Gbps fiber connection and a current-generation smartphone is not a neutral product. It is a product that serves some people and excludes others. That choice was made by the people who designed it.

05 / 05 -- Case Study: IBM Watson for Oncology

IBM Watson for Oncology was marketed as an AI system that could review a patient's medical records and suggest evidence-based cancer treatment options. IBM positioned it as a breakthrough in AI-assisted oncology. By 2017, it had been sold to dozens of hospitals globally, including major cancer centers.

Case Facts

What was claimed: Watson for Oncology analyzed patient data against medical literature to recommend treatments concordant with expert oncologist guidelines.

What was discovered: The system was not trained on real patient data at scale. It was trained primarily on a small set of hypothetical patient scenarios developed by physicians at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center. Its recommendations reflected the practice patterns of those physicians, not broader clinical evidence.

What happened: Internal documents leaked to Stat News in 2018 showed that Watson had recommended "unsafe and incorrect" cancer treatments. Multiple hospital customers discontinued the product. IBM quietly withdrew it from oncology markets by 2022.

Ethical dimension: Hospitals paid millions for a product whose underlying training methodology was not disclosed. Physicians who deferred to Watson's recommendations may have provided suboptimal care. Patients who received those recommendations were not informed that an AI trained on hypothetical cases had influenced their treatment plan.

Analysis Questions

IBM disclosed that Watson was trained by oncologists. It did not prominently disclose that the training data was primarily hypothetical rather than derived from real patient outcomes. Is that omission an ethical failure or acceptable product marketing?

A hospital CIO who purchased Watson did so based on IBM's marketing. The physicians who used it believed the training methodology was more robust than it was. Who bears ethical responsibility for the treatments recommended?

Apply the Reynolds ethical decision-making process: identify the stakeholders, the options available at each decision point, and the framework that best captures the ethical failures in this case.

Marketing an AI as "evidence-based" when it was trained on hypothetical scenarios is not a technical failure. It is a communication decision made by people who knew what the training data actually was.
