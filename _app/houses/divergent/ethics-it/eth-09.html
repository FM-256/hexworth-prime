01 / 05 -- Software Quality and the "Good Enough" Problem

Software quality refers to the degree to which a product meets its specified requirements and satisfies user needs. In practice, software is never perfect -- every system shipped contains defects. The ethical question is not whether defects exist, but how many are acceptable given the stakes involved.

Reynolds frames this as the "how good is good enough" problem. The answer depends on context. A spelling error in a word processor is a minor defect. A rounding error in medical device dosage software can kill a patient. The same standard cannot apply to both situations, yet commercial pressure often treats them the same.

Quality Dimension Description Ethical Significance
Functionality Does the software do what it claims? False advertising if it does not; breach of contract if specified in writing
Reliability Does it work consistently over time? Unreliable systems in critical infrastructure create foreseeable harm
Safety Does failure harm users or bystanders? The highest ethical bar -- safety failures can be life-threatening
Maintainability Can defects be found and corrected? Technical debt deferred indefinitely becomes an ethical liability

The software industry has long resisted the product liability standards applied to physical goods. Shrink-wrap and click-through license agreements routinely disclaim all warranties. This legal shield does not eliminate the ethical obligation to ship software that does not cause foreseeable harm.

When the specification says "best effort" and the deployment context is a hospital, the specification is the ethical problem -- not just a legal one.

02 / 05 -- Safety-Critical Systems

A safety-critical system is one where failure can result in loss of life, serious injury, or significant environmental damage. Software in these contexts operates under fundamentally different ethical obligations than commercial consumer applications.

Examples include medical devices, avionics, nuclear plant control systems, air traffic management, and increasingly autonomous vehicles. In each domain, engineering standards are more rigorous, testing requirements more extensive, and the ethical stakes of cutting corners dramatically higher.

Historical Precedent: Therac-25

What happened: Between 1985 and 1987, the Therac-25 radiation therapy machine delivered massive overdoses to at least six patients due to a race condition in the control software. Three patients died. Three were severely injured.

Root cause: The software reused code from an earlier model (Therac-20) but removed hardware safety interlocks that the previous version relied on. The software was not adequately tested for the new configuration. Engineers dismissed early error reports as user error.

Ethical failure: The development team prioritized schedule over verification. Error codes were cryptic. Incidents were investigated slowly. When patterns emerged, action was delayed.

Lesson: Removing hardware safety interlocks and relying on software alone -- without proportionate verification -- is an ethical failure regardless of whether it is legal. The engineers did not intend to harm anyone. The harm was foreseeable from the design decisions they made.

The Therac-25 case remains the defining reference for software ethics in safety-critical systems. Every IT professional working in any domain where software touches human health or safety should know this case.

The engineers who wrote the Therac-25 code were not malicious. They were rushed, they cut corners they thought were safe to cut, and they dismissed warning signs. That pattern repeats in every safety-critical failure.

03 / 05 -- Product Liability and Development Methodology Ethics

Product liability refers to the legal responsibility of manufacturers and sellers for defects that cause harm. Software has historically been treated as a service rather than a product, limiting liability exposure. This legal distinction does not resolve the ethical question of who bears responsibility when software causes harm.

Waterfall Ethics

Sequential phases with defined requirements upfront. Ethics benefit: clear documentation of decisions and sign-offs. Ethics risk: late testing means defects are found after significant investment, creating pressure to ship anyway.

Agile Ethics

Iterative development with continuous delivery. Ethics benefit: faster feedback loops, easier course correction. Ethics risk: "move fast" culture can deprioritize security and safety in favor of feature velocity, allowing technical debt to accumulate.

Negligence Standard

A developer may be held liable if they had a duty of care, the duty was breached, and that breach caused harm. In safety-critical software, duty of care is well-established. In consumer software, it is emerging through litigation and regulation.

Strict Liability

Some legal theories hold manufacturers liable regardless of fault -- if the product is defective and causes harm, liability attaches. Software is moving toward this standard in safety-critical contexts. The EU's AI Liability Directive is a leading example.

The methodology is not the ethics. The ethics is what the team does when the methodology gives them permission to cut corners they know they should not cut.

04 / 05 -- Case Study: Autonomous Vehicle Software Ethics

Self-driving vehicles represent one of the most complex ethical challenges in software development. They require software to make decisions -- in milliseconds, at highway speed -- that have life-or-death consequences for occupants and bystanders. These systems embed ethical decisions into algorithms before any incident occurs.

Case: Uber ATG -- Tempe, Arizona (March 2018)

What happened: An Uber autonomous test vehicle struck and killed Elaine Herzberg, a pedestrian crossing outside a marked crosswalk. A safety driver was present but distracted. The vehicle's software detected the pedestrian 6 seconds before impact but failed to take corrective action.

Software factor: The system was designed to suppress false-positive detections to reduce unnecessary braking. This suppression prevented it from acting on a true positive. The safety driver alert system had been disabled.

Ethical dimension: The decision to suppress detections was made by engineers to improve ride comfort in testing -- optimizing for one metric while degrading safety. That decision was made before anyone died. The harm was foreseeable from the engineering tradeoff.

Ethical Question Who Decides? Implication
Occupant vs. bystander priority Software engineers, product managers The programmer encodes a moral position -- not a technical one
Acceptable false-positive rate Engineering teams, safety committees The tradeoff between comfort and safety is a value judgment
When to deploy at scale Company leadership, regulators Public roads are a live test environment -- users did not consent
What Would You Do?

You are a software engineer on an autonomous vehicle team. Your detection system has a 2% false-positive rate causing phantom braking. Your manager asks you to implement detection suppression to reduce complaints from test drivers. You know suppression also reduces true-positive response rates.

Do you implement it? Do you document the safety tradeoff? Do you escalate to a safety committee? What happens to your position if you refuse or slow the timeline?

The Uber case is the documented answer to what happens when engineers implement suppression without adequate safety process.

Every algorithm that touches the physical world encodes a value system. The engineer who writes it is making ethical choices whether they frame them that way or not.

05 / 05 -- Professional Responsibility and the Developer's Obligation

The ACM Code of Ethics and the Software Engineering Code of Ethics both address the developer's obligation to the public. The core principle: software engineers shall act consistently with the public interest. When professional judgment conflicts with organizational pressure, the public interest obligation takes precedence.

In practice, this obligation surfaces in several ways. A developer who discovers a security vulnerability and is told to defer patching has a professional responsibility to document the decision and escalate if the risk is significant. A developer asked to disable safety checks to meet a deadline has an obligation to refuse or formally record the override.

The phrase "I was just following orders" has no standing in professional ethics. The engineer who builds the defeat device, the developer who implements surveillance software, and the programmer who writes the suppression logic all made a choice. Employment does not eliminate individual professional responsibility.

The code you write is your signature. Years later, when the system fails, no one will remember who gave the order. They will ask who wrote the function.
