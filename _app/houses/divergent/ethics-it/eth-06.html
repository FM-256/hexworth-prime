First Amendment Scope and Limits

The First Amendment prohibits the government from abridging freedom of speech, press, assembly, and petition. It does not apply to private actors. A social media company that removes content is not violating the First Amendment. A university that is a public institution may have different obligations than a private one.

Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act

Section 230 (1996) provides: "No provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider." This provision gives platforms two distinct protections.

No Publisher Liability Platforms are not liable for content posted by users. Without this protection, platforms could not host user-generated content at scale -- the liability exposure would be crushing.
Good Samaritan Moderation Platforms may moderate content in "good faith" without becoming publishers. Section 230 protects the moderation decision itself, allowing platforms to remove content without triggering editorial liability.
The Ethical Challenge Algorithmic amplification of content may constitute editorial judgment. Critics argue that platforms with billions of users and amplification systems are qualitatively different from the 1996 bulletin boards Section 230 was designed for.
Exceptions Section 230 does not protect platforms from liability for FOSTA-SESTA (sex trafficking facilitation), federal criminal liability, or intellectual property claims under the DMCA.

Defamation, Online Anonymity, and DMCA Abuse

Defamation requires a false statement of fact (not opinion), published to a third party, that causes damage to the subject's reputation. Online defamation follows the same standard with jurisdiction complications.

DMCA Takedown Abuse Pattern

The DMCA provides a "safe harbor" for platforms that respond promptly to takedown notices. Platforms that receive a qualifying notice must remove the content or lose safe harbor protection. This creates a known abuse vector: parties who want to suppress criticism, satire, or unfavorable reporting can file DMCA notices against content they do not actually own copyright in, using the takedown process as a censorship tool. The counter-notice process restores the content, but the chilling effect on smaller creators can be significant before restoration occurs.

Online anonymity is protected under a line of Supreme Court precedent as an aspect of First Amendment protection for political speech. Courts have applied balancing tests to subpoenas seeking to unmask anonymous speakers, weighing the strength of the plaintiff's claim against the speaker's First Amendment interest in anonymity.

Case Study: Reno v. ACLU (1997)

Case Facts: Reno v. ACLU

The Communications Decency Act of 1996 included provisions (beyond Section 230) that criminalized the knowing transmission of "obscene or indecent" material to minors online. The ACLU challenged the provisions as unconstitutional restrictions on free speech. The Supreme Court struck down the indecency provisions 9-0, ruling that the Internet deserved the highest First Amendment protection -- equivalent to print media rather than the more limited protection accorded to broadcast media.

The Court's reasoning was that unlike broadcast media, the Internet was not "invasive" -- users had to actively seek content rather than having it thrust upon them by limited spectrum allocation. The Court also rejected the "captive audience" argument and found that the provisions were not the least restrictive means of protecting minors.

Warehouse Lore

Every content moderation decision is an editorial decision. The argument that platforms are "neutral infrastructure" is inconsistent with the fact that platforms design the algorithms that determine what content users see, in what order, and how widely it is amplified. Neutrality was never the offer. The question is who controls the editorial judgment and whether that control is accountable.
