Morality, Ethics, and Law Defined

ConceptDefinitionEnforcement MechanismScope
MoralityA personal set of beliefs about right and wrong derived from upbringing, culture, and experienceInternal -- self-enforced through conscienceIndividual
EthicsA code of behavior defined by the group to which an individual belongs (profession, organization, community)Social and professional consequence -- peer pressure, reputation, employmentGroup
LawFormal rules established by government authority, codifying the minimum behavioral standards society requiresLegal system -- fines, injunctions, incarcerationSociety
Key Distinction

Something can be legal but unethical (selling user data without meaningful disclosure), ethical but illegal (rare edge cases in whistleblowing), or any combination. The law is not an ethical ceiling. Professional codes often demand more than the law requires.

Four Major Ethical Frameworks for IT

Stockholder Theory Milton Friedman: a company's primary obligation is to maximize returns for shareholders within legal rules. Ethical constraints = legal constraints only.
Stakeholder Theory R. Edward Freeman: a company has obligations to all parties affected by its decisions -- employees, customers, suppliers, communities -- not only shareholders.
Utilitarian Choose actions that produce the greatest good for the greatest number. Focus on outcomes. The majority benefit can be weighed against minority harm.
Deontological Some acts are inherently right or wrong regardless of consequences. Follow universal duties. (Kant: act only as you would will everyone to act.)

The Ethical Decision-Making Process

StepActionKey Question
1. RecognizeIdentify that an ethical issue existsDoes this decision harm someone, violate rights, or conflict with a professional obligation?
2. FactsGather all relevant informationWho is affected? What are the realistic options? What is known and unknown?
3. OptionsIdentify possible courses of actionWhat does each ethical framework say about each option?
4. DecisionChoose the most defensible optionWhich option survives scrutiny under the most frameworks? Which obligations are non-negotiable?
5. ReflectEvaluate outcomes and update judgmentWas the decision right? What would you change? How does this inform future decisions?

Case Study: Volkswagen Emissions Scandal

Case Facts: VW Emissions Defeat Device (2009-2015)

Volkswagen engineers programmed diesel engine control units to detect when the vehicle was undergoing emissions testing and activate full emissions controls during the test only. During normal driving, the controls were disabled, and engines emitted NOx at levels up to 40x the legal limit. The defeat device was installed in approximately 11 million vehicles globally.

The decision to implement the defeat device was not made by one rogue engineer. It was the product of incremental institutional decisions made over years by engineers, managers, and executives who prioritized regulatory compliance performance over actual emissions reduction.

FrameworkAnalysis of the VW Decision
StockholderDefeat device enabled competitive positioning and market share growth -- aligned with shareholder interest until exposure caused a $30B+ loss in market capitalization.
StakeholderViolated obligations to customers (deceived about product they purchased), communities (health effects of NOx), regulators (deliberate evasion), and employees (career consequence of institutional fraud).
UtilitarianShort-term market gain does not outweigh health harm to millions of people exposed to elevated NOx emissions over years plus the systemic harm to regulatory integrity.
DeontologicalDeliberate deception of regulators and customers is wrong regardless of outcome. The defeat device was a lie encoded in software. No outcome calculation changes the duty violation.
What Would You Do?

You are a software engineer at VW in 2009. Your manager assigns you to implement a feature in the engine control unit that behaves differently during test cycles than during normal operation. You are not explicitly told why. You are told it is a "calibration optimization." What professional obligation applies at this point, before you know the full picture?

Developing a Personal Code of Ethics

Professional codes define minimum group standards. An IT professional's personal code integrates those standards with examined personal values and an understanding of the five professional relationships: employer, client, supplier, user, and society.

The transparency test is one practical heuristic: if you would not be comfortable having your decision reported on the front page of a national newspaper, the decision requires further ethical analysis before proceeding.

Warehouse Lore

The engineers who maintained the VW defeat device for six years were not monsters. They were professionals who made incremental compromises in an organizational culture that normalized those compromises. A personal code of ethics is the thing that breaks that incremental drift before it reaches the point where the decision has already been made.
