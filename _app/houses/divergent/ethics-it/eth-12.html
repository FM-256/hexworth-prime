01 / 05 -- Social Networks: Personal Use, Business Use, and the Line Between

Social networking platforms serve multiple simultaneous functions: personal communication, professional networking, brand building, customer service, recruitment, advertising, and political organizing. This multiplicity creates ethical complexity because norms that apply in one context are routinely violated in others.

Organizations use social media for legitimate business purposes: customer engagement, brand awareness, market research, and crisis communication. The ethical issues arise at the edges: monitoring employee social media activity, using social content in hiring decisions, targeting vulnerable populations with advertising, and the extraction of behavioral data that users did not knowingly provide.

Use Category Common Practice Ethical Issue
Recruitment screening Reviewing candidate social media before making hiring decisions Access to protected class information (age, religion, race) that cannot legally influence hiring but inevitably does
Employee monitoring Tracking employee social media activity during and outside work hours Conflicts with privacy expectations; chills protected speech; may violate NLRA
Targeted advertising Using behavioral data to serve ads to specific demographic or psychological profiles Exploits vulnerable populations; uses data provided in one context for another
Social commerce Influencer marketing, embedded purchase flows, algorithmic product recommendations Blurs disclosure lines between editorial content and paid promotion

When a hiring manager views a candidate's Instagram before the interview, they have seen the candidate's religion, age, race, family status, and political views. The law says this cannot influence the decision. The brain does not have an off switch.

02 / 05 -- CDA Section 230: Platform Immunity and Its Limits

Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act (1996) provides that "No provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider." In plain terms: platforms are not liable for content posted by their users.

Section 230 immunity is the legal foundation on which every major social platform operates. Without it, platforms could not moderate content (because moderation implies editorial judgment, which implies publisher status) or allow user-generated content at scale. The provision was designed to allow the early internet to develop without crushing liability.

The ethical debate centers on whether the immunity appropriate for an emerging medium is still appropriate for platforms with billions of users and algorithmic amplification systems that actively promote content. Critics argue that algorithmic curation is itself editorial judgment, which should carry liability. Defenders argue that removing immunity would cause platforms to either over-moderate or under-moderate -- neither outcome being desirable.

Section 230 does not prevent platforms from moderating content. It prevents them from being sued for moderating. The decision about what stays and what gets removed is an ethical choice made by product managers who are insulated from the consequences.

03 / 05 -- Fake News, Misinformation, and Platform Responsibility

Misinformation refers to false information spread without deliberate intent to deceive. Disinformation is false information spread with the intent to deceive. The distinction matters legally and ethically: platform liability differs based on whether the platform knowingly amplifies disinformation campaigns.

Algorithmic amplification of false content is not a side effect. Engagement algorithms optimize for interaction, and false or emotionally provocative content generates more interaction than accurate content. The algorithm has no knowledge of truth -- it amplifies what gets clicked, shared, and commented on. When false content generates more engagement than accurate content, the algorithm amplifies the false content by design.

IT professionals who design engagement algorithms are making ethical decisions. The choice to optimize for raw engagement rather than engagement with accurate content is a value choice. The argument that "the algorithm is neutral" is technically illiterate and ethically evasive. Algorithms embed the values of whoever designed the objective function.

The engineer who set the engagement optimization target knew, or should have known, that high-engagement content skews toward outrage. The choice to ship that objective function anyway was an ethical decision. It just was not framed as one.

04 / 05 -- Case Study: Cambridge Analytica and Data Harvesting

The Cambridge Analytica case is the defining reference for social media data ethics. It illustrates what happens when platform data access policies, academic research exemptions, and political data operations intersect without adequate ethical oversight.

Case Facts

The mechanism: Cambridge Analytica obtained Facebook profile data on approximately 87 million users through a third-party quiz app (thisisyourdigitallife). Facebook's API at the time allowed apps to collect data not only on users who installed them but on those users' entire friend networks -- without the friends' knowledge or consent.

The use: The harvested data was used to build psychographic profiles of voters for use in targeted political advertising. The data was used in the 2016 US presidential election and the UK Brexit referendum, among others.

The violation: Facebook's terms of service prohibited selling data to third parties. Cambridge Analytica violated those terms. Facebook knew data had been improperly obtained and did not inform users for over two years.

The consequence: Facebook paid a $5 billion FTC fine -- the largest in FTC history at the time. Cambridge Analytica dissolved. Multiple investigations were launched globally. The case catalyzed GDPR enforcement and accelerated US privacy legislation debates.

Ethical Analysis

Facebook's API design that allowed friend-network data harvesting was not an oversight. It was a design choice that enabled a robust third-party app ecosystem, which increased platform engagement. The ethical failure was not building the API -- it was not auditing how it was being used and not disclosing the breach when it was discovered.

Cambridge Analytica's psychographic targeting of voters raises a deeper question: is it ethical to use behavioral data to manipulate political preferences? The law does not clearly prohibit it. Professional ethics codes that require respect for privacy and the public interest clearly implicate it.

Cambridge Analytica did not steal data. It used data Facebook provided through an API Facebook built. The question of who bears ethical responsibility when a platform is weaponized is still being litigated in every jurisdiction that matters.

05 / 05 -- IT Professional Obligations in Social Media Contexts

IT professionals who build or maintain social media systems -- whether at large platforms or organizations that use social data -- carry specific ethical obligations that the platform's legal team cannot discharge on their behalf.

Those obligations include: designing for contextual integrity (data should only flow in ways consistent with the context in which it was shared), not building systems that enable discrimination in hiring or credit based on social media proxies, and flagging when data collection practices exceed what users have meaningfully consented to.

The ACM Code of Ethics requires members to "respect the privacy of others" and "avoid harm." A developer who builds a system that harvests friend-network data without consent is not insulated from those obligations by the fact that their employer's legal team reviewed the terms of service.

The fact that the platform's privacy policy permits something does not mean it is ethical. Privacy policies are written by lawyers to minimize liability. They are not ethical frameworks.
