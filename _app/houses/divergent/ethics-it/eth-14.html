"Four codes. Four perspectives. Same profession. Which one governs you?"

01 / 05 -- Why Professional Codes of Ethics Exist

A professional code of ethics is a formal statement of the values and principles that a professional body expects its members to uphold. Codes exist because professionals possess specialized knowledge and capabilities that give them power over people who lack that knowledge. That asymmetry creates an obligation: professionals must use their expertise in ways that serve the people they work with, not exploit them.

Medicine, law, and engineering developed codes of ethics because they recognized that professional autonomy requires professional accountability. IT is a younger profession and its codes are less mature and less uniformly enforced. But the underlying logic is the same: IT professionals have access to systems, data, and capabilities that most of their clients and employers do not fully understand. That access must be governed by something beyond personal preference.

Codes do not replace judgment. They provide a structure for exercising judgment. When you face a situation the code does not directly address, the code's principles tell you which direction to walk. When following your employer's instructions conflicts with the code, the code tells you which obligation takes precedence.

A code of ethics that an organization adopts but does not enforce is decorative. The question is not whether your company has a code. The question is what happened to the last person who actually used it.

02 / 05 -- The Four Codes: Scope and Emphasis

Four professional organizations publish widely referenced codes applicable to IT practitioners. They share core values but differ in scope, emphasis, and enforcement mechanism.

ACM Code of Ethics
Association for Computing Machinery (1992, revised 2018)

Broadest scope. Covers all computing practitioners. Strong emphasis on public welfare, privacy, and anti-discrimination. Principle 1.1: "Contribute to society and to human well-being."

Enforcement: ACM can revoke membership. Rarely enforced against individuals directly. More commonly used as a reference standard in litigation and policy.

IEEE Code of Ethics
Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (1979, revised 2020)

Engineering-focused. Requires members to "hold paramount the safety, health, and welfare of the public." Explicit obligation to disclose conflicts of interest. Most directly applicable to hardware and systems engineers.

Enforcement: IEEE can take membership action. Structured as 10 commitments members agree to upon joining.

PMI Code of Ethics
Project Management Institute (2006)

Project management focus. Organized around four values: Responsibility, Respect, Fairness, and Honesty. Each value has aspirational and mandatory standards. IT project managers are governed by this code if they hold PMI certifications (PMP, CAPM).

Enforcement: PMI ethics complaints can result in suspension or revocation of certification. More active enforcement mechanism than ACM or IEEE in practice.

AITP Code of Ethics
Association of Information Technology Professionals

IT management and practitioner focus. Addresses obligations to management, colleagues, the profession, and society. Emphasizes professional development and knowledge-sharing obligations. Directly relevant to IT management roles covered in Reynolds.

Enforcement: AITP membership-based. Code compliance is primarily self-enforced.

If you are a software developer with a PMP managing your project, you are simultaneously subject to ACM and PMI codes. If those codes give different answers to the same question, you have to decide which obligation governs. Neither organization will make that decision for you.

03 / 05 -- Where the Codes Agree

Despite their different scopes and emphases, the four major codes converge on several non-negotiable principles that constitute the baseline of IT professional ethics.

Principle ACM IEEE PMI AITP
Public welfare first Explicit (1.1) Explicit ("hold paramount") Implicit (responsibility value) Explicit
Honesty and transparency Explicit (1.3, 2.1) Explicit (conflict of interest) Explicit (honesty value) Explicit
Competence obligation Explicit (2.2) Explicit Explicit (responsibility value) Explicit
Privacy protection Explicit (1.6) Implicit Implicit Explicit
No discrimination Explicit (1.4) Explicit Explicit (fairness value) Explicit

The universal agreement on public welfare primacy is significant. All four codes establish that when professional obligations to employers or clients conflict with obligations to the public, the public interest takes precedence. This is not aspirational -- it is the stated structural priority in each code. Professionals who say they followed employer instructions and therefore had no choice are not applying the codes correctly.

The one thing all four codes agree on is the one thing most professionals invoke last: the public. Your employer pays you. The public is who the code was written for.

04 / 05 -- Where the Codes Conflict and the SE Code

The codes do not always give the same answer to the same question. The most significant areas of practical tension are enforcement, scope, and the treatment of employment conflict.

The Software Engineering Code of Ethics and Professional Practice (SE Code, ACM/IEEE joint publication, 1999) provides the most detailed guidance for software developers. Its eight principles address: Public, Client, Product, Judgment, Management, Profession, Colleagues, and Self. The SE Code is notable for its Management principle: software engineering managers shall not ask their engineers to do anything that would be unethical under the code, and engineers shall refuse unethical instructions.

Applied Conflict Analysis

Scenario: You are a PMP-certified IT project manager. Your client instructs you to misrepresent project progress in a status report to their executive team. Your employer wants you to comply to protect the client relationship.

PMI Code (Honesty value, mandatory standards): "We do not engage in or condone behavior that is designed to deceive others." Mandatory: you cannot comply.

ACM Code (Principle 1.3): "Be honest and trustworthy." Mandatory: you cannot comply.

IEEE Code: "be honest and realistic in stating claims or estimates." You cannot comply.

All three codes give the same answer. But only PMI has an active enforcement mechanism that could result in loss of your PMP certification if a complaint is filed. The obligation is universal; the consequence for non-compliance depends on which organization governs you.

The codes were not written by people who assumed professionals would always be comfortable using them. They were written with the expectation that using them would sometimes cost you something. That cost is what makes them meaningful.

05 / 05 -- How to Apply a Professional Code to a Real Situation

Professional codes are not lookup tables. You cannot search for "data breach" and find the answer. They are frameworks that structure your reasoning. Reynolds provides a method for applying codes to real situations that integrates the ethical decision-making process from Chapter 1 with the specific obligations in professional codes.

Step Question Code Application
1. Identify What is the ethical issue? Which domain of the code applies: public welfare, honesty, privacy, competence?
2. Stakeholders Who is affected? Which code obligations run to each stakeholder: employer, client, colleague, public?
3. Options What can be done? Which options does the code prohibit? Which does it affirmatively require?
4. Priority When obligations conflict, which governs? All four codes prioritize public welfare over employer/client obligations when they conflict.
5. Decide and document What is the defensible choice? Could you defend your decision before the ethics committee of the relevant professional body?

The final test is the one that matters in practice: if a complaint were filed against you with ACM, IEEE, or PMI, could you explain your decision in terms of the code? If yes, you have done the job. If not, you have work to do before you act.

Most professionals will never face a formal ethics complaint. But the value of the codes is not in their enforcement. It is in the habit of asking the question before you act, rather than after you are caught.
