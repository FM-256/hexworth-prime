Professional versus Worker: The Ethical Distinction

DimensionIT WorkerIT Professional
Primary obligationEmployer -- follow instructions, deliver assigned workMultiple parties -- employer, client, profession, public, society
Knowledge standardAdequate for assigned tasksOngoing competence obligation -- must maintain current knowledge
Ethics governanceEmployer policy and applicable lawProfessional codes (ACM, IEEE, PMI, AITP) in addition to law and policy
Conflict resolutionFollow employer directionProfessional codes take precedence when employer direction violates them
AutonomyLimited -- defined by job descriptionGreater -- with autonomy comes corresponding responsibility
Why It Matters

IT workers who are not professionals in the ethical sense bear less individual responsibility for the consequences of their work. IT professionals -- those who have accepted the professional role and its codes -- carry obligations that extend beyond completing assigned tasks.

Five Professional Relationships

Employer Primary practical relationship. Follow reasonable instructions, protect confidential information, avoid conflicts of interest, provide honest assessments of work status and risk.
Client The party the professional serves. Interests may differ from employer's interests. When they conflict, professional codes generally require the professional to be honest with both.
Supplier Third parties who provide components, services, or tools. The IT professional has an obligation to select suppliers ethically and not accept gifts or incentives that compromise judgment.
User The people who use the systems IT professionals build. Users typically have the least power in the relationship and the most exposure to harm if the system fails or behaves deceptively.
Society The broadest obligation. IT systems affect people who never chose to interact with them. Society comes last in daily priority but first in ethical priority when it conflicts with other relationships.

Professional Codes: ACM, IEEE, AITP, PMI

OrganizationCodeKey EmphasisEnforcement
ACMCode of Ethics and Professional Conduct (2018)Broadest scope: public welfare, privacy, anti-discrimination, avoiding harmMembership revocation -- rarely enforced against individuals

Compliance Laws That Govern IT Professionals

LawDomainIT Implication
SOX (Sarbanes-Oxley, 2002)Financial reporting at public companiesIT systems that support financial reporting must be auditable; IT professionals involved in financial fraud face criminal liability
HIPAA (1996)Healthcare data privacy and securityIT professionals at covered entities must implement required safeguards; violations can result in civil and criminal penalties
FCPA (Foreign Corrupt Practices Act)Bribery of foreign government officialsIT professionals who enable or conceal bribery through systems (accounting, approvals) can be personally liable
CFAA (Computer Fraud and Abuse Act)Unauthorized computer accessGoverns what IT professionals can and cannot do with systems -- including systems they administer for others
Compliance Is Not Ethics

Compliance with applicable law is the minimum standard. Professional ethics codes generally require more. An IT professional who has done everything the law requires has satisfied the legal standard. They have not necessarily satisfied their professional ethical obligations.

Case Study: SAP and the FCPA Violation

Case Facts: SAP SE FCPA Violation

SAP SE, the German enterprise software company, paid $220 million in 2021 to resolve FCPA charges. SAP's subsidiaries had paid bribes to government officials in South Africa and several other countries to secure government software contracts. The payment mechanism involved creating fake entities and falsely characterizing the payments in accounting systems.

IT professionals were directly involved in maintaining the systems that processed, classified, and concealed the payments. Some were aware of the nature of the transactions; others implemented systems without understanding what they were being used for.

RoleWhat They DidEthical Obligation They Failed
IT architects designing the payment systemBuilt systems that could process payments to shell entities without adequate controls or audit trailsSystems should be designed with controls that make fraud difficult, not easy
IT staff processing transactionsProcessed payments classified in ways they may have known were inaccurateObligation not to falsify records; obligation to refuse instructions that require falsification
Compliance IT staffMaintained reporting systems that did not flag the patternObligation to ensure compliance systems function effectively, not as theater
What Would You Do?

You are an IT professional at SAP maintaining the expense and payment approval system. You notice that a significant number of payments to a particular vendor in South Africa are being approved using an expedited override code that bypasses normal documentation requirements. The payments are coming from a business unit manager you have never interacted with. What professional obligation applies?
