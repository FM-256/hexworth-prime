Slide 3 of 13 · The field
What Cyberethics Actually Is
A branch of applied ethics. Not a separate moral system — the application of moral reasoning to the distinct conditions of networked technology.
Descriptive
What do people in fact believe is right or wrong about technology use? Surveys, studies, observation.
Normative
What should people believe and do? The argued position. Most professional ethics work lives here.
Comparative
How do different cultures, professions, or eras answer the same question differently? Where do the answers diverge and why?
Why the distinction matters in security: "everyone in our company writes their password down" is descriptive. "No one should write their password down" is normative. "European admins use password managers more than US admins" is comparative. All three help you understand a behavior; only the second tells you what to do.
Slide 4 of 13 · The course's central frame
Lessig's Four Modalities of Regulation
From Lawrence Lessig, Code and Other Laws of Cyberspace (1999); rewritten as Code: Version 2.0 (2006). The single most-cited model in cyberethics.
Law
What the state will do to you if you do X. Statutes, regulations, court orders.
Norms
What other people will do to you if you do X. Social pressure, reputation, professional codes.
Market
What X will cost you. Price, scarcity, the economics of a choice.
Architecture (Code)
What X will let you do. The physical or digital constraints of the environment itself.
The insight: all four constrain behavior. In physical space, law and norms dominate. In cyberspace, code dominates — and code is written by engineers. That makes engineering decisions ethical decisions whether anyone framed them that way or not.
Slide 5 of 13 · The famous claim
"Code is Law"
The slogan everyone quotes. What it actually means.
Lessig's claim
In cyberspace, the architecture of the system regulates conduct as effectively as — often more effectively than — statutes do. The DRM that prevents copying is more binding than the copyright law that forbids it.
The implication
Whoever writes the code makes the rules. Engineers who claim "we just build the platform; we don't decide policy" are claiming a neutrality that does not exist.
A platform that requires real names regulates speech more decisively than any government decree against anonymous posting. The product manager who chose the policy made a constitutional decision. The engineer who implemented the check enforced it.
The security-professional version: when you implement an access control list, you are writing law for that system. The choice of which roles get which permissions is not a technical decision — it is a power-distribution decision wearing a technical costume.
Slide 6 of 13 · The toolkit
Four Ethical Frameworks (Quick Reference)
You will see all four invoked in cybersecurity arguments. Knowing the names lets you see what is actually being assumed.
Utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill)
The right act maximizes total well-being. Ask: "What produces the greatest good for the greatest number?" — The framework most often invoked in "the breach harmed fewer people than the lawsuit will" arguments.
Deontology (Kant)
The right act respects duty and the dignity of persons regardless of consequences. Ask: "Could I will this rule to apply to everyone?" — The framework behind "you cannot use a person as a means to an end" objections to user-tracking.
Virtue ethics (Aristotle)
The right act is what a person of good character would do. Ask: "What would the security professional I want to be do here?" — The framework behind professional-code reasoning.
Contractualism (Rawls, Scanlon)
The right act is one no one could reasonably reject. Ask: "Would the people affected agree to this if they were behind a veil of ignorance about which role they'd play?" — The framework behind fairness arguments in algorithmic design.
Slide 7 of 13 · The hard case
When the Frameworks Disagree
Most real ethical decisions live where two frameworks recommend different actions.
A security researcher discovers a vulnerability that, if disclosed, will let users protect themselves but will also let attackers exploit it before the patch ships. Disclose now, disclose after the patch, or never?
FrameworkRecommendsReasoning
UtilitarianCoordinated disclosure (after patch)Greatest total well-being if attackers don't get a head start
DeontologicalTension — competing dutiesDuty to inform users (right to know) vs. duty to not enable foreseeable harm to third parties (Kant's "do not will" test). Reasonable Kantians land on either side.
Virtue ethicsCoordinated disclosure with timelineWhat the responsible professional does — honesty plus prudence
ContractualismCoordinated disclosureBehind the veil of ignorance, no one would sign up for "vulnerabilities are dropped without warning"
The takeaway: three of four frameworks point toward coordinated disclosure with a deadline (the industry's de facto norm). Deontology splits internally because it has multiple duties in tension. The convergence-with-internal-disagreement pattern is what most professional ethics decisions actually look like.
Slide 8 of 13 · Reference case
Sony BMG Rootkit (2005)
A canonical Spinello case — what happens when a corporation enforces copyright through code.
What happened
Sony BMG shipped CDs containing DRM software (XCP and MediaMax) that silently installed a rootkit when the disc was played on a Windows PC. The rootkit hid files, weakened system security, and was difficult to remove. Researcher Mark Russinovich discovered and publicized it on October 31, 2005.
The fallout
XCP-affected discs were estimated in the millions across roughly 50 titles. Sony issued a recall. Class-action lawsuits, FTC consent order (Jan 2007), and settlements with attorneys general in dozens of states. The rootkit's cloaking became a vector exploited by malware authors as a hiding mechanism.
The Lessig analysis: Sony chose code to enforce law (copyright). The architecture overrode user consent in pursuit of a legitimate legal interest. The framework collapse: utilitarian harm to ~4.7M users + downstream malware victims dwarfed any utilitarian gain. Deontology: users were used as means to Sony's anti-piracy ends without their meaningful consent.
The engineer who wrote the rootkit cloaking code may have believed they were "just implementing the spec." Spinello's framework says: every line of that code was an ethical act.
Slide 9 of 13 · Reference case
Apple v. FBI (2016)
Encryption as the front line of "code is law" — and the limits of legal compulsion.
The setup
San Bernardino shooting (Dec 2015). FBI recovered an iPhone 5C used by one of the attackers. Could not unlock it — iOS would erase data after 10 wrong PIN attempts. FBI sought a court order under the All Writs Act of 1789 compelling Apple to write firmware that would disable the auto-erase.
Apple's refusal
Apple publicly refused. Argued the request would force them to write a tool that, once it existed, could be used to weaken every iPhone. Tim Cook published an open letter framing it as a precedent issue, not a single-case issue.
How it ended: the FBI withdrew the request after an unidentified third-party vendor provided an unlock method (later attributed by reporting to Azimuth Security, never confirmed by the parties). The legal question was never resolved. The ethical question continues.
All four modalities collided: law (court order), code (the encryption that made the order necessary), norms (public sentiment split sharply), and market (Apple's brand was built on user trust). The case is taught precisely because none of the four was decisive.
Slide 10 of 13 · The professional layer
Why Security Professionals Have Codes
The frameworks tell you how to reason. The codes tell you what your profession has agreed reasoning produces.
ASIS Code
Professional Code of Conduct for security practitioners. Confidentiality, conflicts of interest, professional development.
ISACA Code
Auditing-focused. Independence, due diligence, lawful conduct, support of legitimate organizational interests.
GIAC Code
Certified practitioner-focused. Honesty, fairness, lawful behavior, technical competence, public welfare.
EthicsfIRST
Incident response specific. From FIRST.org. Coordination, confidentiality, evidence-handling, conflict resolution.
The course's argument: when frameworks disagree (slide 7), professional codes are usually the tiebreaker. They encode generations of practice about which considerations dominate in this specific profession. Module CSE-02 is the deep dive on these four codes — this slide is the preview.
Slide 11 of 13 · The CSF bridge
How This Module Maps to NIST CSF
Cyberethics is not separate from the framework — it is what the framework requires you to think about.
ID.BE — Business Environment
Spinello Ch 1 forces you to articulate what cyberspace is for in your organization's context. Without that, ID.BE is empty boilerplate.
ID.GV — Governance
Lessig's modalities of regulation are a vocabulary for governance choices. Code, norms, market, law — each is a governance lever.
PR.AT — Awareness/Training
The frameworks (utilitarian, deontological, virtue, contractual) are the awareness baseline every security professional should carry into any decision.
Why this matters: a security program that treats CSF as a checklist will pass an audit. A security program that treats CSF as a vocabulary for ethical choices will protect what matters when the audit isn't watching.

Module 01 Takeaways
Six anchors for everything that follows.
1Cyberspace breaks ordinary moral intuition through anonymity, reproducibility, action at distance, and persistence. Old frameworks need translation.
2Cyberethics is applied ethics — descriptive, normative, comparative. The course is mostly normative: arguing what should be done.
3Lessig's four modalities — law, norms, market, code — constrain behavior in different proportions. In cyberspace, code dominates.
4"Code is law" means engineers make rules whether they admit it or not. The neutrality claim is incoherent.
5Four ethical frameworks — utilitarian, deontological, virtue, contractual — usually agree, sometimes don't. The disagreements are where real decisions live.
6Sony BMG (2005) and Apple v FBI (2016) are reference cases for the rest of the course. Memorize the facts, the modalities, and which frameworks each side invoked.
