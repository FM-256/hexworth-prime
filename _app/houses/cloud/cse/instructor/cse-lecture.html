Chapter 1 · EC-Council C|CSE

What is Cloud?

Before anyone can secure it, they have to know what “it” is.

Someone else’s computer — and why that answer fails

Open with the joke, then take it apart.

One rented machine versus an API that makes a thousand The control plane is the product

The NIST definition, one clause at a time

SP 800-145 — still the definition every cert exam uses.

On-demand — nobody approves it A shared pool — a stranger on your silicon Self-service ships mistakes at machine speed

Five essential characteristics

The student deck lists them. Your job is to attach a consequence to each.

Four of them make your job harder Measured service is the one on your side

Service models are a division of labour

Teach it as “who patches what”, not as a vocabulary list.

Rent the machine rent the runtime rent the finished app Identity and data never transfer

Deployment models — and which ones they will actually meet

Weight your time toward what they will really work on.

Public is where they will work Private just moves the failure to you Breaches live at the seam

Shared responsibility: the one diagram that runs the course

Spend real time here. Every later chapter stands on this slide.

The line slides toward the provider The green foundation never moves
Chapter 2 · EC-Council C|CSE

Cloud Security Fundamentals

Same attackers, same goals — a different terrain.

What actually changes

Anchor against what they already know from on-prem security.

The wall stopped being the control Identity is the new perimeter Every request is checked, not just the edge

The threat landscape, in cloud terms

Map familiar attacks onto their cloud shapes.

Same attackers, new terrain They scan the whole internet in minutes You defend one estate, by hand

Misconfiguration is the number-one cause. Say why.

Not because engineers got worse — because the defaults meet self-service.

Not exotic exploits A default nobody changed The lock was never the weak part

CIA triad, cloud clothing

Close the chapter by tying back to ground they already trust.

Confidentiality: who can read it Integrity: has it been altered Availability: can you still reach it
Chapter 3 · EC-Council C|CSE

Identity and Access

The control plane has one front door, and it is identity.

Vocabulary first, and be strict about it

IAM discussions collapse when these words blur. Hold the line all chapter.

Authentication: who are you Authorisation: what may you do Passing the first is not passing the second

Most identities are not people

The census surprises students every time.

Machines outnumber people many times over They hold most of the privilege None of them can carry MFA

Least privilege is engineering, not aspiration

Every student will nod at the principle. Teach the practice.

Start at zero, not at convenient Add a permission only on evidence Every key you keep is a key that can leak

The IAM failures that actually cause breaches

Close on the failure catalogue — it maps to the lab.

Long-lived keys nobody rotates Wildcard permissions Admin accounts without MFA A leaked key IS the breach
Chapter 4 · EC-Council C|CSE

Data Protection

The data is yours in every service model. So is the key question.

Three states, three control families

Everything in this chapter hangs off this frame.

At rest: in the vault In transit: on the move In use: open on the bench — the hard one

Encryption at rest: the checkbox and the question

Turning it on is trivial. Knowing what it protects against is the lesson.

Ticking the box is easy The real question is who holds the key Encrypted by whom, from whom

Key management is the actual subject

Ciphers are commodity. Custody is the decision.

The key service holds it, not you Whoever can disable the key can dark every copy Key custody is data custody

Classification and lifecycle: the half that decides audits

Unglamorous, and where governance actually lives.

Not all data is equal — say which is which Retention is a decision, not a default Deleting on schedule is a control
Chapter 5 · EC-Council C|CSE

Network Security

The network stopped being a wall and became a blast radius.

The virtual network is a blast radius, not a perimeter

Reframe before the vocabulary: VPC, VNet, tenant network are the same idea.

Assume something inside will fall over Segment so it cannot spread The question is how far, not whether

Security groups vs NACLs — the exam favourite

Students conflate these every single term. Draw the difference hard.

One rides on the machine One sits on the land around it Stateless means you must allow the way back

The rule that quietly allows everything

Spend a full minute here. This single line causes more incidents than any exploit in the syllabus.

One narrow door, one open to everyone It succeeds silently — no warning Every rule answers two questions: port AND source

Private connectivity: the public internet is optional

Endpoints, private links, peering, transit. Teach the motive, not the product names.

The default route is public, even when encrypted A private path removes the audience Unreachable beats defended
Chapter 6 · EC-Council C|CSE

Application Security in Cloud

The code is yours everywhere. There is nowhere to delegate this.

The 3 Rs — and a naming collision to get ahead of

Read this before you teach the slide. Two different "3 Rs" exist and students will find both.

Rotate the secrets Repave the machine — rebuild, don't clean Repair the code Short-lived beats well-guarded

Testing methods, and what each one is blind to

Students memorise the acronyms. Teach the blind spots instead.

Static reads the code, not the running thing Dynamic pokes the running thing, not the code Every method is blind somewhere

Shift left is an economic argument

Not a slogan. Give them the reason it wins.

The same flaw, found later Cost is not linear — it explodes Shift left is an economics argument, not a virtue

Your dependencies and your images are the realistic path in

Close the chapter where the actual incidents happen.

Most of what you ship, strangers wrote The base image is the bulk of it Known flaws are already inside the pile
Chapter 7 · EC-Council C|CSE

Monitoring and Incident Response

Cloud records everything, and destroys evidence just as fast.

Three kinds of telemetry, three different questions

Students treat "logs" as one thing. Separate them and the tooling makes sense.

Audit: who did what Logs: what happened inside — and there is too much Metrics: a spike is often the first signal

The acronym fog, cleared

SIEM, SOAR, CSPM, CNAPP. Give each a one-line job so they stop blurring.

Different tools, different jobs Most overlap less than the marketing suggests Name the job, then the acronym

What incident response actually changes in cloud

The phases are unchanged. Three of the steps are not.

The instance can vanish in seconds Snapshot before you terminate Containment is an API call — so is destroying the evidence
Chapter 8 · EC-Council C|CSE

Risk and Governance

Where the whole course is finally cashed in.

Risk is a sentence, not a colour

Push back on the red/amber/green reflex immediately.

Threat, weakness, asset, likelihood The rating is the output — never the input Accept is a real answer, if someone owns it

Compliance is not security — and is not optional either

Land both halves. Students overcorrect in whichever direction they heard last.

A certificate is a point in time It proves a control existed, not that it works And you still need it — just do not confuse the two

The responsibility line reappears as an audit boundary

Chapter one's diagram, third and final appearance. Make the callback explicit.

The provider's evidence you inherit Your half you must produce yourself The line moves the paperwork too

Governance is guardrails, not documents

Close the course on the idea that makes all of it operational.

A policy nobody can violate beats a policy nobody reads Make the wrong thing impossible Guardrails are code, not paper
