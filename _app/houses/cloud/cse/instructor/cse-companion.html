Chapter 1 · companion to CSE-01

What is Cloud?

Before anyone can secure it, they have to know what "it" is.
The NIST definition and why it earns its place · the five essential characteristics · service models as a division of labour · deployment models · the one diagram that underlies the whole course: shared responsibility.

Someone else's computer — and why that answer fails

Open with the joke, then take it apart.

"The cloud is just someone else's computer" is a punchline, not a definition. The class should leave knowing exactly why it is wrong.

  • What the joke gets right. Yes, it is physical hardware in a building someone else owns. There is no magic. Every cloud region is concrete, power, and racks.
  • What it misses. Renting someone's computer gets you a computer. Cloud gets you an API that can create a thousand computers in a minute and destroy them at lunch. The product is not the machine — it is the control plane.
  • Why this matters for security. The entire discipline of this course exists because of that control plane. Nobody walks into the data centre; they walk into the API. Which means the API is the attack surface.
Class anchor: "You will not defend a building this term. You will defend an API."

The NIST definition, one clause at a time

SP 800-145 — still the definition every cert exam uses.

"Ubiquitous, convenient, on-demand network access to a shared pool of configurable computing resources that can be rapidly provisioned and released with minimal management effort or service provider interaction."

  • On-demand. No procurement, no ticket, no waiting for a human. The moment a definition requires a human in the loop, it stops being cloud.
  • Shared pool. Multi-tenancy. Your workload and a stranger's workload on the same silicon. Half of cloud security exists because of this clause alone.
  • Rapidly provisioned and released. Elasticity cuts both ways: attackers also provision rapidly. A leaked key becomes a crypto-mining fleet in minutes.
  • Minimal provider interaction. Self-service means user mistakes ship at machine speed with nobody reviewing them.
Ask the room: "Which clause of that definition do you think causes the most security incidents?" Let them argue; steer to self-service + shared pool.

Five essential characteristics

The student deck lists them; your job is to attach a security consequence to each.

CharacteristicSecurity consequence to say out loud
On-demand self-serviceMisconfiguration ships instantly. The reviewer is gone.
Broad network accessEverything is reachable, so everything is probeable. There is no "internal only" by default.
Resource poolingTenant isolation is now a provider promise you must verify, not a wall you can see.
Rapid elasticityCost becomes an attack surface: a stolen key does not steal data, it runs up a bill.
Measured serviceThe good news — everything is metered, so everything can be logged and alarmed.
Class anchor: "Four of these five make security harder. Measured service is the one that fights for you — remember it when we reach monitoring in chapter six's material."

Service models are a division of labour

IaaS / PaaS / SaaS — teach it as "who patches what", not as a vocabulary list.

  • IaaS. You rent the machine. Provider secures the concrete up to the hypervisor; the OS, patches, network rules and everything above are yours. Most control, most responsibility. The OpenStack sandbox they use in this house is exactly this.
  • PaaS. You rent the runtime. The OS disappears from your worry list; your code, its dependencies and its configuration remain. Students underestimate the "dependencies" part — that is where PaaS breaches live.
  • SaaS. You rent the finished application. What remains is the part nobody can outsource: who can log in, what they can see, and where the data goes. Identity and data are ALWAYS yours.
Ask the room: "In SaaS, the provider does almost everything. So how do SaaS tenants still get breached?" Answer to draw out: their users, their tokens, their sharing settings.

Deployment models — and which ones they will actually meet

Public, private, hybrid, community. Weight your time accordingly.

  • Public. Provider-owned, multi-tenant, internet-delivered. Where nearly every graduate will work. Give it most of the airtime.
  • Private. Cloud mechanics, single tenant. The point to land: private does not mean secure — it means the isolation failures are now YOUR failures. Our own OpenStack deployment is a private cloud, and the class has seen it misconfigured in labs.
  • Hybrid. The realistic enterprise answer. The security story is the SEAM: identity federation and the network path between the two halves. Breaches live at seams.
  • Community. Mention for the exam, move on. Regulated groups sharing infrastructure; think research consortiums or agencies.
Class anchor: "Exam answer: four models. Career answer: public, hybrid, and the seam between them."

Shared responsibility: the one diagram that runs the course

Spend real time here. Every later chapter stands on this slide.

The provider secures the cloud. The customer secures what they put IN the cloud. Every incident this course dissects is someone misreading that line.

  • Always the provider's: physical facilities, hardware, the hypervisor, the global network fabric.
  • Always the customer's: identity and access, data classification, and configuration. No service model takes these away. Say that twice.
  • The sliding middle: OS, runtime, application — slides toward the provider as you go IaaS to SaaS.
  • The trap. "The provider is certified, so we are secure." Provider certification covers their half. Most cloud breaches happen entirely inside the customer half — a public bucket, an over-broad role, a leaked key.
Class anchor: "When you read about a cloud breach, ask one question first: whose half of the line was it on? It is almost always the customer's."
The line slides right as you move to SaaS — but the green band never moves.
Chapter 2 · companion to CSE-01

Cloud Security Fundamentals

Same attackers, same goals — a different terrain.
What actually changes when workloads leave the building · the threat landscape in provider terms · misconfiguration as the number-one cause · where the classic CIA triad shows up in cloud clothing.

What actually changes

Anchor against what they already know from on-prem security.

  • The perimeter dissolves. On-prem, the firewall was the moat. In cloud, every resource has its own edge and identity IS the perimeter. This sentence previews chapter three; plant it now.
  • Infrastructure becomes code. A typo in a template is now a security incident. But the same fact means security can be reviewed, versioned and tested like code — the defender's best new tool.
  • Speed symmetry. Defenders deploy in minutes; so do attackers. The window between "vulnerable" and "found" is hours, not months. Show them a honeypot study if time allows — new public endpoints get probed within minutes.
  • Visibility inverts. You lose the ability to tap the wire, and gain logs of every single API call. Trade packet capture for CloudTrail-style audit trails — better, if you actually read them.
Class anchor: "Nothing about the attacker changed. The terrain changed. This course is terrain training."

The threat landscape, in cloud terms

Map familiar attacks onto their cloud shapes.

Classic threatIts cloud shape
Stolen passwordLeaked access key or token — in a public repo, a paste site, a CI log. No login page involved.
Open portPublic storage bucket, permissive security group, exposed management API.
Privilege escalationRole-chaining: assume a slightly-too-broad role, use it to reach a broader one. Pure IAM, no exploit code.
Insider threatSame, plus the provider's insiders — which is what provider audits and encryption-with-your-keys are for.
Denial of serviceTwo flavours: knock it down, or make it scale until the BILL is the damage. Economic DoS is cloud-native.
Ask the room: "Which of these needs zero technical exploitation?" Most of them. Cloud attacks are mostly authorized actions by the wrong party.

Misconfiguration is the number-one cause. Say why.

Not because engineers got worse — because the defaults meet self-service.

  • Scale of surface. A modest cloud estate has thousands of individually-configurable resources, each with its own policy document. Every one is a chance to be wrong.
  • Nobody reviews. Self-service (chapter one, clause one) means the misconfiguration ships the moment it is typed.
  • Drift. Correct on Monday is not correct in June. Configuration decays unless something continuously re-checks it — that is why posture management tools exist.
  • The classic case. Public object storage: gigabytes of customer data behind a checkbox. Walk one real disclosure with the class — the pattern repeats yearly with new names attached.
Demo cue: in the OpenStack sandbox, create a security group rule open to 0.0.0.0/0 and let the class watch how unremarkable it looks. The point: the worst mistake in cloud security produces no error message.

CIA triad, cloud clothing

Close the chapter by tying back to ground they already trust.

  • Confidentiality becomes IAM plus encryption plus not-being-public. The failure mode is rarely broken crypto; it is correct data served to the wrong principal.
  • Integrity becomes versioning, immutability flags, and signed artifacts. Object storage with versioning turned on is an integrity control — students rarely see it that way until told.
  • Availability becomes architecture: multi-AZ, auto-scaling, backups in a different blast radius. They have played this — The Nines game in this house is availability engineering wearing a game's clothes.
Class anchor: "Old triad, new levers. If you can name which leg of the triad a control protects, you understand the control."
Chapter 3 · companion to CSE-02

Identity & Access Management

Identity is the new perimeter — now prove it to them.
The vocabulary that must be exact · human and non-human identities · authentication versus authorization · least privilege as an engineering practice · the IAM failures that actually cause breaches.

Vocabulary first, and be strict about it

IAM discussions collapse when these words blur. Hold the line all chapter.

  • Identity — who or what something is. A user, a service, a machine.
  • Principal — an identity in the act of making a request. The subject of every policy decision.
  • Authentication — proving the identity. Passwords, keys, tokens, MFA. AuthN answers "is it really you?"
  • Authorization — deciding what the proven identity may do. Policies, roles, scopes. AuthZ answers "are you allowed?"
  • The confusion to kill early: a request can be perfectly authenticated and still be an attack. Most cloud breaches are AUTHENTICATED requests — the right credentials in the wrong hands, or the right hands with too much power.
Class anchor: "AuthN is the door checking your ID. AuthZ is the rooms your badge opens. Breaches walk through doors that opened correctly."

Most identities are not people

The census surprises students every time.

In a real cloud estate, machine identities outnumber humans many times over — and they hold most of the privilege.

  • Human identities: engineers, analysts, admins. Federated from a directory, protected by MFA, and comparatively well-watched.
  • Service identities: the app talking to its database, the function reading the queue, the CI pipeline deploying. No MFA possible. Credentials are keys and tokens.
  • Why attackers prefer machines: service credentials do not get suspicious, do not report phishing, and often sit in code, config files and CI logs waiting to be found. A leaked service key IS the modern breach.
  • The design answer (name it now, deepen it later): short-lived credentials issued at runtime beat long-lived keys stored anywhere. If a credential must exist forever, it will eventually leak.
Ask the room: "Your phone gets MFA. What protects the CI pipeline's credentials?" Sit in the silence a moment — that silence is the lesson.
The privilege sits in the row you cannot put MFA on.

Least privilege is engineering, not aspiration

Every student will nod at the principle. Teach the practice.

  • Why over-permissioning happens: broad access makes the error go away at 2am, and nothing ever forces the cleanup. Privilege only ratchets upward unless something pushes back.
  • Start from zero. Grant nothing, add what breaks. The reverse — grant everything, remove what seems unused — never completes. Nobody dares remove the last unknown permission.
  • Prefer roles over identities. Assign privilege to the JOB, let identities assume the job temporarily. Time-boxed assumption beats permanent attachment.
  • Audit the gap. Modern platforms can diff permissions GRANTED against permissions actually USED in the last 90 days. That diff is your least-privilege to-do list, generated for free.
Demo cue: the students met this the hard way — the IAM Policy Debugger game in this house is ten rounds of exactly this slide. Assign it as the homework companion.

The IAM failures that actually cause breaches

Close on the failure catalogue — it maps to the lab.

FailureWhat to say about it
Leaked long-lived keysCommitted to a repo, pasted in a ticket, baked into an image. Scanners find them in minutes. Rotation limits the blast; short-lived credentials remove it.
Wildcard policiesResource star, action star. Written to make an error disappear; discovered in the post-incident review.
No MFA on privileged humansOne phished admin password becomes the whole estate. MFA on privileged accounts is the single highest-return control in this chapter.
Orphaned identitiesThe contractor left; the access did not. Joiner-mover-leaver is an IAM process, not an HR process.
Role-chain escalationA modest role that can EDIT policies is a superuser with extra steps. Watch for privilege to grant privilege.
Class anchor: "None of these is an exploit. Every one is paperwork with consequences. That is IAM."
Chapter 4 · companion to CSE-03

Data Protection & Encryption

The data is the target. Everything else is the route.
The three states of data and the control that owns each · encryption at rest and in transit without the hand-waving · key management as the real subject · classification and lifecycle as the unglamorous half that decides audits.

Three states, three control families

Everything in this chapter hangs off this frame.

  • At rest — sitting in storage. Owned by encryption plus storage access policy. The state where the checkbox is easy and the key question is hard.
  • In transit — moving between parties. Owned by TLS. The modern default is encrypt EVERYTHING in motion, including service-to-service inside your own network — the "internal traffic is safe" assumption died with the perimeter.
  • In use — loaded in memory, being computed on. The frontier: confidential computing, enclave tech. One slide of awareness now; it will be an exam topic within a few years.
  • The framing that sticks: for any dataset, ask "where does it rest, how does it move, who computes on it" — and you have just produced its protection requirements.
Class anchor: "Name the state, and the control names itself."

Encryption at rest: the checkbox and the question

Turning it on is trivial. Knowing what it protects against is the lesson.

  • What the checkbox buys: protection against stolen disks, decommissioned hardware, and some classes of provider-side exposure. Real, but narrow.
  • What it does not buy: protection from any principal with read permission. Storage encryption is invisible to an authorized API call — the bucket breach happens over HTTPS to encrypted storage, and the encryption never enters the story.
  • Therefore: encryption at rest COMPLEMENTS access control; it never substitutes for it. Students conflate the two on exams and in production alike.
  • Envelope encryption — teach the two-tier shape: data encrypted by a data key, data key encrypted by a master key that never leaves the key service. Rotation and revocation become key-service operations instead of re-encrypting terabytes.
Ask the room: "The bucket was encrypted and the data still leaked. What happened?" If they can answer this cold, the slide worked.

Key management is the actual subject

Ciphers are commodity. Custody is the decision.

ModelCustodyWhen it fits
Provider-managed keysProvider generates, stores, rotatesDefault. Fine for most workloads; zero operational load.
Customer-managed keysProvider's key service, YOUR key policy and rotationWhen you need revocation power and per-key audit — the usual compliance answer.
Bring / hold your own keyKey material originates — or lives — outside the providerRegulated edges. The trade: sovereignty up, complexity and failure modes up with it.
  • The kill switch framing: whoever can disable the key can make the data unreadable everywhere at once. Key custody is data custody.
  • Key policy is IAM again — a key with a wildcard policy undoes the whole chapter. Chapters three and four are one subject wearing two names.
Class anchor: "Nobody breaks the cipher. They obtain the key, or they obtain permission. Guard those."
Rotation and revocation become key-service operations — not re-encrypting terabytes.

Classification and lifecycle: the half that decides audits

Unglamorous, and where governance actually lives.

  • You cannot protect what you have not classified. Public, internal, confidential, restricted — the tiers matter less than the act of tiering. Controls attach to tiers, not to individual files.
  • Tag at creation. Classification retrofitted onto an existing data lake is archaeology. Classification at write time is a field in a form.
  • Lifecycle is a security control: data you deleted on schedule cannot be breached, subpoenaed, or ransomed. Retention policy is the cheapest encryption there is.
  • Residency — where data physically lives now carries legal weight. Regions are a compliance control; note it here, deepen it in the governance chapter's material.
Class anchor: "The breach report never says 'they broke AES.' It says 'we did not know that data was there.' Classification is how you know."
Chapter 5 · companion to CSE-04

Network Security in Cloud

The network did not disappear. It became software you can get wrong at scale.
The virtual network as the new blast-radius boundary · the two filters students conflate · segmentation that survives contact · private connectivity and why the public internet is optional · the rules that quietly allow everything.

The virtual network is a blast radius, not a perimeter

Reframe before the vocabulary: VPC, VNet, tenant network are the same idea.

Chapter two said the perimeter dissolved. This chapter is what replaced it: many small boundaries instead of one big one.

  • Same concept, three names. AWS calls it a VPC, Azure a VNet, OpenStack just a network. Do not let vendor vocabulary become a third thing to learn — teach the concept once, then name it three ways.
  • What it actually gives you: a private address space you control, and a default deny at its edge. Everything inside can talk; nothing outside gets in without an explicit path.
  • Why "blast radius" is the better word. A perimeter implies inside is safe. A blast radius implies: when — not if — something in here is compromised, how far does it reach? That question designs better networks.
  • The design consequence. More, smaller networks beat one large one, for the same reason bulkheads beat one big hull.
Class anchor: "Do not ask whether the network is secure. Ask how far an attacker gets from any single foothold inside it."

Security groups vs NACLs — the exam favourite

Students conflate these every single term. Draw the difference hard.

Security groupNetwork ACL
Attaches toThe instance / interfaceThe subnet
StateStateful — return traffic is automaticStateless — you must allow the return path yourself
RulesAllow onlyAllow AND deny, evaluated in order
Typical useThe everyday control. Most work happens here.Coarse subnet-wide guardrails, explicit blocks
  • The failure this produces: a student writes a NACL allowing inbound 443, tests it, and nothing works — because the ephemeral-port return traffic was never allowed. Stateless is the whole answer.
Ask the room: "You allowed inbound 443 and the page still will not load. Which of the two did you edit?" The answer teaches statefulness better than the definition does.
One sits on the subnet, one sits on the machine. That is the whole distinction.

The rule that quietly allows everything

Spend a full minute here. This single line causes more incidents than any exploit in the syllabus.

0.0.0.0/0 means "the entire internet". It is one keystroke from 10.0.0.0/8 and produces no warning, no error, and no visible difference in the console.

  • Where it is defensible: port 443 on a load balancer that is deliberately public. That is the job.
  • Where it is a breach: port 22, 3389, or a database port. Management and data planes should never face the internet, and mass-scanners find them in minutes — chapter two's speed symmetry, made concrete.
  • The habit to teach: every rule answers two questions, port AND source. Students fixate on the port and default the source to anywhere. Make them say the source out loud each time.
  • The systemic fix: guardrails that reject the rule at creation beat audits that find it later. Preventive control over detective control — a theme that returns in chapter eight.
Demo cue: in the OpenStack sandbox, create a security group rule open to 0.0.0.0/0. Let them see how completely ordinary it looks. The worst mistake in cloud networking produces no error message.
Same rule, same console, no warning. One is 443 on a load balancer. The other is the entire internet.

Private connectivity: the public internet is optional

Endpoints, private links, peering, transit. Teach the motive, not the product names.

  • The default surprises people. Reaching a provider's own storage service from your own subnet normally traverses a public endpoint. Encrypted, yes — but publicly addressable.
  • Service endpoints / private links pull that service onto a private address inside your network. Traffic never touches the public internet, and policy can then require that path.
  • Peering joins two networks directly. The trap: peering is not transitive. A peered to B and B peered to C does NOT give A a path to C — students assume it does.
  • Transit gateway / hub exists because of that non-transitivity: a central hub instead of a mesh that grows as the square of your network count.
  • Bastion / jump hosts for administrative access — one hardened, logged door instead of port 22 open on everything.
Class anchor: "If the data never needs the public internet, do not give it a route there. Unreachable beats defended."
Chapter 6 · companion to CSE-05

Application Security in Cloud

The code is yours in every service model. There is nowhere to delegate this.
The 3 Rs — and a naming collision to get ahead of · testing methods and what each one cannot see · shift left as economics · dependencies as the realistic attack path · containers and what the image actually contains.

The 3 Rs — and a naming collision to get ahead of

Read this before you teach the slide. Two different "3 Rs" exist and students will find both.

The student deck teaches Reliability, Resiliency, Recovery. That is what is assessed — teach it. But be ready for the other one.

  • The deck's three: Reliability (it works as expected), Resiliency (it withstands misuse or attack), Recovery (it can be restored with minimal disruption). A design-quality frame.
  • The industry three: Rotate, Repave, Repair — rotate credentials often, rebuild servers from known-good images frequently, patch fast. An operations frame, widely cited since 2016 and very likely what a student finds when they search the phrase.
  • Do not pretend the collision is not there. Name both, say which one the assessment uses, and note they answer different questions: one is about how the software is designed, the other about how the platform is operated.
  • The link worth drawing: "repave" is why immutable infrastructure is a security control — a server rebuilt nightly gives an attacker a very short tenancy.
Class anchor: "Two frameworks share a nickname. On the exam, the three Rs are Reliability, Resiliency, Recovery. In a job interview, be able to discuss both."

Testing methods, and what each one is blind to

Students memorise the acronyms. Teach the blind spots instead.

MethodSeesCannot see
SAST (static, white box)Source code, before it runsAnything that only exists at runtime — config, environment, real data flow. Famously noisy.
DAST (dynamic, black box)The running application from outsideCode paths it never exercises. Finds real bugs, misses unreached ones.
SCA (composition)Third-party dependencies and their known CVEsFlaws in YOUR code. Different question entirely.
  • The point to land: these are not competitors, they are different instruments. Any one alone leaves a category of bug invisible.
  • The practitioner reality: SAST false positives are the reason security tools get ignored. A tool nobody trusts is worse than no tool, because it manufactures the belief that someone is looking.
Ask the room: "Which of these would have caught Log4Shell in your app?" SCA — it was a dependency, not your code. That lands the distinction permanently.

Shift left is an economic argument

Not a slogan. Give them the reason it wins.

  • The claim: a flaw caught in design costs a conversation. In code review, minutes. In production, an incident with customers, regulators and a bill.
  • Be honest about the numbers. The famous cost-multiplier figures are old and contested. The DIRECTION is not: fixing later costs more. Teach the direction, not a fabricated multiplier — students will meet people who ask where the number came from.
  • What it means concretely: threat modelling at design time, security tests in the pipeline, and a build that FAILS on a critical finding. A warning nobody must act on is not a control.
  • The counter-pressure to acknowledge: shift left done badly becomes a wall of alerts developers route around. Fewer, higher-confidence gates beat comprehensive noise.
Class anchor: "Shift left is not about finding more. It is about finding earlier, when the fix is still cheap and nobody is on a call about it."

Your dependencies and your images are the realistic path in

Close the chapter where the actual incidents happen.

Most application code in a modern service was written by strangers. That is the supply chain, and it is the attack path that keeps working.

  • The dependency tree is deeper than anyone thinks. A handful of direct dependencies routinely pulls in hundreds transitively. Nobody has read them. SCA exists because nobody can.
  • Containers do not shrink this, they package it. A base image carries an entire OS distribution's worth of packages, most unused, all inheritable as CVEs. Minimal and distroless bases are a security control, not a size optimisation.
  • Provenance beats scanning alone: signed images, pinned digests instead of mutable tags, and a registry you control. :latest is not a version, it is a promise someone else can change.
  • Secrets in images — baked-in keys survive in every layer forever, even if a later layer deletes the file. Layers are additive; deletion is cosmetic.
Demo cue: pull any popular base image and run a scanner. The count of known CVEs in something nobody wrote a line of is the whole lesson.
The small bright cube is what your team wrote. Everything holding it up arrived with the base image.
Chapter 7 · companion to CSE-06

Security Monitoring & Incident Response

Chapter one promised measured service would fight for you. Collect on that promise.
The three telemetry types and which answers which question · the audit log that matters most · SIEM, SOAR, CSPM, CNAPP without the acronym fog · what incident response actually changes in cloud.

Three kinds of telemetry, three different questions

Students treat "logs" as one thing. Separate them and the tooling makes sense.

TypeAnswersSecurity use
Audit / activityWho did what, to which resource, when, from whereThe primary security source. Every control-plane API call.
Application & system logsWhat happened inside the workloadDetail during investigation. Volume problem at scale.
MetricsHow much, how fast, how manyAnomalies — a cost or egress spike is often the first breach signal.
  • The one to emphasise: the audit log. It is cloud's genuine advantage over on-prem — every administrative action, recorded, by default. On-prem never had this.
  • The catch worth stating: it is only useful if it is enabled everywhere, kept long enough, and written somewhere the attacker cannot edit. All three fail routinely.
Class anchor: "In cloud you trade packet capture for a complete record of every action taken. That is a good trade — if anyone reads it."
Cloud records every control-plane call by default. All three conditions below fail silently.

The acronym fog, cleared

SIEM, SOAR, CSPM, CNAPP. Give each a one-line job so they stop blurring.

  • SIEM — collects logs from everywhere and correlates them. The question it answers: "did something bad happen across these systems?"
  • SOAR — automates the response once something fires. "Do the first five steps without waiting for a human at 3am."
  • CSPM — continuously checks CONFIGURATION against policy. Not "did something happen" but "is anything currently wrong" — the public bucket, the wildcard role. Directly answers chapter two's misconfiguration problem.
  • CNAPP — the consolidation: posture plus workload plus identity plus code, one platform. Mostly a market term; teach it as "the category vendors converged on", not as a distinct technology.
Ask the room: "A bucket has been public for six months, with no attack. Which tool tells you?" CSPM. A SIEM sees events; only posture management sees a bad state that is quietly sitting there.

What incident response actually changes in cloud

The phases are unchanged. Three of the steps are not.

Prepare, detect, contain, eradicate, recover, learn — the framework survives. The mechanics change in ways worth naming.

  • Containment is an API call. Isolate by revoking a role or replacing a security group, in seconds. Faster than any physical response ever was — and reversible if you are wrong.
  • Evidence is ephemeral. Terminate the instance and the disk may be gone forever. Snapshot BEFORE you contain. Students' instinct is to kill it first; correct that instinct explicitly.
  • Identity is the first question, not the last. Which credential did this? Cloud incidents are usually credential incidents. Rotating the key is often the actual containment.
  • Recovery can be a redeploy. If infrastructure is code, rebuild from source rather than clean a compromised host. Chapter six's "repave", arriving on schedule.
Class anchor: "Snapshot, then contain. Do it in the other order and you have destroyed the only copy of the evidence."
Chapter 8 · companion to CSE-07 and CSE-08

Risk, Compliance & Governance

The chapter students dismiss as paperwork, taught as the thing that decides budgets.
Risk as a sentence with numbers in it · the NIST RMF without reciting steps · compliance versus security · the shared-responsibility line reappearing as an audit boundary · governance as guardrails rather than documents.

Risk is a sentence, not a colour

Push back on the red/amber/green reflex immediately.

"High risk" is not a finding. A risk statement names a threat, an asset, a likelihood and an impact — and only then earns a rating.

  • The shape: a threat actor exploits a vulnerability against an asset, causing an impact, with some likelihood. Miss any part and you cannot decide anything.
  • Why the rating comes last. The colour is an output. Students want to start there because it is the part that appears on the slide.
  • The four responses: mitigate, transfer, avoid, accept. Accept is a legitimate engineering answer — documented, owned, revisited. Students think accept means failure; it means someone with authority made a decision.
  • Cloud-specific twist: some risk transfers to the provider automatically, and it is NOT the risk students expect. You transfer hardware and facility risk. You never transfer identity, data, or configuration — chapter one's line, in risk vocabulary.
Ask the room: "Name a risk you would accept rather than mitigate." Their answers reveal whether they understand cost, or just fear.
The colour is an output. Students want to start there because it is what appears on the slide.

Compliance is not security — and is not optional either

Land both halves. Students overcorrect in whichever direction they heard last.

  • Compliant and breached is a normal sentence. A framework is a floor built from past incidents, assessed periodically. Attackers do not consult it.
  • And yet it is load-bearing. Compliance is what makes security funded, scheduled and audited. Engineers who sneer at it tend to work somewhere the budget request already succeeded.
  • Know which is which: GDPR / FISMA / FERPA / HIPAA are law — non-compliance is a legal matter. ISO 27001 / SOC 2 / PCI DSS are standards and contracts — non-compliance loses customers. FedRAMP is the gate for US federal cloud work.
  • CSA CCM and STAR are cloud-specific: a control matrix mapped across the other frameworks, and a public registry of provider attestations. Genuinely useful for evaluating a provider — show them the registry rather than describing it.
Class anchor: "Compliance is the floor, not the ceiling. Build to the floor and you will pass the audit and still get breached."

The responsibility line reappears as an audit boundary

Chapter one's diagram, third and final appearance. Make the callback explicit.

Provider certification covers the provider's half. Your auditor asks about yours — and this is where inherited controls become a real technique.

  • Inheritance. Physical security, environmental controls and hardware disposal come from the provider's attestation. You reference their report; you do not re-audit their data centre.
  • What never inherits: identity, data classification, configuration, logging, key custody. Same list as chapter one, third time asked. Repetition is the point.
  • Evidence is now queryable. Cloud's genuine gift to auditors: "prove every database is encrypted" is an API call, not a sampling exercise. Continuous compliance is possible for the first time.
  • The trap: "our provider is SOC 2 certified, so we are compliant." False, and stated confidently in real organisations. Their certificate is not yours.
Class anchor: "You inherit their controls, not their compliance. The auditor is asking about your half."

Governance is guardrails, not documents

Close the course on the idea that makes all of it operational.

A policy nobody can violate beats a policy everybody has signed. In cloud, policy can be code that refuses.

  • Preventive beats detective. A guardrail that refuses to create a public bucket is worth more than a report listing the public buckets you already have. Same policy, radically different outcome.
  • Where guardrails live: organisation-level policies, pipeline checks, and templates that are secure by default. Make the paved road the easy road and compliance stops depending on discipline.
  • Landing zones — a pre-governed account structure new teams are given. The idea worth leaving them with: security that arrives before the workload does.
  • The honest limit: guardrails constrain, and constraint gets routed around when it blocks real work. Governance that ignores developer experience produces shadow IT, which is worse than the risk it prevented.
Class anchor: "Every chapter of this course ends up here: make the secure path the easy path, and you stop relying on everyone being careful."
