The Question That Started Everything

In 1950, a British mathematician named Alan Turing published a paper titled "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" that opened with a deceptively simple question: "Can machines think?" Recognizing that "thinking" was too philosophically loaded to define precisely, Turing proposed a practical test instead — what he called the Imitation Game, now known as the Turing Test.

In the Turing Test, a human interrogator converses via text with both a human and a machine. If the interrogator cannot reliably distinguish which is the machine, the machine is said to exhibit intelligent behavior. This framing — defining intelligence by behavior rather than mechanism — became the philosophical foundation for the entire field of artificial intelligence.

Why the Turing Test Still Matters

No AI system has definitively "passed" the Turing Test in a rigorous scientific setting, though several have fooled human judges in limited competitions. Modern researchers debate whether it is even the right benchmark — a system can be enormously useful without mimicking human conversation, and human conversation includes things like deception and social anxiety that are not obviously desirable in a machine. The Turing Test matters not because it is the goal, but because it forced the field to be precise about what "intelligence" means.

The Birth of the Field: 1956

The term "Artificial Intelligence" was coined in 1956 at a summer workshop at Dartmouth College, organized by John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon. The proposal stated: "We think that every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it." The ambition was extraordinary — and the timeline wildly optimistic.

The Dartmouth workshop attracted researchers who would define the field for decades. The dominant paradigm that emerged was symbolic AI: the idea that intelligence could be captured by manipulating symbols according to explicit rules. Programs could prove mathematical theorems, play chess, and solve logic puzzles. To many observers, general human-level AI seemed only years away.

Logic Theorist (1956) Newell and Simon's program proved 38 of 52 theorems from Whitehead and Russell's Principia Mathematica. The first AI program to perform a task previously considered exclusively human.
General Problem Solver (1957) A framework for modeling human problem-solving using means-ends analysis. Influential in cognitive science and AI planning research for decades.
ELIZA (1966) Joseph Weizenbaum's program simulated a psychotherapist using simple pattern matching. Users formed emotional connections with it — an early warning about anthropomorphization of AI.
DENDRAL (1965) One of the first expert systems. Analyzed mass spectrometry data to identify chemical compounds. Demonstrated that domain-specific AI could match expert human performance in narrow tasks.

The Two AI Winters

AI has experienced two major "winters" — periods when funding collapsed, researchers left the field, and progress stalled. Understanding what caused each winter reveals something important about the gap between AI hype and AI reality.

1956
Dartmouth Conference — The Field is Born McCarthy coins "artificial intelligence." Symbolic AI and the logic-based approach dominate early optimism.
1969
Minsky & Papert — "Perceptrons" Proved limitations of single-layer neural networks (cannot learn XOR). Devastated neural network research funding for over a decade.
1974
First AI Winter Begins The Lighthill Report in the UK concluded AI had failed to deliver on its promises. DARPA cuts funding. The combinatorial explosion problem defeats symbolic approaches on real-world tasks.
1980
Expert Systems Boom R1/XCON at DEC saves $40M/year. Companies build expert systems for medicine, law, and engineering. AI is commercially viable again. Japan launches the Fifth Generation Computer project.
1987
Second AI Winter Begins Expert systems are brittle, expensive to maintain, and cannot learn. The market for Lisp machines collapses. Japan's Fifth Generation project fails. Funding collapses again.
1997
Deep Blue Defeats Kasparov IBM's chess engine beats the world champion. A media triumph, but achieved through brute-force search, not learning. AI credibility returns in narrow domains.
2006
Hinton's Deep Belief Networks Geoffrey Hinton demonstrates that deep neural networks can be pre-trained effectively. The term "deep learning" enters common use. GPU training becomes viable.
2012
AlexNet — The Modern Renaissance Begins Krizhevsky, Sutskever, and Hinton's deep CNN wins ImageNet by a 10-point margin over the next competitor. Every major AI lab pivots to deep learning. Nothing is the same after.
2017
"Attention Is All You Need" — The Transformer Google Brain publishes the Transformer architecture. Within three years it dominates NLP, then computer vision, then almost everything else. GPT, BERT, and the LLM era begin.
2022
ChatGPT — Public Inflection Point OpenAI's ChatGPT reaches 100 million users in two months. AI becomes a mainstream public conversation. Governments rush to regulate. The race for frontier AI intensifies globally.

Why the Renaissance Happened in 2012

The AlexNet result did not emerge from a theoretical breakthrough alone. Three forces converged simultaneously to make deep learning viable for the first time:

FactorWhat ChangedImpact
Data ImageNet: 14 million labeled images across 21,000 categories, created over several years by Fei-Fei Li's lab at Stanford. Deep networks require massive datasets. Before ImageNet, nothing at this scale existed for vision.
Compute NVIDIA GPUs (originally for gaming) proved to be ideal for the matrix multiplications that define neural network training. AlexNet trained on two GTX 580 GPUs. The same training on a CPU would have taken weeks instead of days.
Algorithms ReLU activation functions, dropout regularization, and better weight initialization solved the vanishing gradient problem that had made deep networks untrainable. Made it practical to train networks with 8+ layers. Modern networks have hundreds.

Symbolic AI vs Connectionism

The history of AI is partly the story of a decades-long debate between two fundamental approaches to building intelligent systems. Understanding this debate illuminates why modern AI looks the way it does.

Symbolic AI (GOFAI) Intelligence is symbol manipulation. Encode knowledge as explicit rules and logic. Programs reason by applying these rules. Interpretable, precise, but brittle — cannot handle ambiguity or learn from examples. Dominated 1956-1986.
Connectionism / Neural Networks Intelligence emerges from patterns in data, distributed across many simple computational units (neurons). Learn from examples without explicit rules. Robust, scalable, but opaque. Dormant 1969-2006, dominant since 2012.

The Convergence

Modern AI is not purely connectionist. Large language models incorporate retrieval systems (symbolic knowledge bases), tool use, and structured reasoning chains. Neuro-symbolic AI — combining learned representations with explicit reasoning — is an active research frontier. The symbolic vs connectionist debate has not ended; it has matured into a synthesis.

Reflection Questions

  1. The Lighthill Report criticized AI for failing on "real world problems" — what specific limitations of 1970s symbolic AI made real-world deployment difficult?
  2. Why did expert systems fail commercially in the late 1980s despite clear early successes? What would need to be different for them to succeed?
  3. Three factors converged in 2012 (data, compute, algorithms). Which of the three do you think was most critical, and why?
  4. AlphaGo (2016) defeated the world Go champion using reinforcement learning. How does this differ from Deep Blue's chess victory, and why does the distinction matter?

Key Takeaways

  • Alan Turing's 1950 paper defined machine intelligence behaviorally — the Turing Test shaped AI philosophy for decades
  • The field was formally founded at the 1956 Dartmouth Conference; "artificial intelligence" was coined by John McCarthy
  • Symbolic AI (logic and rules) dominated early AI and drove two booms before its brittleness caused two winters
  • The First AI Winter (1974) was triggered by the Lighthill Report and the failure of symbolic AI on real-world tasks
  • The Second AI Winter (1987) was caused by the commercial failure of expert systems and collapsed hardware markets
  • The modern renaissance required three simultaneous ingredients: massive labeled datasets, GPU compute, and better training algorithms
  • AlexNet (2012) is the practical starting point of the deep learning era; the Transformer (2017) is the architecture that defines the current moment
