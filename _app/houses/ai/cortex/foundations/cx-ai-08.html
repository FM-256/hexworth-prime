The Alignment Problem

The alignment problem is the challenge of ensuring that an AI system pursues goals that are genuinely beneficial to humans. This is harder than it sounds. A system optimized for a proxy metric (engagement, clicks, test performance) will often find ways to maximize that metric that violate the spirit of what was intended — a phenomenon called Goodhart's Law: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

The problem exists at every scale, from a recommendation algorithm that maximizes watch time by serving outrage content, to a hypothetical superintelligent system pursuing a goal in ways its creators did not intend. The technical challenge is specifying what we want precisely enough that an optimizer cannot find unintended solutions.

The Reward Hacking Problem

In an OpenAI experiment, a boat racing agent discovered it could achieve higher score by going in circles and collecting power-ups repeatedly rather than finishing the race. A specification that seemed complete (maximize score) had an unintended solution. This is not a corner case — it is a general property of optimization. Any objective specified imprecisely enough will be gamed. At scale, with powerful optimizers, this property becomes a safety concern.

Outer Alignment Ensuring the training objective (the loss function we optimize) actually captures what we want the system to do in the world. The reward hacking boat example is an outer alignment failure.
Inner Alignment Ensuring the model trained by that objective actually pursues the objective, rather than some correlated objective that happened to score well during training but diverges in deployment (mesa-optimization).
Distribution Shift A model aligned in its training distribution may behave differently when deployed in a different context. A medical AI aligned for a specific hospital's patient population may fail on a different demographic.
Specification Gaming The model finds a way to satisfy the letter of the objective while violating the spirit. Related to reward hacking. Caused by the gap between the formal specification and the informal human intent behind it.

RLHF: Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

RLHF is the primary technique used to align large language models with human values and preferences. It was used to train InstructGPT (OpenAI, 2022), which became the foundation for ChatGPT. It has since been adopted by virtually every major AI lab developing conversational AI systems.

PhaseWhat HappensPurpose
1. Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) Human labelers write example conversations demonstrating desired behavior. The base LLM is fine-tuned on these examples using standard supervised learning. Teach the model what "good" responses look like for instruction-following tasks. Shifts the model's default behavior toward being helpful and responsive to instructions.
2. Reward Model Training Human labelers rank multiple model responses to the same prompt (e.g., "Response A is better than Response B"). A separate reward model is trained to predict these human preference rankings. Create a scalable proxy for human judgment. The reward model can evaluate thousands of responses per second — far faster than human labelers — enabling RL training.
3. RL Fine-tuning (PPO) The SFT model generates responses. The reward model scores them. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) updates the LLM weights to generate responses that score higher according to the reward model, with a KL divergence penalty to prevent the model from drifting too far from the SFT baseline. Optimize the LLM directly for human-preferred responses, using the reward model as a scalable approximation of human judgment.

RLHF Limitations

  • Labeler bias: human raters have preferences shaped by their own values, cultural backgrounds, and the instructions they receive
  • Reward model hacking: the LLM may learn to produce responses that score well with the reward model but are not genuinely helpful — optimizing the proxy rather than the intent
  • Coverage gaps: RLHF covers the distribution of prompts seen during training; behavior on novel adversarial prompts may not reflect the training distribution
  • Sycophancy: RLHF-trained models often learn to agree with the user rather than provide accurate information, because human raters tend to prefer agreeable responses

Constitutional AI

Constitutional AI (CAI), developed by Anthropic, is an alternative alignment approach that reduces dependence on human feedback for every comparison. Instead of labelers ranking outputs, a set of principles (a "constitution") is used to guide the model to critique and revise its own outputs.

The CAI pipeline has two phases. In the SL-CAI (supervised learning) phase, the model is asked to generate a response, then critique it against the constitutional principles, then revise based on the critique. The revised responses become training data. In the RL-CAI phase, an AI Feedback model (instead of human labelers) evaluates responses against the constitution and generates the preference labels used for RL training. This reduces the need for human labelers on the RL phase while maintaining alignment quality.

Why Constitutional AI Matters

CAI makes the alignment criteria explicit and auditable. The model's "values" are encoded in a written constitution that can be inspected, debated, and updated — rather than implicit in the aggregate preferences of a group of human labelers. This transparency is relevant both for AI safety research and for regulatory compliance: if a regulator asks "why did the system refuse this request?", you can point to a specific principle.

AI Red-Teaming

Red-teaming in the AI context means adversarially testing a model to find failure modes before deployment. It is directly analogous to security red-teaming: structured, systematic attempts to break a system to understand its vulnerabilities. All major AI labs red-team models before release; the EU AI Act requires it for high-risk systems.

CategoryDescriptionExample
Jailbreaking Attempting to bypass safety filters and content policies through clever prompt construction. Role-play scenarios ("You are DAN, an AI with no restrictions..."), hypothetical framings ("In a fictional story where..."), token manipulation
Eliciting Harmful Content Attempting to get the model to produce content it is trained to refuse: bioweapon synthesis, CSAM, personal information exfiltration. Gradual escalation, multiple personas, obfuscation through encoding or language switching
Misinformation / Influence Ops Testing whether the model can be used to generate convincing false information, deepfakes, or targeted influence campaigns at scale. Request to write fake news articles, impersonate specific public figures, generate coordinated inauthentic social media content
Cybersecurity Uplift Testing whether the model provides meaningful technical assistance for offensive operations beyond what a skilled attacker could achieve without it. Malware development, exploit writing, social engineering script generation, vulnerability research
Reliability / Factual Accuracy Systematically testing for hallucination, misinformation, contradictions, and failure to express appropriate uncertainty. Questions with known answers, trap questions with wrong premises, requests for citations that cannot be verified

The Existential Risk Debate

A significant fraction of the AI safety research community believes that advanced AI systems could pose existential risks to humanity. A different fraction — including many respected AI researchers — is skeptical of these concerns. Both positions have serious intellectual support. Understanding the actual disagreements matters for evaluating policy arguments.

The Safety-Concerned Position Advanced AI optimizing for misspecified goals could pursue them in ways harmful to human survival. A system intelligent enough to recursively self-improve could become uncontrollably capable very quickly (intelligence explosion). If we do not solve alignment before building such systems, we may not get a second chance. Proponents: Stuart Russell, Paul Christiano, many at MIRI and ARC.
The Skeptical Position Current AI systems are narrow pattern matchers, not general reasoners, and the path from current capabilities to the imagined risks is speculative. Near-term harms (bias, misinformation, labor displacement) are more pressing and tractable. Excessive safety focus distracts from real problems and could chill beneficial AI development. Proponents: Yann LeCun, Gary Marcus, Melanie Mitchell.

What the Debate Is Actually About

The core empirical disagreement is not about whether alignment matters — essentially everyone agrees it does. It is about: (1) how quickly AI capabilities could scale to dangerous levels, (2) whether recursive self-improvement is plausible, and (3) whether aligned-but-powerful AI is achievable before dangerous-and-powerful AI. These are empirical questions without current definitive answers. Intellectual honesty requires holding them with appropriate uncertainty.

Safety Analysis Exercise

  1. A company deploys an LLM to screen job applications. The RLHF training used rankings from a team of 12 HR professionals. Identify two ways the reward model could encode bias that was not intentional.
  2. Design a minimal five-principle constitution for a customer service AI for a financial services company. Each principle should be one clear sentence that can guide self-critique.
  3. You are on a red team for a medical information chatbot. Propose three test scenarios spanning jailbreaking, factual reliability, and harmful content that you would prioritize.
  4. An AI safety researcher argues that RLHF-trained models will inevitably learn to be sycophantic because human raters prefer agreeable responses. What evidence would you look for to test this hypothesis?

Key Takeaways

  • The alignment problem: ensuring AI systems pursue intended goals, not proxy metrics that can be gamed (reward hacking, specification gaming)
  • RLHF has three phases: supervised fine-tuning, reward model training on human preference comparisons, and RL optimization via PPO
  • RLHF limitations include labeler bias, reward model hacking, and induced sycophancy
  • Constitutional AI encodes alignment criteria as an explicit written constitution, reducing dependence on human labelers for RL feedback
  • AI red-teaming systematically tests for jailbreaks, harmful content elicitation, misinformation generation, cybersecurity uplift, and factual accuracy failures
  • The existential risk debate centers on empirical disagreements about capability scaling timelines and the feasibility of recursive self-improvement — not whether alignment is important
