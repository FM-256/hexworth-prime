Why the Pi Cannot Drive Motors Directly
Understanding the electrical constraints of GPIO pins.
GPIO Limits
Each GPIO pin on the Pi supplies a maximum of 16mA at 3.3V. The total across all pins must not exceed 50mA. A small DC motor draws 200-500mA. A servo draws 500-1500mA. Connecting a motor directly to GPIO will damage the Pi.
The Solution: Motor Drivers
A motor driver is a power amplifier. The Pi sends low-current control signals (direction, speed via PWM). The motor driver uses an external power supply (battery pack) to deliver high current to the motor. Electrical isolation protects the Pi.
Back-EMF
Motors generate voltage spikes when they stop or reverse (back-EMF). These spikes can travel back through the driver and damage the Pi. Motor driver boards include flyback diodes to absorb these spikes. Never skip the driver board.

DC Motors
Continuous rotation motors controlled by voltage polarity and PWM.
How They Work
Apply voltage and the motor spins. Reverse the voltage polarity and it spins the opposite direction. Speed is proportional to voltage. No position feedback -- they spin freely until power is removed. Cheap, simple, high-speed.
Speed Control via PWM
Pulse Width Modulation rapidly switches the motor on and off. 50% duty cycle = half speed. 100% = full speed. The motor's inertia smooths the pulses into continuous rotation. Pi GPIO can generate software PWM; hardware PWM (GPIO 12, 13, 18, 19) is more precise.
Common DC Motors for Pi Robots
TT motors (yellow gearbox motors) are the standard for hobby robotics. 3-6V, ~200mA no-load, ~800mA stall current. Built-in gear reduction provides usable torque at reasonable speeds. ~$2 each.

The H-Bridge
The circuit that controls motor direction by switching voltage polarity.
Q1-Q4Four transistors (switches) arranged in an H pattern. The motor sits in the crossbar of the H. By closing different pairs of transistors, current flows through the motor in either direction.
FWDForward: Close Q1 (top-left) and Q4 (bottom-right). Current flows left-to-right through the motor. Q2 and Q3 are open.
REVReverse: Close Q3 (top-right) and Q2 (bottom-left). Current flows right-to-left through the motor. Q1 and Q4 are open.
BRKBrake: Close Q1 and Q3 (both top) or Q2 and Q4 (both bottom). Motor terminals are shorted -- back-EMF decelerates the motor quickly.
CSTCoast: All transistors open. No current flows. Motor spins freely on its own inertia until friction stops it.
Shoot-Through
Never close Q1+Q2 or Q3+Q4 simultaneously. This creates a direct short from power to ground through the transistors -- called shoot-through. The resulting current spike destroys the transistors instantly. Motor driver ICs have built-in shoot-through protection.

Motor Driver Boards
Common H-bridge ICs and driver boards for Pi robots.
L298N
Dual H-bridge, 2A per channel. 5-35V motor voltage. Older design with high voltage drop (2-3V wasted as heat). Cheap and widely available. Good for learning, not efficient for battery-powered robots.
TB6612FNG
Dual H-bridge, 1.2A continuous (3.2A peak). MOSFET-based with very low voltage drop (~0.5V). Much more efficient than L298N for battery-powered projects. Used in Adafruit and Pololu breakout boards.
DRV8833
Dual H-bridge, 1.5A per channel. 2.7-10.8V motor voltage. Ultra-compact. Built-in current limiting and thermal shutdown. Excellent for small robots with TT motors. ~$3 on breakout boards.
Choosing a Driver
Match the driver to your motor's stall current (not no-load current). TT motors stall at ~800mA, so a 1.2A driver works. Larger motors may stall at 3-5A and need beefier drivers like the BTS7960 (43A continuous).

Servo Motors
Position-controlled motors with built-in feedback.
Standard Servo (180 degrees)
Rotates to a specific angle based on PWM pulse width. 1ms pulse = 0 degrees. 1.5ms = 90 degrees. 2ms = 180 degrees. Pulse frequency is 50Hz (20ms period). Internal potentiometer provides position feedback to a control circuit.
Continuous Rotation Servo
Modified servo where the potentiometer feedback is disabled. Pulse width controls speed and direction instead of position. 1.5ms = stop. Less than 1.5ms = one direction. Greater than 1.5ms = other direction. Useful as geared motors with easy Pi control.
# Control a servo with Pi hardware PWM (gpiozero) from gpiozero import Servo from time import sleep servo = Servo(17) # GPIO 17 servo.min() # 0 degrees sleep(1) servo.mid() # 90 degrees sleep(1) servo.max() # 180 degrees

Stepper Motors
Precise rotational control in discrete angular steps.
How They Work
The motor has multiple electromagnetic coils arranged around a toothed rotor. Energizing coils in sequence rotates the rotor one step at a time. A common NEMA 17 stepper has 200 steps per revolution (1.8 degrees per step). No feedback needed -- position is known by counting steps.
Microstepping
Instead of fully energizing one coil at a time, microstepping applies proportional current to adjacent coils. 1/16 microstepping divides each step into 16 microsteps: 3,200 positions per revolution (0.1125 degrees). Smoother motion but lower torque per step.
Stepper vs. DC vs. Servo
Steppers excel at precise positioning (CNC machines, 3D printers) but are heavy, power-hungry, and slow. DC motors are fast and efficient but have no position feedback. Servos have feedback but limited rotation range. Choose based on your project's primary requirement: speed, position, or torque.

Encoders -- Adding Feedback to DC Motors
Turning a dumb DC motor into a precision actuator.
Optical Encoder
A slotted disc attached to the motor shaft passes between an LED and photodetector. Each slot generates a pulse. Count pulses to measure distance. Measure pulse frequency to calculate speed. 20-slot disc on a TT motor gives 20 pulses per wheel revolution.
Quadrature Encoder
Two sensors offset by 90 degrees generate overlapping pulse trains (Channel A and B). By comparing which channel leads, you can determine rotation direction. Doubles resolution compared to single-channel. Essential for bidirectional position tracking.
PID Control
With encoder feedback, a PID (Proportional-Integral-Derivative) controller can maintain precise speed. The Pi reads encoder pulses, calculates the error between desired and actual speed, and adjusts PWM duty cycle. This is how differential-drive robots maintain straight-line travel despite unmatched motors.

Differential Drive
The most common locomotion model for Pi robots.
FWDForward: Both motors at equal speed, same direction. Robot drives straight (in theory -- motor matching determines actual straightness).
REVReverse: Both motors at equal speed, opposite direction from forward. Robot drives backward.
LEFTPivot left: Stop left motor, run right motor forward. Robot turns left around the stopped wheel. Turning radius equals the wheelbase width.
SPINSpin in place: Left motor forward, right motor reverse (or vice versa) at equal speed. Robot rotates around its center point. Zero turning radius.
ARCArc turn: Both motors forward but at different speeds. The slower motor traces the inner arc. Speed ratio determines the turn radius. This is how smooth curved paths are achieved.

Line Following
An IR reflectance sensor array guides the robot along a dark line on a light surface.
Sensor Array
TCRT5000 IR reflectance sensors. Each contains an IR LED and photodiode pair. Dark surfaces absorb IR (low reflection = line detected). Light surfaces reflect IR (high reflection = no line). 3-5 sensors in a row provide position resolution.
Control Algorithm
Read sensor array. Calculate line position as a weighted average. If line is centered, drive straight. If line drifts left, speed up right motor (or slow left). PID controller smooths corrections. The proportional term handles current error; derivative term prevents overshoot.
# Simplified line-following logic # sensors: [left, center-left, center, center-right, right] # 1 = line detected, 0 = no line position = (-2*s[0] + -1*s[1] + 0*s[2] + 1*s[3] + 2*s[4]) / sum(s) error = 0 - position # target = center correction = Kp * error + Kd * (error - prev_error) left_speed = base_speed + correction right_speed = base_speed - correction

Pi Robotics Software Stack
Libraries and frameworks for building Pi-based robots.
gpiozero
Python library with high-level motor abstractions. Motor(), Servo(), Robot() classes. Two-line motor control. Built into Pi OS. Best for beginners and rapid prototyping.
pigpio
Low-level GPIO library with hardware-timed PWM. Microsecond precision. Supports remote GPIO (control one Pi's pins from another). Better servo jitter performance than software PWM.
ROS 2 (Robot Operating System)
Full robotics middleware framework. Publisher-subscriber messaging, sensor fusion, path planning, SLAM. Massive ecosystem but heavy -- Pi 5 with 8GB RAM recommended. Used in research and commercial robotics.
Choosing a Stack
Start with gpiozero for learning motor control fundamentals. Move to pigpio when you need precise PWM timing (servo jitter, encoder reading). Graduate to ROS 2 only when your project needs sensor fusion, mapping, or multi-robot coordination.

Key Takeaways: Motor Control & Robotics
The five facts that matter.
1Never connect motors directly to GPIO. Pi GPIO supplies 16mA max; motors draw 200-1500mA. Always use a motor driver board (L298N, TB6612FNG, DRV8833) with a separate power supply.
2H-bridges control direction by switching voltage polarity. Four transistors in an H configuration. PWM on the enable pin controls speed. Shoot-through (both top or both bottom transistors on) must be prevented.
3Three motor types serve different needs. DC motors: speed and simplicity. Servos: angular positioning (180 degrees). Steppers: precise multi-revolution positioning without feedback.
4Differential drive is the standard Pi robot model. Two independently driven wheels plus a caster. Speed difference between wheels controls turning radius. Spin-in-place requires opposite motor directions.
5Line following combines IR sensors with PID control. Sensor array detects line position, PID controller adjusts motor speeds to center the robot on the line. Proportional term corrects error; derivative term prevents overshoot.
