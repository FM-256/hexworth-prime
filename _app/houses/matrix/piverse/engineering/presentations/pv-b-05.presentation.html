Signal Integrity: Clean Signals, Clean Data

At low frequencies, a wire is just a wire. At high frequencies, a wire is a transmission line with impedance, reflections, and radiation. The RP2040's USB runs at 12 Mbps and QSPI flash at 50+ MHz. Signal integrity determines whether those signals arrive clean or corrupted.

When Does Signal Integrity Matter?
The rule of thumb: when the trace length exceeds 1/10 of the signal's wavelength.
Low Frequency (No Concern)
A GPIO toggling at 1 kHz has a wavelength of 300 km. Your 50mm trace is 0.00002% of the wavelength. Signal integrity is irrelevant. The wire is just a wire.
High Frequency (Critical)
USB Full Speed at 12 MHz has a wavelength of 25m. QSPI at 50 MHz = 6m. The edge rate matters more than the frequency -- a 1ns rise time means wavelength of 300mm. Your trace IS a transmission line.
RP2040 Signals That Need Care
USB D+/D- (12 Mbps, tight impedance requirement). QSPI flash (4 data lines + clock at 50 MHz). SWD debug (up to 24 MHz). PIO outputs driving fast protocols. Everything else -- GPIO, UART at 115200, I2C at 400 kHz -- is fine with basic routing.

Impedance and Transmission Lines
A PCB trace over a ground plane forms a transmission line with a characteristic impedance.
Characteristic Impedance (Z0)
Determined by trace width, copper thickness, distance to ground plane, and dielectric constant of FR-4 (usually 4.2-4.5). USB requires 90 ohm differential impedance. Most general signals target 50 ohm single-ended.
Impedance Mismatch = Reflections
If the trace impedance does not match the driver/receiver impedance, the signal reflects back like an echo. Reflections cause ringing, overshoot, and data corruption. Matching eliminates reflections.
Calculating Trace Impedance
Use a trace impedance calculator (Saturn PCB Toolkit is free). Input: trace width, copper weight, dielectric height (distance to ground plane), and Er (4.3 for standard FR-4). For JLCPCB 4-layer stackup, an 8 mil trace over 7.7 mil dielectric gives approximately 50 ohm single-ended impedance.

USB Differential Routing
USB D+ and D- form a differential pair. They must be routed together with controlled impedance.
190 ohm differential impedance. The USB 2.0 spec requires 90 ohm +/- 10%. This determines trace width and spacing for your specific stackup. Typically 8-10 mil traces with 6-8 mil gap on a 4-layer board.
2Length matching. D+ and D- must be the same length within 2mm (150 mil). If one trace is longer, add a small serpentine to the shorter one. KiCad's differential pair router handles this automatically.
327-ohm series resistors. The RP2040 requires 27-ohm resistors on D+ and D-, placed close to the chip. These match the output impedance of the USB PHY to the trace impedance.
4Keep the pair together. Do not separate D+ and D- around obstacles. Route them as a unit. Do not change layers mid-pair (the via discontinuity disrupts impedance).

Crosstalk
Crosstalk is unwanted coupling between adjacent traces. A signal on one trace induces a signal on its neighbor.
Capacitive Crosstalk
Electric field coupling between traces. Proportional to the parallel run length and inversely proportional to the spacing. Dominates at high impedance (slow edges).
Inductive Crosstalk
Magnetic field coupling between traces. Also proportional to parallel length and inversely proportional to spacing. Dominates at low impedance (fast edges).
The 3W Rule
Space traces at 3x the trace width center-to-center to reduce crosstalk by 70%. For critical signals (USB, high-speed SPI clock), use wider spacing. Never run a fast clock trace parallel to a sensitive analog trace for more than a few millimeters.

EMI: Electromagnetic Interference
Every current loop is an antenna. EMI is the radiation from those antennas.
Current Loops
A signal travels out on a trace and returns through the ground plane. The area enclosed by this loop determines how much it radiates. Smaller loop = less EMI. This is why ground planes matter.
Ground Plane Splits
If the ground plane has a gap under a signal trace, the return current must detour around it. The enlarged loop area radiates more. This is the primary cause of EMI failures in PCB designs.
Practical EMI Reduction
Continuous ground plane (no splits). Short traces for high-frequency signals. Decoupling caps close to IC power pins (they reduce the AC current loop through the power supply). Series resistors on outputs slow down edges, reducing harmonic content. Shielding (metal can) as a last resort.

QSPI Flash Routing
The flash interface runs at high speed. Trace routing directly affects reliability.
1Short traces. Keep flash chip within 10-15mm of the RP2040. Shorter traces have lower inductance and less signal degradation.
2Length matching. All 4 data lines (SD0-SD3) and the clock (SCLK) should be matched within 5mm. The flash reads all data lines simultaneously on the clock edge -- length mismatch causes setup/hold violations.
3Ground plane underneath. Route all QSPI traces over continuous ground. Do not route them over a split or gap in the ground plane.
4100nF decoupling on flash VCC. Place it between the flash chip's VCC pin and ground, within 2mm. Flash switching transients must be locally decoupled.

Return Paths
Every signal has a return path. At DC, current returns through the shortest conductor. At AC, current returns through the path of least inductance -- directly under the signal trace.
Ground Plane Return
High-frequency return current flows in the ground plane directly beneath the signal trace. This is why signals must always have a continuous ground reference underneath them. Break the ground and you break the return path.
Layer Changes and Stitching Vias
When a signal changes layers via a via, the return current must also change layers. Place a ground via (stitching via) next to every signal via to provide a return path. Without it, the return current detours and creates EMI.
Via Stitching
Sprinkle extra ground vias around the board, especially near signal vias and along board edges. These "stitching vias" connect the top and bottom ground pours, keeping the ground impedance low and providing return paths everywhere they are needed.

Series Termination
A series resistor at the source absorbs reflections and slows edge rates.
How It Works
The resistor value = Z0 minus the driver output impedance. For a 50-ohm trace with a 10-ohm driver, use a 39-ohm series resistor. The signal travels half-amplitude to the far end, reflects, and adds to full amplitude. Clean, ringing-free.
RP2040 USB Resistors
The 27-ohm series resistors on USB D+/D- serve exactly this purpose. The RP2040 USB PHY output impedance plus 27 ohms matches the 90-ohm differential pair impedance. Without them, USB communication is unreliable.
Slide 10 of 12
Crystal Layout
The 12 MHz crystal is a high-impedance, noise-sensitive component. Bad layout causes clock failure.
1Place crystal within 5mm of the RP2040. Short traces minimize parasitic capacitance and inductance that detune the oscillator.
2Ground guard ring. Surround the crystal and load caps with a ground pour on the same layer. This shields the high-impedance oscillator circuit from nearby switching noise.
3No signal traces underneath. Do not route any traces under the crystal on any layer. The coupling can inject noise into the oscillator.
4Load caps next to crystal pads. The two 15pF load capacitors connect directly from the crystal pads to ground. Place them within 2mm of the crystal.

Controlled Impedance Ordering
When ordering your PCB, you must specify impedance requirements to the fab house.
Impedance Specification
Tell the fab: "90 ohm differential for USB pair on Layer 1, referenced to Layer 2 ground." They adjust the dielectric thickness to hit your target. JLCPCB supports controlled impedance on 4-layer boards for a small surcharge.
Stackup Confirmation
The fab provides a stackup document showing actual layer thicknesses. Recalculate your trace widths against the actual stackup before finalizing Gerbers. The target width changes if the dielectric height changes.
Do You Need Controlled Impedance?
For USB Full Speed (12 Mbps) on a short trace (under 50mm), you can often get away without formal impedance control. But for USB High Speed (480 Mbps), long USB cables, or SDIO interfaces, controlled impedance is mandatory. When in doubt, specify it -- the cost increase is minimal.

Key Takeaways: Signal Integrity
Clean signals start with understanding physics. Dirty signals start with ignoring it.
1Signal integrity matters above ~10 MHz. USB, QSPI flash, and fast PIO outputs all need controlled routing. GPIO and UART do not.
2USB requires 90-ohm differential impedance. Matched length, 27-ohm series resistors, routed as a pair, no layer changes.
3Crosstalk: 3W rule. Space traces at 3x width center-to-center. Keep fast signals away from sensitive analog.
4Ground plane continuity is everything. Unbroken ground plane under all signals. Stitching vias at every layer change.
5Crystal layout: short, shielded, isolated. Within 5mm, ground guard, no traces underneath.
