PCB Anatomy: Layers, Traces, and Planes

A PCB is a sandwich of copper and fiberglass, precisely engineered to carry signals and power between components. Understanding the anatomy of this sandwich is the foundation of every board you will ever design.

What is a PCB?
A Printed Circuit Board replaces breadboard wires with permanent, reliable copper traces on a rigid substrate.
FR-4 Substrate
Fiberglass-reinforced epoxy laminate. The most common PCB material. Cheap, strong, and good enough for frequencies up to several GHz. Green color comes from the solder mask, not the substrate.
Copper Layers
Thin copper foil (typically 1 oz/ft2 = 35 um) laminated onto the FR-4. Copper is etched away to leave only the traces and pads you designed. More layers = more routing flexibility.
Layer Count
A 2-layer board has copper on top and bottom. A 4-layer board adds two internal copper layers. The Pico is a 4-layer board. Most hobby projects use 2 layers. Production boards use 4-8 layers. Server boards can exceed 20 layers.

Copper Traces
Traces are the wires of the PCB. Their width determines how much current they can carry.
Width Matters
A 0.15mm (6 mil) trace carries about 0.3A on the outer layer. A 0.5mm (20 mil) trace carries about 1A. Power traces need to be wider than signal traces.
Thickness
Standard 1 oz copper is 35 micrometers thick. 2 oz copper (70 um) doubles current capacity. Heavier copper costs more and is harder to etch fine features.
Spacing
Minimum spacing between traces depends on voltage and manufacturer capability. Typical: 0.15mm (6 mil) for low voltage. High voltage needs wider gaps to prevent arcing.
Design Rule: Power vs Signal
Signal traces (GPIO, UART, SPI) can be thin (6-8 mil). Power traces (VCC, GND) should be wide (20-50 mil) or use copper pours. USB differential pairs need specific widths for impedance control (typically 8-10 mil with specific spacing).

Vias: Connecting Layers
A via is a plated hole that connects copper on one layer to copper on another layer.
Through-Hole Via
Drilled completely through the board. Connects all layers. Most common type. Typical drill size: 0.3mm hole, 0.6mm pad. Cheap to manufacture.
Blind Via
Connects an outer layer to an inner layer without going all the way through. More expensive. Used in high-density designs.
Buried Via
Connects two internal layers only. Not visible from outside. Most expensive. Used in advanced designs with 6+ layers.
Via-in-Pad
Placing a via directly in a component pad saves space but requires the via to be filled and plated over. JLCPCB and most fabs support this for a small surcharge. Essential for QFN packages like the RP2040 where the ground pad needs vias for thermal relief.

Ground Planes
A ground plane is an entire copper layer (or large pour) dedicated to ground. It is the single most important feature of a well-designed PCB.
Signal Return Path
Every signal needs a return path. A continuous ground plane provides the shortest, lowest-inductance return path directly beneath each signal trace. This minimizes EMI and crosstalk.
Thermal Dissipation
Large copper areas spread heat. The RP2040's exposed ground pad on the bottom of the QFN package connects to the ground plane through thermal vias, conducting heat into the board.
Rule: Never Split the Ground Plane
Routing signal traces on the ground plane layer creates splits. A split ground plane forces return currents to take longer paths, creating antenna loops that radiate EMI. On a 4-layer board, keep one entire inner layer as an unbroken ground plane.

Board Stackup
The stackup defines the order and thickness of copper and dielectric layers.
L1Top Copper (Signal): Components and signal traces. Most routing happens here.
L2Inner 1 (Ground Plane): Continuous ground pour. Signal return path. Do not route signals here.
L3Inner 2 (Power Plane): 3.3V pour, or split between 3.3V and other rails. Some signal routing if needed.
L4Bottom Copper (Signal): Additional signal routing, ground pour fill, and some components (in double-sided assembly).
Why 4 Layers for RP2040?
The RP2040 QFN-56 package has pins on all four sides plus a ground pad underneath. A 2-layer board makes routing extremely difficult. The 4-layer stackup with dedicated ground and power planes gives you clean power delivery, good signal integrity, and room to route all 30 GPIO pins.

Solder Mask and Silkscreen
Two non-copper layers that protect the board and communicate component placement.
Solder Mask
The colored coating (usually green) that covers all copper except pads and vias. Prevents solder bridges between closely-spaced pads during assembly. Also protects copper from oxidation and environmental damage.
Silkscreen
White (or other color) ink printed on the solder mask. Shows reference designators (R1, C1, U1), component outlines, pin 1 markers, board name, version number, and orientation markers.
Colors Are Cosmetic
Green solder mask is traditional but any color works: black, white, red, blue, purple, matte black. JLCPCB charges extra for non-green. The Raspberry Pi Pico uses green. Your custom boards can use whatever you like.

Component Footprints
A footprint is the physical pad pattern on the PCB that a component solders to.
Through-Hole (THT)
Pins go through holes in the board and are soldered on the opposite side. Larger, easier to solder by hand. Used for connectors, large capacitors, and pin headers.
Surface Mount (SMD)
Pads sit flat on the surface. Components are placed on top and soldered with reflow. Smaller, cheaper for mass production. 0402, 0603, 0805 are common resistor/cap sizes.
QFN (RP2040)
Quad Flat No-lead. Pads on all four edges plus an exposed ground pad underneath. The RP2040 is QFN-56: 56 pads, 7mm x 7mm. Requires reflow soldering.

Copper Pours and Fills
Large areas of copper that fill unused board space. Essential for power distribution and EMI control.
Ground Pour (Fill)
Fill unused areas on the top and bottom layers with copper connected to GND. This extends the ground plane, reduces EMI, and improves thermal performance. Always use ground pours unless you have a specific reason not to.
Thermal Relief
Where a pad connects to a copper pour, a thermal relief pattern (spoked connection) is used instead of a direct solid connection. This makes hand soldering possible -- a pad directly connected to a huge copper pour would wick away heat too fast to solder.
Pour Order Matters
In KiCad and other EDA tools, copper pours have priority numbers. Higher-priority pours fill first. GND pours typically have the lowest priority so they fill around everything else. Always re-pour (rebuild) fills before generating Gerber files.

Board Outline and Mounting
The edge cuts layer defines the physical shape of your board.
Edge Cuts
A closed polygon on the Edge.Cuts layer defines the board outline. Can be rectangular, rounded, or any custom shape. The router at the fab house follows this line to cut your board out of the panel.
Mounting Holes
Non-plated holes for screws. Typically M2.5 or M3. Place them at corners, inset by at least 3mm from the board edge. Connect to ground plane if using metal standoffs for grounding.
Keep-Out Zones
Nothing should be placed within 0.5mm of the board edge (copper, components, or vias). This is the fabrication clearance. Also keep components clear of mounting holes -- the screw head needs clearance. Define keep-out zones in your EDA tool to enforce this.
Slide 11 of 12
Design Rule Check (DRC)
The DRC is your last line of defense before sending the board to fabrication.
1Clearance violations: Traces or pads too close to each other. The fab cannot reliably etch copper below its minimum clearance (typically 0.15mm for JLCPCB).
2Unconnected nets: A net in the schematic that is not fully routed on the PCB. Every connection in the netlist must have a physical copper path.
3Minimum trace width: Below 0.1mm (4 mil), most budget fabs cannot reliably produce traces. Standard minimum is 0.15mm (6 mil).
4Drill size violations: Minimum drill is typically 0.2mm for mechanical, 0.3mm for vias. Smaller drills cost more and break more easily.
5Courtyard overlaps: Component courtyards define the physical space a component occupies including clearance. Overlaps mean components will physically collide.

Key Takeaways: PCB Anatomy
You now understand the physical structure of every PCB. Next: powering it.
1A PCB is layered copper and FR-4. 2-layer for simple, 4-layer for RP2040 designs. Each layer serves a purpose: signals, ground, power.
2Trace width determines current capacity. Thin for signals (6 mil), wide for power (20+ mil). Use copper pours for high-current paths.
3Vias connect layers. Through-hole vias are cheapest. Via-in-pad is essential for QFN packages like the RP2040.
4Ground planes are non-negotiable. Continuous, unbroken ground plane on an inner layer. Never split it with signal routing.
5Always run DRC before fabrication. Clearance, unconnected nets, minimum widths -- the tool catches what your eyes miss.
