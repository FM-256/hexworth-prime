RP2040 Power Requirements
The RP2040 needs two voltage rails: 3.3V for I/O and an internal 1.1V for the digital core.
IOVDD (3.3V)
Powers GPIO, USB PHY, ADC, and pad logic. All IOVDD pins must connect to the same 3.3V rail. Current draw varies with GPIO activity: ~5-50mA typical.
DVDD (1.1V)
Powers the digital core (CPU, SRAM, PIO). Generated internally by the RP2040's built-in LDO from IOVDD. You supply the input; the chip handles the rest.
USB_VDD (3.3V)
Separate power pin for the USB PHY. Connect to the same 3.3V rail but with its own decoupling cap. Noisy USB transients should not propagate to the core.
Total Power Budget
At 125 MHz with both cores active, typical current draw is 40-60mA from 3.3V. Add GPIO loads, LEDs, sensors, and you may need 100-300mA. Size your regulator for peak load plus 20% margin.

LDO vs Switching Regulators
Two fundamentally different approaches to voltage regulation. Each has trade-offs.
LDO (Linear)
Simple: input voltage in, lower output voltage out. Excess energy burned as heat. Efficiency = Vout/Vin. Example: 5V to 3.3V = 66% efficiency. Silent (no switching noise). Cheap. Small. Best when Vin is close to Vout.
Buck (Switching)
Chops the input voltage with a transistor switch, filters with an inductor and capacitor. 85-95% efficient regardless of Vin/Vout ratio. But generates switching noise at the switching frequency (typically 500kHz-2MHz). Needs inductor and output caps.
The Pico's Choice
The Pico uses an RT6150B buck-boost converter (U2). "Buck-boost" means it can step voltage up OR down: input 1.8-5.5V, output 3.3V. This lets the Pico run from USB (5V), LiPo battery (3.0-4.2V), or two AA batteries (2.4-3.0V). The trade-off is complexity -- L1, C5-C7, R5, R6 are all part of the converter circuit.

Decoupling Capacitors
The most important passive components on your board. Every power pin needs decoupling.
100nF Ceramic (Per Pin)
One 100nF (0.1uF) ceramic capacitor on EVERY VDD pin, placed as close to the pin as physically possible. This filters high-frequency noise (10-100MHz range). Non-negotiable.
10uF Bulk (Per Rail)
One or two 10uF capacitors near the voltage regulator output. Provides bulk charge storage for sudden current demands. Stabilizes the rail during load transients.
1uF ADC Reference
The RP2040 ADC reference pin (ADC_AVDD) needs a 1uF cap for noise filtering. ADC accuracy depends directly on power supply cleanliness. A noisy reference means noisy readings.
Placement is Everything
A decoupling cap 10mm away from the pin it serves is almost useless. The inductance of the trace connecting them defeats the purpose. Place caps within 2mm of the pin. Route directly from the pad to the via, then to the cap. The shortest path wins.

Power Input Protection
Protect your board from reverse voltage, overvoltage, and inrush current.
Reverse Polarity
A Schottky diode in series (like D1 on the Pico) prevents damage if power is connected backwards. Drops about 0.3V. A P-channel MOSFET is more efficient (millivolts of drop).
Overvoltage (TVS)
A TVS diode clamps voltage spikes. For a 5V USB input, a 5.5V TVS will clamp anything above that to protect downstream components.
Inrush Current
Large capacitors on the output cause a surge when power is first applied. A soft-start circuit or NTC thermistor limits the initial current spike. Important for battery-powered designs.

Power Sequencing
Some chips require power rails to come up in a specific order. The RP2040 is forgiving, but others are not.
RP2040 Sequencing
The RP2040 generates its own 1.1V core voltage from 3.3V via an internal LDO. No external sequencing needed. Apply 3.3V and the chip handles the rest. The POR (Power-On Reset) circuit holds the chip in reset until voltages stabilize.
When Sequencing Matters
FPGAs, DDR memory, and multi-rail SoCs often require precise power sequencing: core voltage first, then I/O voltage, then aux. Wrong order can cause latch-up (destructive short circuit inside the chip).
3V3_EN Pin on the Pico
The Pico exposes a 3V3_EN pin. Pull it low to disable the 3.3V regulator and put the entire board to sleep. This is useful for ultra-low-power battery designs: an external RTC or sensor can wake the board by releasing 3V3_EN.

Thermal Management
Every watt dissipated becomes heat. Manage it or your board overheats.
LDO Heat
An LDO dropping 5V to 3.3V at 200mA dissipates (5-3.3) x 0.2 = 0.34W. That is enough to heat a SOT-23 package to 80+ degrees C without adequate copper area for heat spreading.
Thermal Vias
Vias under the regulator's thermal pad connect to internal ground planes, spreading heat through the board. The RP2040's QFN ground pad also needs thermal vias -- typically a 3x3 or 4x4 array of 0.3mm vias.
Rule of Thumb
If your regulator dissipates more than 0.5W, consider a switching converter instead. If it dissipates more than 1W, you MUST use a switching converter or add a heatsink. Calculate: P = (Vin - Vout) x Iload. This is the waste heat equation for linear regulators.

Battery Power Design
Running the RP2040 from a LiPo battery introduces new design considerations.
LiPo Voltage Range
A single-cell LiPo varies from 4.2V (fully charged) to 3.0V (empty). A buck-boost converter maintains 3.3V output across this entire range. An LDO only works above 3.3V + dropout.
Charge Controller
A TP4056 or MCP73831 IC manages safe LiPo charging from USB. It handles constant-current, constant-voltage charging and terminates when full. Essential safety component.
Load Sharing
When USB is connected, the board should run from USB power while charging the battery. A Schottky diode OR-ing circuit or a dedicated load-sharing IC handles this. The Pico's VSYS diode (D1) provides basic OR-ing between USB and external power.

Power Layout Best Practices
How you route power on the PCB matters as much as the schematic design.
1Wide traces or pours for power. Minimum 20 mil for 3.3V distribution. Use copper pours connected to the power plane for maximum current capacity and minimum voltage drop.
2Star topology for sensitive analog. Route ADC power separately from digital power. They connect at one point (the regulator output) to prevent digital noise from coupling into analog measurements.
3Input caps before output caps. Place the regulator's input capacitor close to its input pin. Place output capacitors close to its output pin. The inductor goes between.
4Short, direct paths. The loop area formed by the switching node, inductor, output cap, and ground should be as small as possible. Large loops radiate EMI.

Measuring Power Quality
How to verify your power design works after the board is built.
Multimeter: DC Voltage
Verify 3.3V rail reads 3.3V +/- 3%. Check under load (all peripherals active) -- if voltage drops below 3.1V, your regulator is undersized or your traces have too much resistance.
Oscilloscope: Ripple
Probe the 3.3V rail with AC coupling. Switching converters produce ripple at the switching frequency. Acceptable: under 50mV peak-to-peak. Over 100mV: add more output capacitance or check layout.
Test Points
Add test points (TP) on your PCB for every power rail. A test point is a small exposed pad that you can touch with a probe tip. Label them: TP_3V3, TP_VBUS, TP_GND. They cost nothing and save hours of debugging.

Common Power Mistakes
Errors that cause boards to fail or behave erratically.
XMissing decoupling caps. The RP2040 has multiple VDD pins. Missing even one decoupling cap can cause random crashes, USB dropouts, or ADC noise.
XDecoupling caps too far from pins. A cap 10mm away on a thin trace adds inductance that negates the benefit. Place within 2mm, route directly.
XWrong regulator for the job. Using an LDO for 12V to 3.3V at 300mA = 2.6W of waste heat. Use a buck converter instead.
XNo bulk capacitance. Only 100nF per pin but no 10uF bulk cap. The rail sags during current spikes (motor start, LED strip update).
XGround plane splits under the regulator. The switching converter's return current cannot flow through the ground plane, creating EMI.

Key Takeaways: Power System Design
Clean power is invisible. Bad power destroys everything downstream.
1LDO for simplicity, buck for efficiency. Use LDO when Vin is close to Vout. Use switching when the difference is large or efficiency matters (battery designs).
2100nF on every VDD pin. Placed within 2mm. No exceptions. Plus 10uF bulk cap per rail near the regulator.
3Protection circuits prevent destruction. Reverse polarity diode, TVS for ESD, soft-start for inrush. Design defensively.
4Thermal budget: P = (Vin-Vout) x I. If your LDO dissipates more than 0.5W, switch to a buck converter.
5Test points on every rail. Free to add, invaluable for debugging. You will thank yourself later.
