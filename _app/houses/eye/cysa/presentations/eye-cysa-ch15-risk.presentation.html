CHAPTER 15 — PRESENTATION

Risk Management

Quantifying, prioritizing, and managing cybersecurity risk — CIA triad, threat/vulnerability relationships, quantitative and qualitative analysis, BIA, risk treatment strategies, data classification, and technical controls.

Slide 1 — Cybersecurity Objectives: The CIA Triad

The Foundation of Security Thinking

Every risk, attack, and security control can be characterized by its relationship to one or more of the three foundational security objectives. Understanding which objective is threatened or protected guides control selection and risk prioritization.

Confidentiality

Protect data against unauthorized access. Only authorized parties should be able to read sensitive information. Threats: unauthorized disclosure, data exfiltration, eavesdropping, social engineering. Controls: encryption (in transit and at rest), access controls, DLP, data classification.

Integrity

No unauthorized modifications. Data should be accurate, complete, and trustworthy. Threats: unauthorized modification, ransomware, SQL injection, man-in-the-middle. Controls: digital signatures, hashing, change control, write protection, input validation.

Availability

Meet the needs of legitimate users — systems and data must be accessible when and how authorized users need them. Threats to availability include denial-of-service attacks, ransomware, hardware failure, and natural disasters. Controls include redundancy, load balancing, business continuity planning, and backup strategies.

Risk analysis always considers which objective is threatened. A SQL injection attack that exfiltrates a database is a confidentiality breach. The same attack that corrupts the database is an integrity breach. A DDoS attack is an availability breach. Understanding the threat type determines the appropriate defensive posture.

Slide 2 — Evaluating Security Risks

Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Risk

Vulnerabilities are weaknesses in devices, systems, applications, or processes. They are internal factors — things that exist within or about the organization. An unpatched operating system, weak password policy, or misconfigured firewall rule are all vulnerabilities.

Threats are outside forces that may exploit a vulnerability. Threat actors — nation-states, criminal organizations, disgruntled insiders, script kiddies — are the agents. Natural events (floods, power outages) are also threat sources.

Risk occurs at the intersection of threats and vulnerabilities. A vulnerability with no threat actor capable or motivated to exploit it carries low risk. A critical vulnerability being actively exploited in the wild carries very high risk.

Risk Severity = Probability × Magnitude

Probability: likelihood the threat will materialize | Magnitude: impact on the organization if it does

Slide 3 — Quantitative Risk Assessment

Putting Numbers on Risk

Quantitative risk assessment uses numerical data to calculate risk in financial terms. While more objective than qualitative methods, it requires accurate data — asset values, historical incident frequencies, and loss estimates — that are often difficult to obtain precisely. For the CySA+ exam, know these formulas and be able to apply them to scenarios.

Asset Value (AV)

The dollar value of the asset. Can be acquisition cost, replacement cost, or depreciated value. For data assets: may include regulatory fine exposure, reputational damage, and business impact.

Exposure Factor (EF)

The percentage of the asset expected to be damaged if a risk materializes. A fire that destroys half a data center has an EF of 50%. Total destruction = EF of 100%.

Single Loss Expectancy (SLE)

SLE = AV × EF. The financial damage expected each time the risk occurs. Example: $100,000 server × 90% damage = $90,000 SLE.

Annualized Rate of Occurrence (ARO)

How often the risk is expected to materialize per year. Once per year = ARO of 1.0. Once every 10 years = ARO of 0.1. Three times per year = ARO of 3.

ALE = SLE × ARO

Annualized Loss Expectancy — the expected financial loss from this risk per year

Worked Example: Email Server DoS Attack

An email server generates $1,000 in sales revenue per hour. A DoS attack is expected to occur 3 times per year, lasting 3 hours each time. During the attack, the server operates at 10% capacity.

EXAM APPLICATION

The ALE drives the cost-benefit analysis for security controls. A DDoS mitigation service costing $5,000/year is justified against an ALE of $8,100 — it costs less than the expected annual loss it prevents. A $15,000/year solution is not justified by the numbers alone (though qualitative factors like reputational damage might still support it). This is how CISOs justify security budgets to boards who think in dollar terms, not threat levels.

Slide 4 — Qualitative Risk Assessment

When Numbers Are Not Available

Qualitative risk assessments substitute subjective judgments and categorical ratings for strict numerical analysis. Useful when precise asset values or occurrence rates are unavailable, when assessing risks that are difficult to quantify (reputational damage, regulatory risk), or when speed matters more than precision.

Typical qualitative approach: rate probability and impact on a scale (Low/Medium/High or 1-5) and combine them in a risk matrix. Risks in the high-probability, high-impact quadrant are prioritized for treatment. This approach is faster and more accessible to non-technical stakeholders than quantitative analysis.

The Business Impact Analysis (BIA) formalizes this approach — it is a structured process for identifying which business functions are most critical, what the impact of their disruption would be, and how long the organization can operate without them (Recovery Time Objective and Recovery Point Objective).

Slide 5 — Risk Treatment Strategies

What to Do With a Risk After You Identify It

Identifying risk is only the beginning. Organizations must decide how to respond to each risk. Four fundamental strategies exist — no single strategy is always correct. The choice depends on the risk's magnitude, the cost of controls, the organization's risk tolerance, and regulatory requirements.

Risk Mitigation

Apply controls to reduce the probability or magnitude of the risk to an acceptable level. The most common strategy — but remember that all risk cannot be eliminated, only reduced. Mitigation has a cost; the control cost should be justified against the ALE reduction achieved.

Risk Avoidance

Change the system design or business decision to eliminate the risk entirely. If the risk of running a public-facing web server is too high, don't run a public-facing web server. Avoidance often has significant business cost — it may mean not doing something valuable.

Risk Transference

Shift the financial impact of the risk to another party — typically through insurance (cyber liability policy) or contractual indemnification with service providers. Transference does not reduce the probability of the risk occurring; it only changes who pays when it does.

Risk Acceptance

Consciously acknowledge the risk and decide to live with it, without applying additional controls. Appropriate when the risk is low enough that countermeasures are not cost-effective, or when adequate controls already exist. Acceptance must be documented and approved by appropriate leadership — silent acceptance is just negligence.

Slide 6 — Data Ownership and Classification

Organizing Data by Sensitivity

Information classification organizes data into categories based on sensitivity, ensuring appropriate controls are applied to each category. Different organizations use different classification schemes — the US government scheme is the exam standard.

Level Description Impact if Disclosed Corporate Equivalent
Top Secret Highest degree of protection Exceptionally grave damage to national security Restricted / Board Confidential
Secret Substantial degree of protection Serious damage to national security Confidential
Confidential Some protection required Identifiable damage to national security Internal Use Only
Unclassified No classification standards met None / minimal Public

Data Ownership

Organizations designate specific senior executives as data owners for different data types. Data owners are responsible for establishing appropriate classification, determining access controls, and delegating day-to-day data stewardship. Clear ownership creates accountability and prevents the "nobody's responsible" gap.

Data Lifecycle Protection

Data Minimization: Collect the smallest amount of data necessary. Data you do not have cannot be breached. Applies at collection time — limit what fields you capture.

Purpose Limitation: Use data only for the purpose consented to at collection. Marketing data cannot become HR data without re-consent.

Data Retention: Keep data only as long as necessary for its original purpose, then securely dispose of it.

Slide 7 — Technical Controls: DLP and Data Obfuscation

Preventing Data Loss Through Technical Means

Encryption: Uses mathematical algorithms with encryption keys to protect confidentiality of data in transit and at rest. The baseline technical control for sensitive data — without encryption, network interception or physical media theft is trivially successful.

Data Loss Prevention (DLP)

DLP systems enforce information handling policies to prevent unauthorized data exfiltration. Two primary detection mechanisms: Pattern Matching — watches for signatures of sensitive data (SSNs, credit card numbers, specific document watermarks); Watermarking — electronic tags applied to sensitive documents that DLP systems recognize even if content is modified.

Host-based DLP: Software agents on endpoints that monitor file operations, clipboard, print, and removable media access. Can prevent data from leaving a specific machine.

Network-based DLP: Dedicated devices on the network perimeter that inspect outbound traffic for sensitive data patterns. Can block email, web uploads, or FTP transfers containing classified information.

Data Obfuscation Techniques

When data must be used for analytics, development, or testing, obfuscation removes or disguises identifying information without destroying the data's utility.

Hashing: Transforms values to a fixed-length hash. One-way by design — the original value cannot be recovered. Used for password storage, integrity verification, and pseudonymization of lookup values.

Tokenization: Replaces sensitive values with a unique identifier using a secure lookup table. Unlike hashing, the original value can be recovered by authorized parties. Used in payment card processing — the card number never leaves the tokenization vault.

Data Masking: Partially redacts sensitive information — showing only the last four digits of a credit card number, for example. Maintains recognizability for legitimate use while protecting full sensitive data.

De-identification: Removes the ability to link data back to an individual. Used to allow analysis of sensitive datasets (health records, financial data) without exposing personal information. Governed by HIPAA Safe Harbor and Expert Determination methods for health data.

Slide 8 — Training and Red/Blue Team Exercises

Beyond the Penetration Test

Red/Blue team exercises put offensive and defensive teams against each other in simulated environments — providing more realistic training than a standard penetration test, because defenders know an attack is coming and must actually defend in real-time. These exercises are conducted in simulated (not production) environments.

Red Team — Attackers

Uses reconnaissance and exploitation tools to gain network access. Mimics real adversary tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs). May use social engineering, phishing, and physical access attempts alongside technical exploitation. Objective: achieve defined goals (data exfiltration, persistence, lateral movement) without being detected.

Blue Team — Defenders

Secures the network and attempts to detect and prevent red team intrusions using security controls, monitoring, and incident response. Made up of SOC analysts, system and network administrators. Develops detection rules, improves logging, and practices the operational reality of defending against active attackers.

White Team — Referees and Facilitators

The White Team coordinates the exercise, arbitrates disputes, maintains the simulated environment, and monitors that exercise rules are followed. They set the scope, define what is in and out of bounds, track the exercise timeline, and produce the after-action review. The White Team's debrief is often as valuable as the exercise itself — it surfaces observations from an objective vantage point that neither red nor blue team can see.

Purple Teaming: A more collaborative variant where red and blue teams work together — red team explains their techniques in real-time while blue team builds detection. Accelerates learning for both teams and is increasingly preferred over pure adversarial exercises for improving SOC capability.
