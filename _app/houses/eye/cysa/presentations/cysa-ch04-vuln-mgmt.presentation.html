CHAPTER 4 — PRESENTATION

Designing a Vulnerability Management Program

Regulatory requirements (PCI DSS, FISMA), identifying scan targets, scan frequency and scope, active vs. passive scanning, SCAP standards, remediation prioritization, and scanning tool selection.

Slide 1 — What Is Vulnerability Management?

Definition and Purpose

Vulnerability management is the systematic process of identification, prioritization, and remediation of vulnerabilities before a threat can exploit them. It is an organized approach to continuous assessment of organizational security posture.

Unlike a one-time penetration test, vulnerability management is an ongoing program — it runs continuously, tracking new CVEs, monitoring configuration drift, and ensuring remediation occurs within defined timeframes based on severity.

KEY DISTINCTION

Vulnerability scanning identifies vulnerabilities. Vulnerability management is the complete program around scanning: the policies, procedures, asset inventory, prioritization framework, remediation tracking, and reporting that ensure discovered vulnerabilities actually get fixed. Many organizations scan but never truly manage the results.

Slide 2 — Identifying Requirements: Regulatory and Corporate Drivers

PCI DSS

Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard. Requires both internal and external vulnerability scans (11.2). Run quarterly and after significant changes. External scans must use approved scanning vendors (ASVs). Qualified personnel required. PCI DSS networks must be segmented from the broader organizational network to minimize scan scope.

FISMA

Federal Information Security Management Act. Governs federal agencies and organizations operating federal systems. Systems classified as Low, Moderate, or High impact (FIPS 199) — impact level dictates control requirements. Requires scanning when new threats emerge, interoperable tools, report analysis, risk-based remediation, and findings sharing with other agencies.

Corporate Policy

PCI DSS and FISMA don't apply to all organizations — but vulnerability management is still critical security practice. Organizations can and do mandate scanning under internal corporate security policies. The absence of regulatory obligation does not reduce the operational necessity of managing vulnerabilities.

OTHER REGULATORY DRIVERS

HIPAA (healthcare data), GLBA (financial institutions), and FERPA (educational institutions) all govern information storage and processing. These regulations do not always explicitly mandate vulnerability scanning but imply it through their security safeguards requirements — which in practice includes regular scanning programs.

Slide 3 — Identifying Scan Targets and Building the Asset Map

Target Selection Criteria

Not all systems carry the same risk. When identifying which systems to scan and how frequently, consider four key questions:

Question Why It Matters
What is the data classification? Systems processing confidential, secret, or regulated data (PII, PHI, PCI data) require higher scan frequency and broader coverage.
Is the system internet-exposed? External-facing systems have a dramatically larger attack surface and are targeted by opportunistic and targeted attackers — they need priority treatment.
What services does it provide? Web servers, database servers, and authentication systems are high-value targets. File servers and printers are lower priority but still require coverage.
Is it production, test, or development? Production systems require most care during scanning (avoid disruption). Test/dev environments can be scanned more aggressively to identify issues before production deployment.

Asset inventory tools like QualysGuard can maintain a current map of all organizational assets, automatically discovering new systems joining the network and flagging unmanaged devices.

Slide 4 — Determining Scan Frequency

Frequency Determination Factors

Scan frequency is not arbitrary — it is driven by five key factors that balance security needs against operational realities:

Organizational risk appetite: How much time between a new threat emergence and detection through scanning is acceptable? Low risk tolerance demands near-continuous scanning.

Regulatory requirements: PCI DSS mandates quarterly as a minimum. FISMA requires scanning when new threats emerge. These are floors, not ceilings.

Technical constraints: Network infrastructure may not support scanning everything simultaneously without degrading performance. Bandwidth and processing capacity shape feasibility.

Business constraints: High-traffic periods (e-commerce during holidays, tax season for financial firms) may require scan scheduling to avoid peak business hours.

Licensing limitations: Commercial scanners like Nessus control the number of concurrent scans through licensing. Scan scope must fit within licensed capacity.

Slide 5 — Configuring Vulnerability Scans

Key Configuration Decisions

Scan Sensitivity Levels: Choose the level appropriate to your objectives. High-sensitivity scans are thorough but disruptive. Lower-sensitivity scans are less likely to cause service degradation. Some plugins can disrupt systems or cause data loss — always scan with explicit permission and document the scope.

Plugins: Provide scan functionality for specific services, OSes, and vulnerability types. Enable/disable based on your environment — if scanning Linux systems, disable Windows-specific plugins. Scan templates (provided by vendors or custom-built by admins) prevent errors and save time.

Authenticated Scans: Provide the scanner read-only credentials to access the OS, databases, and applications directly. Overcomes firewall and IPS interference. Dramatically increases scan coverage and accuracy over unauthenticated scans. PCI DSS requires authenticated internal scans.

Scan Perspective: External scans provide the attacker's viewpoint (what can they see from the internet?). Internal scans provide the insider threat viewpoint. Datacenter scans provide a close internal scan that may be blocked from other network positions. PCI DSS requires both internal and external scans — external must use approved outside vendors (ASVs).

Slide 6 — Active vs. Passive Vulnerability Scanning

Two Complementary Approaches

Approach Method Strengths Limitations
Active Scanning Directly interacts with target hosts by probing open ports and testing for vulnerability conditions Comprehensive coverage, authenticated scanning deep access, definitive results Noisy (detectable), can cause service disruption or crashes, may miss systems on isolated network segments
Passive Scanning Monitors network traffic like an IDS — observes what hosts do rather than probing them directly Zero disruption risk, continuous monitoring, captures transient systems and ephemeral containers Only detects vulnerabilities reflected in network traffic — cannot see internal system state without direct access
BEST PRACTICE

Use both approaches as complements. Active scanning provides the authoritative vulnerability inventory. Passive scanning provides continuous monitoring coverage between scheduled active scans, catching new vulnerabilities and misconfigurations as they appear in real-time traffic.

Slide 7 — Security Content Automation Protocol (SCAP)

Standardizing Vulnerability Management

Before SCAP, vulnerability management was performed by numerous different tools with no common language or protocol. SCAP, led by NIST (NIST SP 800-117), standardized vulnerability management between software from different vendors.

CCE — Common Configuration Enumeration

Standard names for system configuration issues. Allows different tools to refer to the same misconfiguration consistently.

CPE — Common Platform Enumeration

Standard naming scheme for product names and versions (OS, applications, hardware). Enables automated identification of affected platforms.

CVE — Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures

Standard names for security-related software flaws. The universal language for discussing vulnerabilities. "CVE-2021-44228" (Log4Shell) is understood by every tool globally.

CVSS — Common Vulnerability Scoring System

Standard 0–10 numerical score expressing severity of software flaws. Considers attack vector, complexity, privileges required, user interaction, confidentiality/integrity/availability impact.

XCCDF — Extensible Configuration Checklist Description Format

Language for writing security checklists and reporting results against them. Used by DISA STIGs and CIS Benchmarks.

OVAL — Open Vulnerability and Assessment Language

Language for low-level testing procedures used by XCCDF checklists. Defines how to actually test for a specific configuration state on a specific platform.

Slide 8 — Prioritizing Remediation

The Four Prioritization Factors

Resources are finite. Vulnerability management is fundamentally about allocating limited remediation capacity to the highest-impact vulnerabilities first. Four factors drive prioritization:

System and Information Criticality

How critical is this system? A customer database breach violating confidentiality, integrity, and availability is catastrophic. An isolated dev server has limited impact even if fully compromised.

Vulnerability Severity (CVSS)

CVSS score provides a normalized severity rating. A remote code execution vulnerability (CVSS 9.8) demands immediate attention. An XSS on an internal-only page is lower priority despite being a real vulnerability.

Vulnerability Exposure

External-facing servers are more exposed than intranet servers. Often better to fix a medium-severity external vulnerability before a high-severity internal-only one because the attack surface is dramatically larger.

Remediation Difficulty

How much time and money does a fix require? Sometimes fixing vulnerabilities 2, 3, 4, and 5 is more valuable than spending all resources on vulnerability 1. Opportunity cost matters.

Slide 9 — Testing Fixes and Delayed Remediation Options

The Remediation Workflow

Before deploying any fix: Test planned remediation activities in a sandbox environment. Never apply untested patches directly to production — patches can break application compatibility or cause system instability.

After deploying a fix: Verify effectiveness by rescanning the affected system. Confirm the vulnerability is no longer present. Update the configuration baseline to reflect the new patched state.

When immediate remediation is not possible, two delayed options exist:

Option 1 — Compensating Controls: Implement additional security measures that address a vulnerability without remediating the underlying issue. Example: implement network-level blocking for a vulnerable port while the vendor patch is in development.

Option 2 — Risk Acceptance: Formally document that the risk is acceptable given current constraints and continue business operations, acknowledging the accepted risk. This must be documented, signed off by appropriate authority, and reviewed regularly.

Slide 10 — Vulnerability Scanning Tools

Infrastructure Vulnerability Scanners

Tenable Nessus

Industry standard. Supports authenticated scanning, agent-based scanning, and compliance auditing against DISA STIGs and CIS benchmarks. Extensive plugin library (190,000+).

Qualys

Cloud-based vulnerability management platform with asset mapping, continuous monitoring, and compliance reporting. QualysGuard provides external attack surface visibility.

Rapid7 Nexpose / InsightVM

Integrated with Metasploit. Provides live endpoint risk scoring that updates continuously rather than on a scan cycle. Strong remediation workflow integration.

OpenVAS

Free, open-source fork of the original Nessus codebase. Maintained by Greenbone Networks. Strong community support and updated vulnerability feeds.

Web Application and Wireless Scanners

Web Application Scanners: Nikto (open source, CLI, tests for SQL injection/XSS/CSRF, difficult to use) and Arachni (packaged GUI scanner for Windows/Mac/Linux). Interception proxies like OWASP ZAP and Burp Suite intercept and modify HTTP requests for manual application security testing.

Wireless Assessment Tools: Aircrack-ng (suite for packet capture, injection, and WEP/WPA/WPA2 key cracking), Reaver (WPA/WPA2 passphrase cracking specifically targeting WPS-enabled networks), Hashcat (general-purpose GPU-accelerated password cracking applicable to wireless handshakes).
