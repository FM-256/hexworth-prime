CHAPTER 1 — PRESENTATION

Today's Cybersecurity Analyst

The CIA triad, privacy vs. security, risk assessment frameworks, threat categories, security controls, and the analyst's operational toolkit — all through the CySA+ lens.

Slide 1 — The CIA Triad: Core Security Objectives
C

Confidentiality

Protect data against unauthorized access. Only authorized parties can read, access, or use sensitive information. Enforced through encryption, access controls, and data classification.

I

Integrity

No unauthorized modifications. Data and systems remain accurate and unaltered. Enforced through hashing, digital signatures, audit logs, and change management controls.

A

Availability

Meet the needs of legitimate users. Systems and data are accessible when required. Enforced through redundancy, backups, DDoS mitigation, and disaster recovery planning.

CySA+ EXAM NOTE

Every attack, control, and risk analysis in the CySA+ exam maps back to one or more CIA properties. When analyzing a scenario, always identify which pillar is being threatened or protected. Ransomware attacks all three — it denies availability, threatens integrity, and may expose confidential data.

Slide 2 — Privacy vs. Security: A Critical Distinction

Two Related but Distinct Concepts

Security focuses on protecting the organization's own information assets from unauthorized access, modification, or destruction.

Privacy focuses on how an organization collects, uses, and shares information it holds about individuals — extending obligations well beyond security controls alone.

The phrase "privacy obligations extend beyond just security" is critical. An organization can have excellent security and still violate privacy — for example, by selling properly-secured customer data to third parties without consent.

GAPP — GENERALLY ACCEPTED PRIVACY PRINCIPLES

GAPP provides 10 best-practice principles (not mandated by law): Management, Notice, Choice and Consent, Collection, Use/Retention/Disposal, Access, Disclosure, Security, Quality, and Monitoring and Enforcement. These form the foundation of sound privacy programs and appear in compliance frameworks like HIPAA, GDPR, and PCI DSS.

Slide 3 — Evaluating Security Risks

The Risk Equation

Risk is not random — it exists at the precise intersection of threats and vulnerabilities.

RISK = THREAT x VULNERABILITY
Threats are external forces. Vulnerabilities are internal weaknesses. Both must be present for risk to materialize.

Vulnerabilities are weaknesses in devices, systems, applications, or processes — internal factors your organization can control.

Threats are outside forces that may exploit a vulnerability — external factors you monitor and prepare for but cannot eliminate.

Slide 4 — NIST Risk Assessment Process (SP 800-30)
STEP 1
Identify Threat Sources
Categorize threats: Adversarial (deliberate — insiders, nation-states, criminals), Accidental (administrator errors, misconfiguration — e.g., AWS 2017 US-EAST-1 outage), Structural (hardware/software failure, HVAC), Environmental (fires, floods, power loss).
STEP 2
Identify Vulnerabilities
While threat identification looks externally, vulnerability identification focuses internally. Match vulnerabilities to the threats identified — a patched system has fewer exploitable vulnerabilities for an adversarial threat to leverage.
STEP 3
Assess Likelihood and Impact
Likelihood: probability a threat source will initiate an event AND it will have adverse impact. Impact: magnitude of effect based on CIA triad properties. These two factors populate the qualitative risk matrix (Low/Med/High).
STEP 4
Quantitative Assessment
SLE (Single Loss Expectancy) = asset value x exposure factor. ARO (Annual Rate of Occurrence) = how often the event occurs per year. ALE (Annual Loss Expectancy) = SLE x ARO. This translates risk into dollars to justify control investments.
ALE = SLE x ARO
Annual Loss Expectancy = Single Loss Expectancy multiplied by Annual Rate of Occurrence
Slide 5 — The Four Threat Categories

Adversarial Threats

Deliberate attempts to undermine security. Sources include trusted insiders, competitors, suppliers, customers, business partners, and nation-states. Assessed by capability, intent, and likelihood. Most complex to defend against.

Accidental Threats

Mistakes by authorized personnel. Classic example: AWS February 2017 US-EAST-1 outage — a technician entered an incorrect SOP command, taking down far more servers than intended and collapsing the entire region. No malice required to cause catastrophic impact.

Structural Threats

Equipment, software, or environmental control failures. Examples: hard drive failure causing data loss, HVAC failure causing server overheating, OS bug triggering a crash. Addressed through redundancy, maintenance schedules, and hardware lifecycle management.

Environmental Threats

Natural or man-made disasters: fires, flooding, severe storms, power grid failures, fiber or telecom lines cut. Addressed through geographic redundancy, business continuity planning, and alternate processing sites.

Slide 6 — Risk Management Strategies

Risk Acceptance

Organization accepts the risk associated with known vulnerabilities. Appropriate when risk is low enough that countermeasures cost more than the potential loss, or when adequate controls are already applied.

Risk Avoidance

Risk is too high to accept, so the system configuration or business process is changed to eliminate the risk entirely. Example: stop offering a high-risk service rather than trying to secure it.

Risk Mitigation

Minimize risk to an acceptable level using controls. The most common strategy — all risk cannot be fully avoided. The goal is reducing likelihood or impact to a tolerable threshold.

Risk Transference

Transfer risk to another entity when the organization cannot accept, avoid, or adequately mitigate it. Classic examples: cyber insurance policies, SLA-backed third-party service providers.

Slide 7 — Security Controls: Technical, Operational, and Management

Control Categories

Security controls enforce CIA requirements. Understanding the three categories helps analysts design layered defenses and map controls to frameworks like NIST SP 800-53.

Category Definition Examples

Operational Practices and procedures performed by people to increase security posture Penetration tests, security awareness training, SOPs, incident response drills, access reviews
Management Oversight and governance mechanisms that guide the overall security program Risk assessments, security policies, audit programs, vendor management, business continuity plans

Slide 8 — Network Access Control (NAC) and Perimeter Defense

NAC Fundamentals

Network Access Control enforces two core principles: (1) limit network access to authorized individuals and (2) ensure that systems accessing the network meet baseline security requirements.

802.1x is the most common standard for NAC — works on both wired and wireless networks. Devices authenticate before receiving network access.

Agent-based NAC requires special software on the endpoint to communicate health status to the NAC server. Agentless NAC performs assessment via web browser or passive monitoring.

Access decisions can be based on time of day, user role (faculty vs. student), physical location, and system health posture (patch level, AV status, firewall state).

Perimeter Defense Layers

Firewalls at network boundaries — typically triple-homed (Internet, DMZ, Intranet). Types include Packet Filtering, Stateful Inspection, NGFWs (context-aware), and WAFs (application-layer protection against SQLi/XSS).

DMZ (Demilitarized Zone) hosts servers that receive both internet and intranet traffic. All traffic passing through the firewall is evaluated against the ACL with implicit deny — if no rule permits it, traffic is blocked.

Network Segmentation divides internal networks into zones of different security levels, limiting lateral movement. Jump boxes control administrative access between segments.

Defense Through Deception uses honeypots (fake lucrative targets to monitor attacker behavior) and DNS sinkholes (reroute botnet command-and-control traffic for analysis and blocking).

Slide 9 — Penetration Testing and Red/Blue/White Teams

Penetration Testing Phases (NIST SP 800-115)

Penetration testers simulate cyber attacks using the same tools and information available to real attackers. Requires authorization — conducting a pentest without written permission is illegal.

Phase Activities
Planning Define timing, scope, rules of engagement, and obtain written authorization. No technical work performed here.
Discovery Reconnaissance, OSINT, port scanning, enumeration, vulnerability scanning, web application scanning.
Execute Attack Exploit vulnerabilities: gain access, escalate privileges, browse systems, install additional tools. May return to discovery.
Reporting Document findings, prioritize by risk posed, provide remediation recommendations. Delivered to management.
RED / BLUE / WHITE TEAMS

Red Team (attackers) uses recon and exploitation tools. Blue Team (defenders) secures the network and responds. White Team (referees) coordinates, arbitrates disputes, and maintains the simulated environment. Exercises are performed on non-production networks for training purposes.

Slide 10 — Reverse Engineering and Malware Analysis

Understanding Malicious Code Without the Manual

Malware authors do not document how their software works. Reverse engineering decomposes finished malicious products to understand inner workings through two primary methods:

Static Analysis — examining the malware code without executing it. For interpreted languages (Python, Ruby), source is readable. For compiled languages (C/C++, Java), a decompiler is required. Reveals logic, strings, API calls, and indicators of compromise.

Dynamic Analysis — placing malware in a sandbox environment and observing its behavior on the system and virtual network in real-time. Automated sandboxes can analyze email attachments in near real-time and flag malicious behavior without human intervention.

Sandboxing isolates unknown or suspicious code, watches its behavior, and reports on activity without allowing it to affect production systems. Hardware should be purchased from trusted suppliers — firmware can be compromised during procurement or shipment.
