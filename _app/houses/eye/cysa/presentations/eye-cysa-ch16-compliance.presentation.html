CHAPTER 16 — PRESENTATION

Policy & Compliance

The policy framework hierarchy — policies, standards, procedures, guidelines, and exceptions. US regulatory landscape, NIST/ISO/COBIT frameworks, security control types, and audit versus assessment distinctions.

Slide 1 — The Policy Document Hierarchy

Information Security Policy Framework

Security governance is built from a hierarchy of documents with different levels of specificity, mandatory compliance, and approval authority. Understanding where each document fits — and who approves it — is essential for both the exam and real-world security program management. Documents flow from broad intent (policies) to specific action (procedures).

POLICY

High-level statements of management intent

Approved by CEO, CISO, CIO, or CSO. Broad statements about cybersecurity objectives. Without management buy-in, the policy is meaningless. Example: "The organization shall protect all customer data in accordance with applicable laws and regulations."

STANDARDS

Mandatory implementation requirements

Approved by Director-level or below C-Suite. Mandatory actions, steps, and rules needed to achieve the policy. Example: "All passwords must be at least 12 characters and meet complexity requirements." Standards may also exist in external frameworks (COBIT, ITIL, ISO 27001).

PROCEDURES

Step-by-step action instructions

Detailed, actionable steps for performing specific tasks. Created for front-line employees. Example: The service desk procedure for creating a new user account. Patching procedures defining frequency and process. Evidence production procedures responding to legal holds.

GUIDELINES

Best practices and recommendations (not mandatory)

Flexible guidance allowing exceptions. Compliance is not required. Can be updated quickly as circumstances change. Example: Recommended steps for securely storing files in cloud services — useful but not enforced.

KEY EXAM DISTINCTION

The hierarchy has two "mandatory" levels and two "flexible" levels. Policies and Standards are mandatory — violations carry consequences. Procedures and Guidelines are not technically mandatory in the same way, but procedures are the "how to" for complying with mandatory standards. Guidelines are the only truly optional tier. On the exam: if asked which document is "not mandatory," the answer is Guidelines.

Slide 2 — Policy Types and Exception Management

Common Policy Examples

Most mature security programs maintain a library of specific policy documents covering different domains: Information Security Policy (master policy), Acceptable Use Policy (AUP), Data Ownership Policy, Data Classification Policy, Data Retention Policy, Account Management Policy, Password Policy, Continuous Monitoring Policy, and Code of Conduct/Ethics Policy.

The AUP deserves special attention — it is typically the document all employees sign, establishing what is and is not permitted use of company systems. It is often the only security policy document that non-security employees ever interact with directly.

Policy Exceptions

No policy fits every situation. Exceptions provide a formal mechanism to deviate from a policy, standard, or procedure when compliance is impractical. The exception request must include: which policy requires exception, reason for the exception, scope and duration, associated risks, and compensating controls that reduce the risk created by the deviation.

Exceptions must be approved by the authority specified in the policy — typically the CISO or designated owner. Undocumented, unauthorized exceptions are policy violations, not exceptions.

Compensating Controls

When an organization cannot implement a required security control (due to technical limitations, business constraints, or cost), compensating controls provide an alternative protection that achieves the same risk reduction objective.

PCI DSS defines compensating controls most rigorously: they must meet the intent and rigor of the original requirement, provide a similar level of defense, and go "above and beyond" other existing requirements. The compensating control cannot simply be "we accept the risk."

Slide 3 — US Cybersecurity Laws and Regulations

Sector-Specific Compliance Requirements

The United States lacks a comprehensive federal privacy law, resulting in a patchwork of sector-specific and state-level regulations. As a CySA+, you must understand which regulations apply to your organization and what they require. The exam tests recognition of these frameworks — understand the purpose and key requirements of each.

Regulation Sector Key Requirements Enforced By
HIPAA Healthcare Security and privacy rules for electronic Protected Health Information (ePHI). Covered entities and business associates. Administrative, physical, and technical safeguards. HHS/OCR
PCI DSS Payment Cards Rules for storage, processing, and transmission of payment card data. Not a law — a contractual obligation. 12 requirements. Annual assessments for large merchants. PCI SSC (contractual)
GLBA Financial Formal security programs required for financial institutions. Designated "responsible individual" (CIO/CSO). Safeguards Rule with administrative, physical, and technical controls. FTC, OCC, banking regulators
SOX Public Companies IT security for financial records processing systems. Protects audit integrity — controls must ensure financial records cannot be improperly modified. SEC, PCAOB
FERPA Education Security and privacy for student educational records. Teachers and students can access; others cannot without consent. US Dept. of Education
State Breach Laws All Sectors Require notification to affected individuals after data breaches. Timeframes vary by state (California SB-1386 pioneered this; now all 50 states have versions). State AGs
DATA SOVEREIGNTY

Data is subject to the laws of any jurisdiction where it is collected, stored, or processed. A US company storing data in an EU data center must comply with GDPR. A European company processing US healthcare data may need to comply with HIPAA. Before deploying any cloud service, determine where data will be stored and the regulatory implications. Require cloud providers in writing to notify you before moving data to new jurisdictions. Use encryption as an additional protection layer.

Slide 4 — Adopting Standard Security Frameworks

Why Use Standard Frameworks?

Creating a cybersecurity program from scratch is a daunting task. Standard frameworks provide structured approaches developed by industry experts, tested across thousands of organizations, and recognized by regulators as evidence of reasonable security practices. Using a framework does not guarantee compliance with specific regulations, but it provides a defensible foundation.

NIST CSF

Voluntary framework of five functions: Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. Widely adopted across US critical infrastructure. Implementation Tiers (1-4) measure organizational maturity. Framework Profiles describe target states.

ISO 27001

International standard for Information Security Management Systems (ISMS). External certification by accredited assessors. 14 control categories covering all aspects of information security. Required for organizations doing business with certain regulated industries internationally.

COBIT

Control Objectives for Information and Related Technologies. Developed by ISACA. Four domains: Plan and Organize, Acquire and Implement, Deliver and Support, Monitor and Evaluate. Strong governance and audit focus.

ITIL

Information Technology Infrastructure Library. Focus on IT service management and value delivery. Less security-specific than others — strong on operational process alignment. Widely used in service desk and ITSM contexts.

TOGAF / SABSA

Enterprise architecture frameworks. TOGAF provides architectural design methodology; SABSA focuses on security architecture derivation. Used at the strategic planning level to align security architecture with business objectives.

CIS Controls

18 prioritized security controls from the Center for Internet Security. Highly practical — each control maps to specific technical implementations. Implementation Groups (IG1/IG2/IG3) allow prioritization based on organizational maturity and resources.

Slide 5 — NIST CSF: Implementation Tiers

Measuring Cybersecurity Maturity

The NIST CSF Implementation Tiers measure how well an organization's risk management practices meet cybersecurity objectives. They describe how an organization moves from informal, reactive security to adaptive, risk-informed security operations. Tiers are not a maturity progression to be "passed" in sequence — they describe where an organization currently operates relative to cybersecurity best practices.

Tier 1

Partial: Cybersecurity risk management is not formalized. Risk is managed ad hoc and sometimes reactively. Limited awareness of cybersecurity risk at the organizational level. No organization-wide approach to sharing cybersecurity information.

Tier 2

Risk Informed: Risk management practices are approved by management but not established as organization-wide policy. Awareness of cybersecurity risk exists, but organization-wide approach to managing risk has not been established. External information sharing occurs informally.

Tier 3

Repeatable: Organization-wide risk management practices are formally approved and expressed as policy. Cybersecurity practices are regularly updated based on risk management processes. Organization consistently and accurately monitors cybersecurity risk and shares information internally.

Tier 4

Adaptive: Organization continuously adapts cybersecurity practices based on lessons learned and predictive indicators. Active participation in sharing information with external parties. Cybersecurity risk management is part of the organizational culture.

Slide 6 — Policy-Based Security Controls

Control Categories by Mechanism

Security controls are categorized both by their mechanism of action (what kind of control it is) and by their desired effect (what they are trying to accomplish). A complete security program combines controls from all categories — defense in depth requires multiple overlapping control layers.

Technical Controls

Enforce security in the digital space through hardware or software. Firewalls, encryption, access control systems, antivirus, IDS/IPS, MFA, logging systems. Implemented and enforced automatically without human intervention once configured.

Operational Controls

Processes and procedures for managing technology securely. User access reviews, log monitoring, vulnerability management, security awareness training, change management. Require human execution and oversight.

Managerial Controls

Procedural mechanisms focused on risk management governance. Periodic risk assessments, security planning, incident response planning, policy development. Focus on the management of security, not specific technical implementations.

Control Types by Desired Effect

Preventive: Stop security issues before they occur. Firewalls, encryption, MFA, security awareness training. The ideal control category — prevents the incident from happening.

Detective: Identify security events that have already occurred. IDS, SIEM, audit logs, security cameras. Cannot stop an event in progress, but enables response.

Corrective: Remediate security issues that have already occurred. Backup restoration after ransomware, patch deployment after vulnerability exploitation, account lockout after brute-force.

Deterrent: Discourage attackers from attempting violations. Warning banners, visible security cameras, signs indicating monitoring. Psychological in nature — reduces attack attempts.

Physical: Controls that impact the physical world. Fences, locks, bollards, biometric entry, security guards, fire suppression. Often overlooked in the focus on technical controls.

Compensating: Mitigate risk when primary controls cannot be implemented. Alternative controls that achieve equivalent risk reduction. Must meet the intent and rigor of the original requirement (especially in PCI DSS).

COMBINING CONTROL OBJECTIVES

No single control category is sufficient. Consider preventing unauthorized data center access: a biometric access control system (technical control) operates alongside regular reviews of who has authorized access (operational control) under governance of a routine risk assessment process (managerial control). Layer all three categories for defense in depth. When one control fails or is bypassed, the others continue to provide protection.

Slide 7 — Audits, Assessments, and the Gerstner Principle

"You Get What You Inspect, Not What You Expect"

Louis Gerstner's principle, borrowed from manufacturing quality control, applies directly to cybersecurity program evaluation. A written policy that is never enforced or verified provides no security value. Evaluation — through both audits and assessments — is essential to an effectively run program. The difference between audits and assessments is critical for the exam and for real-world practice.

Audits

Formal reviews of organizational cybersecurity programs, conducted either internally (internal audit) or externally for compliance with specific requirements (PCI DSS QSA audit, SOX audit, ISO 27001 certification assessment).

Audits are rigorous and formal. They result in a formal declaration by the auditor of compliance or non-compliance. Findings are documented evidence and may carry legal weight. External auditors must be independent — they cannot audit what they implemented.

The audit produces a formal opinion: the auditor attests to the accuracy of their findings based on independent verification. This is different from taking the organization's word for it.

Assessments

Less formal reviews of security controls, typically self-initiated by the organization for process improvement purposes. The goal is improvement, not compliance declaration.

Assessments typically rely heavily on interviews with employees rather than independent technical verification. The employee's statement is generally treated as the truth, rather than independently confirmed. This makes assessments faster and less expensive than audits, but less authoritative.

Penetration tests, vulnerability assessments, security maturity assessments, and tabletop exercises are all forms of assessment. They produce recommendations, not compliance declarations.

NDAs and Data Sovereignty in Compliance

Nondisclosure Agreements (NDAs): Employees handling sensitive organizational information sign NDAs that survive the employment relationship. The NDA creates legal liability for disclosure that extends beyond the employee's tenure. From a security governance perspective, NDAs are a personnel control that complements technical access controls — you restrict access technically, and the NDA provides legal recourse if authorized access is misused.

Data Sovereignty: Data is subject to the legal restrictions of any jurisdiction where it is collected, stored, or processed. Before deploying cloud services, security professionals must determine where data will be stored and the regulatory implications. Cloud providers should be contractually required to specify storage locations and provide advance notice of jurisdiction changes.
