CHAPTER 14 — PRESENTATION

Containment, Eradication & Recovery

The post-identification phase of incident response — stopping the bleeding, removing attacker presence, rebuilding clean systems, validating the environment, and capturing lessons learned.

Slide 1 — Incident Response Process Context

Where We Are in the PICERL Framework

The NIST Incident Response lifecycle (Preparation, Identification, Containment, Eradication, Recovery, Lessons Learned) structures how organizations respond to security incidents. After Identification confirms an incident is real, Containment, Eradication, and Recovery execute in sequence — each building on the previous phase. Skipping Containment to go straight to Eradication is a critical error: you may clean up one system while the attacker continues operating freely on others.

C

Containment

Stop the spread. Isolate affected systems. Preserve evidence. Act quickly but document everything.

E

Eradication

Remove all attacker artifacts. Rebuild compromised systems. Close the vulnerability that allowed entry.

R

Recovery

Restore systems to full functionality. Validate integrity. Monitor closely for recurrence.

Slide 2 — Containment Strategies

Containment: Quick and Dirty, Not Perfect

Containment is not about perfection — it is about speed. The longer an attacker remains active in your environment, the more damage they can do and the harder incident response becomes. Containment accepts some business disruption in exchange for stopping further compromise. Coordinate with stakeholders before taking actions that will cause significant service interruption, but do not let coordination become paralysis.

Segmentation

The proactive strategy — network segmentation uses VLANs, firewalls, and access controls to limit how far an attacker can move laterally from an initial compromise. Organizations with mature segmentation can often contain an incident to a single segment, dramatically reducing impact.

During an active incident, emergency segmentation (firewall ACL changes, disabling inter-VLAN routing) can create containment boundaries that did not previously exist.

Isolation and Removal

Network Isolation: Disconnect the compromised system from the internal network while maintaining a direct connection for investigation. The system can be observed without being able to spread infection.

Sandboxing the Attacker: If you can see attacker activity, sometimes it is strategically valuable to let them continue in an isolated environment to gather intelligence about their tools and objectives — with appropriate legal authorization and leadership approval.

Removal: Complete disconnection. Appropriate when isolation is not sufficient or when the attacker's presence is well-documented and continued operation serves no intelligence value.

Containment Objectives

Limit damage to the organization — This is the primary goal. Every minute of attacker access has a cost: data theft, system destruction, lateral movement, reconnaissance of additional targets.

Preserve evidence for investigation — Containment is the first phase where forensic evidence collection is active. Isolate systems but do not power them off (which destroys volatile evidence) unless absolutely required. Capture memory, network state, and running processes before any other action.

Maintain business operations — If containment takes down a critical system, customers suffer. Balance the security imperative of containment against business continuity needs — ideally defined in advance via Business Continuity and Incident Response planning.

Identifying Attackers: Is It Worth It?

IR teams are often asked: "Who did this?" The honest answer is that attribution is extremely difficult, time-consuming, and often unnecessary for business recovery. Attackers use compromised third-party infrastructure, TOR, VPNs, and false flags. A thorough attribution investigation can consume months and still produce uncertain results.

Law enforcement has a different mandate — they need attribution for prosecution. If law enforcement is involved, preserve evidence and let them lead the attribution work. Your IR team's job is to restore operations. Unless there is a compelling business reason (targeted attack requiring understanding the adversary's intent), deprioritize attribution in favor of containment and recovery.

Slide 3 — Evidence Gathering During Containment

Containment Is Also an Evidence Phase

While the primary goal of containment is limiting damage, evidence is being generated and must be captured. Improper handling during containment can destroy evidence needed for post-incident legal action, regulatory reporting, or forensic root-cause analysis.

System Identification

Record full identifying information for every system: hostname, IP address, MAC address, asset tag, physical location, operating system, and purpose. This creates the evidence map for the investigation.

Personnel Documentation

Record name, title, and contact information of every person who handles evidence — including the IR team. Every handoff must be documented for chain of custody.

Time and Location Stamps

Every evidence-handling event needs a precise timestamp (synchronized to a trusted time source) and physical location. Use UTC to avoid timezone ambiguity across geographically distributed investigations.

Slide 4 — Eradication

Complete Removal of the Attacker's Presence

Eradication removes all artifacts of the incident: malware, backdoors, rogue accounts, modified files, malicious scheduled tasks, WMI subscriptions, registry run keys, and any other persistence mechanisms the attacker established. The guiding principle: once an attacker touches your system, assume it is compromised. Attempting to surgically clean a rootkitted system is extremely difficult and unreliable — rebuilding from known-good is safer.

Reconstruction and Reimaging

Rebuild compromised systems from known-good images or clean OS installations. Do not restore from a backup made after the initial compromise — the attacker may have embedded persistence in the backup.

Before reimaging, understand the root cause of the incident. If you rebuild without closing the vulnerability, the attacker will simply compromise the system again. The new system must be hardened against the attack vector used.

Patching Systems

Patch not only the directly compromised systems, but any systems that share the same vulnerability. An incident that exploits an unpatched vulnerability is an opportunity — and a mandate — to audit your entire environment's patch status.

Use the incident as justification for emergency patching authorization. The business case is already made when a production incident demonstrates the cost of deferred patching.

Sanitization and Secure Disposal

Compromised media must be handled according to its sensitivity level. The three tiers of sanitization define what level of effort is required to render data unrecoverable:

Method Technique Recovery Possible? Use When
Clear Logical overwrite — factory reset or zero-fill (dd if=/dev/zero) Possible with advanced tools Low-sensitivity data; internal reuse
Purge Degaussing (destroys magnetic media), multi-pass overwrite (35x Gutmann), cryptographic erase for SSDs Infeasible with current techniques Moderate-high sensitivity; external disposal or reuse outside security boundary
Destroy Shredding, incineration, disintegration, melting Not possible (drive unusable) Highest sensitivity classification; classified material; SSD where purge may leave flash memory remnants
CRITICAL: SSD SANITIZATION

Traditional multi-pass overwriting does not reliably sanitize SSDs due to wear-leveling algorithms — data written to a "zero-filled" SSD may reside in over-provisioned cells that the OS cannot address. For SSDs, use the manufacturer's Secure Erase command (ATA Security Erase or NVMe Format NVM with Secure Erase) or physical destruction for sensitive media. NIST SP 800-88 provides definitive guidance on media sanitization by media type.

Slide 5 — Validating the Eradication Effort

Confirming the Attacker Is Gone

Before declaring eradication complete, systematically validate that no attacker artifacts remain. A missed persistence mechanism means the incident recurs — often worse than the original, because now the attacker knows your detection capabilities. Validation is not optional and should be documented as part of the incident record.

  • 1

    Account Audit: Only authorized user accounts exist on each system. Compare current accounts against your identity management system's authoritative list. Investigate any discrepancies — rogue accounts are a common persistence mechanism.

  • 2

    Permission Review: Verify permissions assigned to each remaining account. Attackers escalate privileges; the account may be legitimate but now have admin rights it should not have.

  • 3

    Logging Verification: Confirm all systems are logging correctly and logs are forwarding to your SIEM or central log server. Attackers often disable logging; confirm it is re-enabled and functioning.

  • 4

    Vulnerability Scan: Run comprehensive vulnerability scans on all affected (and potentially affected) systems. Compare results against your baseline. New open ports or services are red flags.

Slide 6 — Recovery and Post-Incident Activities

Recovery: Restoring to Full Functionality

Recovery is not a redesign project. It is not an opportunity to buy new equipment or rebuild the entire network. It is the targeted restoration of affected systems to normal operation. Scope creep during recovery causes extended outages, budget overruns, and management frustration. Stay focused on the scope defined in your Incident Response Plan.

Isolation to operation: Systems move from containment isolation back to production only after eradication validation is complete. Premature reconnection is the single most common mistake in recovery.

Monitor closely after recovery: The first 30-90 days after an incident are the highest-risk period for recompromise. Heightened monitoring, more frequent vulnerability scans, and enhanced log review are mandatory during this period.

Change Control Closure

During an active incident, an Emergency Change Management Board typically authorizes rapid changes outside normal change control processes. After the incident, every emergency change must be documented and formally closed in the change management system.

Network diagrams, firewall rule sets, and vulnerability scan profiles must all be updated to reflect post-incident configuration changes. Undocumented changes become tomorrow's mystery failures.

Lessons Learned

A lessons-learned meeting should be conducted as close to incident closure as possible — while memories are fresh and the pain of the incident motivates genuine improvement. This is a fact-finding exercise, not a blame session.

The lessons-learned output feeds directly into: updated IR procedures, new detection rules, patching priorities, training needs, and budget requests. Document it formally — this is how organizations improve their security posture after incidents.

The Final Report

Every incident concludes with a comprehensive written report that serves as the organization's institutional memory and potential legal documentation. The report must stand alone — a reader who was not involved in the incident should be able to understand what happened, why, and how it was resolved.

Required elements: Timeline of incident and response events (with precise timestamps) | Root cause analysis | Evidence location and description | Containment, eradication, and recovery actions with rationale | Estimated impact (financial and operational) | Post-recovery validation results | Lessons learned and recommended improvements

PROFESSOR'S NOTE — THE REAL COST OF A SKIPPED LESSONS LEARNED

Organizations that treat the final report as a compliance checkbox — written but not read, filed but not acted upon — repeat the same incidents. The organizations that improve after incidents are those where leadership reads the final report, asks hard questions, and allocates resources to implement the recommendations. If your lessons learned process does not result in measurable changes to procedures, tools, or training, it is not working. The attacker learned from your environment; you should learn at least as much from the incident.
