CHAPTER 11 — PRESENTATION

Building an Incident Response Program

NIST SP 800-61 IR phases, CSIRT composition, policy and playbook development, incident classification, severity rating, communication plans, post-incident activity, and lessons learned methodology.

Slide 1 — Security Incident Terminology

Events vs. Incidents — A Critical Distinction

Event: Any observable occurrence in a system or network. A user logging in is an event. A failed login is an event. Events are neutral — they are simply things that happened.

Adverse Event: Any event with negative consequences. A server crash is an adverse event. A user receiving a phishing email is an adverse event. Adverse events require attention but may not be security incidents.

Security Incident: An imminent threat of violation, or a violation itself, of a security policy, acceptable use policy, or standard security practice. Incidents require a formal response.

CSIRT: Computer Security Incident Response Team — the team of professionals responsible for handling security incidents using standardized procedures.

CRITICAL EXAM CONCEPT

Not every event is an incident, but every incident contains at least one event. This hierarchy shapes how analysts triage and escalate. An analyst's job is to determine whether a stream of events constitutes an incident requiring formal response activation.

Phase 1: Preparation — Building Before You Need It

Preparation takes the most time and is the most overlooked phase. It requires a proper policy foundation, built cyber defenses, trained personnel, and pre-staged response kits.

Incident Response Toolkit must include: Digital forensic workstations, forensic software, packet capture devices, spare network gear, backup devices, blank removable media, collection/analysis/reporting laptops, portable printers, office supplies, and evidence collection materials.

Analysis Best Practices (NIST 800-61)

  • Profile networks and systems to establish behavioral baselines before incidents occur
  • Understand normal behavior — you cannot detect abnormal without knowing normal
  • Create and enforce a logging policy ensuring all critical systems generate useful logs
  • Perform event correlation — single events rarely tell the full story; the pattern matters
  • Synchronize clocks across all systems — NTP ensures timestamps correlate across log sources
  • Maintain a knowledge base of past incidents, IOCs, and network topology
  • Capture network traffic at the perimeter and internally for forensic capability
  • Filter the noise — tune alerts to reduce false positives that exhaust analyst capacity
Slide 4 — Containment, Eradication, and Recovery

Phase 3: Stopping the Damage and Restoring Operations

Containment strategy must be chosen before action is taken. The wrong strategy can destroy forensic evidence, tip off the attacker, or cause greater damage. Document the decision and rationale.

StepActivityKey Consideration
1. Select StrategyChoose containment approach based on incident type, impact, and business requirementsShort-term vs. long-term containment; some require immediate isolation, others require monitoring to gather intelligence
2. Limit DamageImplement the chosen containment strategyNetwork isolation, account disabling, system quarantine, firewall ACL changes
3. Gather EvidenceCollect forensic evidence before eradication destroys artifactsVolatile evidence (memory, running processes) first; then disk imaging; chain of custody documentation
4. EradicateRemove malware, close access vectors, patch vulnerabilitiesVerify the root cause is eliminated, not just the symptom
5. RecoverRestore systems and verify normal operationsRestore from known-good backups; monitor restored systems closely for re-infection
6. AttributionIdentify the attacking system or attacker (if feasible)Attribution is difficult, often irrelevant to recovery, but may have legal and threat intelligence value
Slide 5 — Post-Incident Activity

Phase 4: Learning and Improving

The CSIRT is not done when the incident is contained and eradicated. The post-incident phase is where the organization grows in resilience — if the lessons are actually implemented.

Lessons Learned Review — Key Questions (NIST 800-61)

  • What happened, and exactly when did each event occur? Build a precise timeline.
  • How did staff perform? Were the right people involved at the right times?
  • Were existing procedures followed? If not, why not?
  • Were procedures adequate for this type of incident? What gaps were exposed?
  • What should have been done differently at each phase?
  • Was information shared effectively internally and with appropriate external parties?
  • How could we have detected the incident sooner? What monitoring gaps exist?
  • What new tools, resources, or training does the organization need?

Evidence Retention Requirements

US Government agencies: Must retain all incident handling records for 3 years due to legal requirements.

Private organizations: Most maintain records for 2 years unless regulatory requirements (HIPAA, PCI-DSS, SOX) mandate longer retention periods.

Evidence retention covers: incident timelines, forensic images, logs, communications, decisions made and their rationale, and remediation actions taken.

Slide 6 — IR Foundation: Policy, Procedures, and Playbooks

IR Policy — The High-Level Authority Document

The IR policy is the foundation of the organization's incident response program. It guides efforts at a high level, provides authority for response actions, is approved by the CEO or CIO, and should be written to remain relevant for years without modification.

Required policy contents: Statement of management commitment, purpose, objectives, scope, definitional terms, roles/responsibilities/authority, priority/rating scheme, incident prioritization scheme, performance measures for CSIRT, reporting requirements, and contact information.

Playbooks — Incident-Specific Response Procedures

A playbook describes the response to a specific high-severity incident type. Unlike general procedures, playbooks provide step-by-step, decision-tree guidance optimized for a particular scenario. Common playbook types:

  • Data breach of financial information or PII
  • Phishing attack targeting customers or employees
  • Web server defacement or DDoS attack
  • Loss or theft of corporate laptop or mobile device
  • Network intrusion / unauthorized access
  • Windows Golden Ticket attack (Kerberos compromise)
  • Ransomware infection and recovery
Slide 7 — CSIRT Composition and Scope

Technical SMEs

System engineers, network administrators, database administrators. Provide technical expertise to investigate systems and implement remediation actions.

IT Support Staff

Execute technical actions as directed by CSIRT leadership. Handle reimaging, account resets, firewall changes, and physical system access.

Legal Counsel

Ensure all response actions comply with legal, policy, and regulatory requirements. Must be consulted before engaging law enforcement or preserving evidence for litigation.

Human Resources

Investigate possible employee misconduct when incidents involve insider threats. Coordinate disciplinary actions and manage personnel matters arising from the incident.

Public Relations

Coordinate all communication with media and public. Ensures consistent messaging and prevents unauthorized disclosures that could damage the organization or compromise the investigation.

Executive Leadership

CISO, CIO, CEO awareness and decision authority. Approve major response decisions — including law enforcement notification, public disclosure, and business impact decisions.

Severity: Functional Impact

Degree of impairment to organizational operations. Ranges from No Effect to Loss of Functionality — considering whether mission-critical systems are impaired and for how long.

Severity: Economic Impact

Financial loss to the organization. Includes direct losses, incident response costs, regulatory fines, legal liability, and reputational damage quantification.

Severity: Recoverability

Amount of time and resources required to recover from the incident. Ranges from Regular (predictable, few hours) through Extended (significant time, external resources) to Not Recoverable.

Severity: Information Impact

Degree of information compromise. Considers data classification (public, sensitive, classified) and whether data was disclosed, modified, or destroyed.

OPERATIONS PERSPECTIVE

In real IR operations, the out-of-band communication requirement is critical. If your SIEM and email are compromised, you cannot coordinate the response through those channels. Pre-established out-of-band means: personal cell phones, Signal, pre-printed contact lists, and conference bridges not running on corporate infrastructure. This is planned during Preparation — not figured out during an active incident.
