CHAPTER 2 — PRESENTATION

Using Threat Intelligence

Open and closed source feeds, STIX/TAXII exchange standards, confidence scoring, the intelligence lifecycle, threat actors, attack frameworks, and proactive threat hunting.

Slide 1 — What Is Threat Intelligence?

Intelligence vs. Raw Data

Threat intelligence is data and information about adversaries — their motivations, capabilities, tools, and methodologies — that has been processed and analyzed to be actionable.

Without threat intelligence, security teams react blindly to events. With it, they can anticipate threat actor behavior, prioritize defenses against realistic threats, and reduce both the likelihood and impact of attacks.

Threat feeds provide up-to-date details about active threats, including: IP addresses, hostnames and domains, email addresses, URLs, file hashes, file paths, and CVE numbers.

EXAM FOCUS

Understand the difference between data (raw logs, IP addresses) and intelligence (analyzed, contextualized, and actionable information). A list of malicious IPs is data. An analysis explaining which threat actor uses those IPs, for what purpose, and what their next likely step is — that is intelligence.

Slide 2 — Open Source vs. Proprietary Intelligence Sources

Open Source Intelligence (OSINT)

Acquired from publicly available sources. The challenge is selecting which sources to trust and prioritize. Key sources include:

  • Senki.org — community-maintained threat data
  • AlienVault Open Threat Exchange (OTX) — collaborative IOC sharing platform
  • MISP Threat Sharing Project — standardized threat feeds from many sources
  • Threatfeeds.io — curated index of open source threat intelligence feeds
  • CISA (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency) — U.S. government alerts and advisories
  • CISA Automated Indicator Sharing (AIS) — machine-speed IOC distribution
  • U.S. DoD Cyber Crime Center — government threat data
  • SANS Internet Storm Center — community-reported threat data with daily diaries
  • VirusShare / VirusTotal — malware samples and analysis
  • Spamhaus — blocklists for malicious IPs and domains
  • Microsoft Threat Intelligence Blog and Cisco's Talos — vendor-published research

Proprietary and Closed Source Intelligence

Commercial security vendors, government organizations, and specialized security firms create and use proprietary intelligence with non-public methods and sources. Organizations pay for this because:

They may want to keep their threat data secret from the adversaries they are tracking. They may sell or license the intelligence as a product. They may not want threat actors to know what data has been gathered about them. Proprietary feeds typically have higher confidence scores and more targeted relevance than broad public feeds.

Slide 3 — Assessing Threat Intelligence Quality

Three Assessment Factors

Timeliness: A feed operating on delay can cause you to miss a threat entirely or react after it is no longer relevant. Freshness is critical for active IOC feeds.

Accuracy: Is it reliable? Is it validated? How many independent sources corroborate the data? A single unverified report carries much less weight than three independent confirmations.

Relevance: Does this intelligence apply to your organization, industry, technology stack, and geographic region? Nation-state APT targeting semiconductor manufacturers may be irrelevant to a regional healthcare provider.

ThreatConnect Confidence Scoring System

Confidence scores measure the trust level of intelligence. Lower scores do not mean intelligence is useless — they inform appropriate caution in decision-making.

Confirmed 90–100
Probable 70–89
Possible 50–69
Doubtful 30–49
Improbable 2–29
Discredited 1
Slide 4 — Threat Indicator Standards: STIX, TAXII, OpenIOC

Standardized Exchange Formats

Managing threat information requires standardization to enable automated processing, machine-speed sharing, and consistent terminology across organizations and tools.

STIX — Structured Threat Information Expression: XML-based language originally sponsored by DHS. Defines 12 STIX domain objects (attack patterns, identity, malware, threat actors, tools, campaigns, course of action, etc.). Objects are related to each other through "relationship" or "sighting" connections.

TAXII — Trusted Automated Exchange of Indicator Information: Companion to STIX. Defines how cyberthreat information is communicated at the application layer via HTTPS. Specifically designed to support STIX data exchange between organizations in near-real time.

OpenIOC — Open Indicators of Compromise: XML-based framework developed by Mandiant for sharing specific technical indicators — registry keys, file hashes, mutexes, and other forensic artifacts that indicate a compromise occurred.

EXAM TIP

STIX = the language/format for describing threat intelligence. TAXII = the transport protocol/mechanism for sharing STIX data. Think of STIX as the content and TAXII as the delivery truck. They are designed to work together but are separate standards.

Slide 5 — The Intelligence Lifecycle

A Structured Approach to Intelligence

Many organizations adopt a threat intelligence life cycle to effectively plan, gather, process, and use intelligence. It prevents ad hoc, reactive collection and ensures intelligence aligns with security priorities.

1

Requirements Gathering

Assess what breaches occurred, what data would have prevented them, and what controls were missing. This drives collection priorities.

2

Data Collection

Gather intelligence from identified sources. Repeats continuously as requirements emerge or are refined by new threat events.

3

Processing and Analysis

Normalize data formats, analyze for meaning, and convert raw data into actionable intelligence reports or automated feed inputs.

4

Dissemination

Distribute intelligence to IR teams, vulnerability management, risk management, and security engineering functions.

5

Feedback

Consumer feedback improves future requirements and output quality. The cycle is iterative, not linear.

Slide 6 — Information Sharing and Analysis Centers (ISACs)

Sector-Specific Threat Intelligence Communities

ISACs were created to enable trusted sharing of threat information between critical infrastructure owners and operators within the same industry sector. They operate on a trust model that allows in-depth sharing of both physical and cyber threat data.

ISACs operate 24/7, providing incident response support and threat analysis to members in their sector. There are 25 recognized ISACs — three are specifically tested on CySA+:

ISAC Sector Website
H-ISAC Healthcare h-isac.org
FS-ISAC Financial Services fsisac.com
A-ISAC Aviation a-isac.com
WHY ISACS MATTER

A threat actor targeting hospital patient records will likely try multiple healthcare organizations using the same TTPs. ISACs allow Hospital A to immediately warn Hospital B about the attack vector, indicators, and mitigation steps — turning a single victim's experience into collective defense for the entire sector.

Slide 7 — Common Threat Actor Categories

Nation-State Actors

State-sponsored groups with country-level resources, patience, and legal cover in their home nation. Examples: APT28 (Russia/GRU), APT41 (China/MSS), Lazarus Group (North Korea). Pursue espionage, sabotage, and financial operations simultaneously. The highest-capability threat category.

Organized Crime

Criminal enterprises with focused financial motivations: ransomware-as-a-service operations, banking trojans, card fraud, and business email compromise. Groups like REvil and Conti operated with HR, legal, and business development functions. Estimated $8T+ annual global impact.

Hacktivists

Use hacking as a means to a political or philosophical end. Range from individual actors to organized groups like Anonymous. Tactics: website defacement, DDoS, data leaks (doxing). Targets chosen for ideological or symbolic value rather than financial gain.

Insider Threats

Current or former employees, contractors, or business partners with authorized access. Motivations: financial gain, grievance, coercion, or ideology. Particularly dangerous because they bypass perimeter controls by definition and know where sensitive data resides.

Slide 8 — Attack Frameworks for Defenders

Why Use Attack Frameworks?

Attack frameworks help defenders model realistic adversary behavior, design layered defenses, and ensure detection capabilities map to actual attack techniques rather than theoretical scenarios.

MITRE ATT&CK

Adversarial Tactics, Techniques, and Common Knowledge. The most comprehensive freely available database of adversary techniques. Covers the full lifecycle: initial access, execution, persistence, privilege escalation, defense evasion, credential access, discovery, lateral movement, collection, exfiltration, and impact.

Diamond Model

Describes how an adversary deploys a capability against an infrastructure targeting a victim. Four vertices: Adversary, Capability, Infrastructure, Victim. Meta-features (timestamps, phase, result, direction) order events into activity threads. Used to discover more about an intrusion by following relationships between elements.

Lockheed Martin Kill Chain + Unified Kill Chain

The Cyber Kill Chain defines 7 attack phases: Reconnaissance, Weaponization, Delivery, Exploitation, Installation, C2, Actions on Objectives. The Unified Kill Chain combines the Kill Chain with ATT&CK using 18 phases to cover attacks inside and outside the defended network perimeter.

Slide 9 — Proactive Threat Hunting

Staying Ahead of Adversaries

Threat hunting is proactive — rather than waiting for alerts, analysts actively search for indicators of threats that have bypassed existing defenses. A hypothesis-driven approach is essential:

Establishing a hypothesis: Based on new threat actor intelligence, new attack technique, or a gap in your detection capability. The hypothesis must be testable and have actionable results.

Profiling threat actors and activities: Understand who might be a threat, why, and what their typical TTPs (Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures) look like before hunting.

Reducing attack surface: Each eliminated attack vector focuses the hunt and makes remaining surface area more manageable to protect.

Bundling critical assets into protection zones: Helps prioritize hunting resources on high-value targets where a compromise would be most impactful.

Integrated intelligence: Combine multiple intelligence sources (endpoint telemetry, network flows, OSINT, threat feeds) for a complete picture — no single source is sufficient for effective hunting.

Improving detection capabilities: Threat hunting is a continuous improvement process — each hunt either finds nothing (and validates existing defenses) or discovers a gap and improves future detection coverage.
