CHAPTER 13 — PRESENTATION

Forensic Analysis & Techniques

Digital forensics fundamentals — building the toolkit, evidence acquisition, chain of custody, disk and memory imaging, network forensics, cloud challenges, and proper investigative reporting.

Slide 1 — What Is Digital Forensics?

Reconstructing Events From Digital Evidence

Digital forensics is the discipline of determining what changes, activities, or actions occurred on a host, server, or network by assembling and analyzing disparate pieces of digital information. Incident response teams and law enforcement use the same core techniques, though with different priorities — IR teams focus on stopping damage and restoring operations; law enforcement focuses on evidence admissibility and prosecution support.

The single most important principle: everything you do must be repeatable by an independent third-party investigator. This is the foundation of forensic integrity. If you cannot demonstrate an unbroken chain of custody and reproducible methods, your findings may be inadmissible in court and challengeable in civil proceedings.

Documentation Is Everything

Every action taken during a forensic investigation must be documented: what you did, when you did it, what tools you used, what the results were. This is not optional paperwork — it is the evidence that your evidence is trustworthy.

Contemporaneous notes (written in real time, not reconstructed later) carry far more weight than post-hoc documentation. Use a case log, timestamp every entry, and never alter prior entries.

Chain of Custody

The chain of custody is the documented record of who had possession of evidence, when, and what they did with it. A break in the chain — unexplained gaps, unsigned handoffs, missing timestamps — creates grounds for legal challenge and can render your entire investigation worthless in court.

Chain of custody forms must record: evidence description, collection date/time, collector identity, location, all transfers with signatures, and storage location. This applies equally to hardware, disk images, and log files.

PROFESSOR'S NOTE

In intelligence and law enforcement work, cases have been thrown out not because the evidence was bad, but because the documentation was sloppy. The actual truth of what happened matters less in a courtroom than what can be proven through properly handled evidence. Build good documentation habits before you need them — you cannot reconstruct a chain of custody after the fact.

Slide 2 — Building the Forensic Toolkit

Workstation Requirements

A dedicated forensic workstation requires significant compute resources: a multicore CPU for analysis tasks, maximum RAM for loading large disk images into memory, and large, fast storage — you need roughly 2-3x the target drive capacity for images, analysis files, and working space. The workstation must be air-gapped or have network access tightly controlled to prevent contamination of evidence.

Software Forensic Tools

FTK (Forensic Toolkit): Commercial platform for imaging, indexing, and analysis. Handles email extraction, password cracking, and timeline visualization.

EnCase: Industry-standard commercial tool, widely accepted in court. Uses the .E01 format with built-in hashing and chain of custody metadata.

SIFT (SANS Investigative Forensic Kit): Free Ubuntu-based workstation with pre-installed forensic tools. Excellent for training and professional use.

The Sleuth Kit (TSK): Open-source command-line tools for file system analysis. Basis for the Autopsy browser-based GUI.

Hardware Components

Write Blockers: Hardware devices that physically prevent any write operations to the source drive during imaging. Hardware write blockers are preferred over software variants for highest forensic integrity — they operate at the hardware level before any OS interaction.

Forensic Drive Duplicators: Dedicated devices (Tableau, Logicube) that clone drives, compute hashes, and record chain of custody metadata without requiring a separate workstation.

Wiped/Sanitized Media: All target drives must be cryptographically wiped before use. An unwashed drive contaminating your image collection is an investigative disaster.

Cables and Adapters

IDE, SATA, SAS, M.2, NVMe, USB adapters. You will encounter every interface type in the field — always carry comprehensive adapter sets.

Digital Camera

Photograph system layout, cable routing, drive labels, BIOS screens, and physical condition before touching anything. Timestamps in EXIF data.

Labels and Forms

Label maker, evidence tags, chain of custody forms, and numbered evidence bags. Label every item before it moves from its original location.

Slide 3 — Forensic Software Capabilities

What Forensic Platforms Can Do

Modern forensic suites go far beyond simple file browsing. Understanding these capabilities helps you know what evidence is recoverable and what techniques attackers use to defeat them.

Capability Description Tools

File Carving Methods

File carving is the recovery of files directly from raw disk blocks without relying on the filesystem. It is essential when the filesystem is corrupted, overwritten, or when deleted files must be recovered.

Header/Footer Carving: Uses known file signatures (e.g., JPEG starts with FF D8 FF, ends with FF D9) to identify file boundaries. Fast and reliable for common formats.

Content-Based Carving: Uses statistical analysis of content (character frequency, compression signatures, text patterns) to identify file types without headers.

File Structure Carving: Uses internal file structure knowledge (FAT entries, NTFS record structure, Office XML schema) to recover even fragmented files.

Slide 4 — Endpoint Forensics

Memory Forensics

Live memory contains running processes, active network connections, decrypted encryption keys, and malware that never touches the disk (fileless malware). Memory evidence is extremely volatile — it vanishes when power is cut.

DumpIt (Windows): Single-executable RAM capture, minimal footprint.

Volatility Framework: Open-source memory analysis — extracts processes, network connections, registry hives, and can detect rootkits and injected code.

Hibernation Files / Crash Dumps: hiberfil.sys and .dmp files contain memory snapshots that survive reboots — often overlooked but valuable.

Disk Forensics

The most common forensic activity. Ranges from reviewing specific files to full bitstream imaging and analysis of entire volumes.

Slack space: The space between the end of a file's data and the end of the last cluster. Older data fragments can linger here.

Unallocated space: Clusters not assigned to any active file. Deleted files often exist here until overwritten.

MFT (Master File Table): NTFS metadata store — records file names, timestamps, attributes, and data locations for every file that ever existed on the volume.

Password Cracking and Cryptographic Tools

Encrypted drives and password-protected files require specialized tools. When possible, avoid brute-force — it is computationally expensive and time-consuming. Capture systems while logged in to bypass full-disk encryption. Use memory dumps to extract keys for drives already mounted at time of seizure.

John the Ripper: Open-source password cracker supporting hundreds of hash types. Dictionary, brute-force, and rule-based attacks.

Hashcat: GPU-accelerated hash cracking — dramatically faster than CPU-only tools for modern hash types.

Advanced Office Password Breaker / ElcomSoft: Specialized tools for Office, ZIP, and other encrypted file formats with format-specific optimizations.

Slide 5 — Order of Volatility

Collect the Most Volatile Data First

Different types of digital evidence persist for different amounts of time. The forensic principle of Order of Volatility requires investigators to collect the most volatile (most easily lost) data before moving to more persistent sources. Performing disk imaging before capturing RAM, for example, means losing any fileless malware or decryption keys residing in memory.

  • 1

    CPU Registers and Cache — Lost immediately on power cycle. Practically not captured in most investigations but is the most volatile data source.

  • 2

    RAM (System Memory) — Contains running processes, network connections, encryption keys, and fileless malware. Capture with DumpIt, LiME, or FMem before any other action on a running system.

  • 3

    Network State — Active connections (netstat), ARP cache, routing tables. Captured with ipconfig, arp -a, netstat -ano, and route print. Changes rapidly as sessions time out.

  • 4

    Running Processes and Services — Tasklist, process handles, open files. Captures what is executing at the moment of investigation.

  • 5

    Disk Storage — NTFS/FAT data, MFT, registry hives, log files. Persistent but can be modified if the attacker still has access. Image before analysis.

  • 6

    Backup Media / Remote Logs — SIEM/syslog data, cloud logs, network device logs. Off-system data that survives host compromise.

  • 7

    Archived and Printed Records — Physical documents, paper audit trails. Least volatile. Preserved through normal physical evidence controls.

Slide 6 — Disk Imaging Techniques

Acquiring Forensic Images

A forensic disk image is a bit-for-bit copy of the entire storage device — including allocated space, unallocated space, file slack, and any data in between. This is fundamentally different from a file backup, which only copies active files. The image is hashed before and after acquisition; matching hashes prove the copy is identical to the original and has not been tampered with.

# dd - standard Linux/Unix bitstream imaging # bs=64k increases block size for speed; if= is source; of= is destination dd bs=64k if=/dev/sda of=/mnt/usb1/evidence_sda.img # Verify with hashing (always use BOTH MD5 and SHA-256) md5sum /dev/sda > sda_original.md5 md5sum /mnt/usb1/evidence_sda.img > sda_image.md5 # Compare: hashes must be identical # Alternative: dc3dd (forensic dd variant with built-in hashing and logging) dc3dd if=/dev/sda hof=/mnt/usb1/evidence.img hash=md5 hash=sha256 log=/mnt/usb1/evidence.log

Write Blockers

Hardware write blockers intercept write commands at the interface level — the drive signals a write error to any software that attempts to write, while the actual disk sectors remain untouched. This ensures forensic integrity even if the analysis software or OS attempts to write to the source.

Software write blockers work at the driver level and are generally acceptable for static analysis, but hardware blockers are the gold standard for evidence admissibility.

Live System Imaging

Sometimes full-disk encryption or memory-resident malware requires imaging a running system. Live imaging is messier — the imaging tool itself modifies memory and potentially swap space, and disk contents change during the imaging process.

Malware may detect imaging tools and attempt to hide or destroy evidence. Document all anomalies. The limitations of live capture must be disclosed in your forensic report.

Slide 7 — Network, Cloud, and Container Forensics

Network Forensics

Captures traffic artifacts from security or network device logs, traffic monitoring data, and full packet capture. Wireshark provides GUI-based capture and analysis; tcpdump is the command-line equivalent available by default on most Linux systems. PCAP files can be analyzed with Zeek (formerly Bro) for protocol-level intelligence.

Network forensics can reveal lateral movement, C2 communication patterns, data exfiltration volumes, and attacker tooling even when host-based evidence has been wiped.

Cloud Forensics Challenges

Shared-tenant cloud models make traditional forensics extremely difficult. You cannot seize a server from AWS. Physical isolation is not available. Chain of custody for cloud-hosted evidence requires the provider's cooperation.

Before an incident: review your contract's investigation provisions, identify what logs are available and their retention period, and establish a point of contact at your cloud providers. After an incident, you may have days — not weeks — before log retention expires.

Virtualization and Container Forensics

Virtualization: VMs can be copied (cold cloned or live migrated) to a secure analysis environment. The hypervisor layer may contain useful artifacts (VMX files, snapshots, memory dumps) not visible from within the guest OS. Virtualization forensics is often easier than physical forensics because the entire environment is portable.

Container Forensics: Containers are designed to be ephemeral and disposable — when an incident occurs, automated systems may destroy the evidence before anyone can collect it. Container forensics requires: pre-incident instrumentation (centralized logging, audit trails), understanding of overlay filesystems (where layer data is actually stored), and network traffic capture at the pod or service level rather than the container level. The fundamental challenge: the forensic artifacts you need may not exist when you need them.

CLOUD FORENSICS REALITY CHECK

Many cloud providers will cooperate with investigations — but only if you have a contract provision requiring it, a legal process (subpoena/warrant), or a pre-established incident response agreement. The time to negotiate forensic access is before the breach, not after. Review your cloud contracts' investigation and audit clauses before you need them.

Slide 8 — The Forensic Process and Reporting

Structured Investigative Process

  • 1

    Determine Goals: What are you trying to prove or disprove? What legal standard applies? IR investigation vs. law enforcement? Define scope before starting.

  • 2

    Outline Data Locations and Types: Where is evidence likely to reside? Physical hosts, cloud storage, mobile devices, network infrastructure, backups?

  • 3

    Document the Plan: Written investigation plan with scope, methods, authorization, and legal review. Prevents scope creep and ensures all parties understand the investigation boundaries.

  • 4

    Acquire and Preserve Evidence: Following Order of Volatility. Hash all evidence immediately upon collection. Photograph before touching. Complete chain of custody forms.

  • 5

    Perform Initial Analysis: Triage — identify the most promising evidence sources, timelines, and early indicators of compromise to guide deeper investigation.

  • 6

    Conduct Deeper Investigation: Full timeline analysis, file carving, log correlation, malware reverse engineering, network traffic analysis.

  • 7

    Report on Findings: Comprehensive written report covering goals, targets, methods, findings, and conclusions. Understandable to both technical and non-technical readers.

The Forensic Report

Every forensic investigation concludes with a written report regardless of what was or was not found. The report serves as the official record of the investigation and must include:

Goals and Scope: What the investigation was tasked with determining, and what was in or out of scope.

Evidence Collected: Complete list of evidence, collection methods, hash values, chain of custody records, and storage locations.

Findings and Analysis: What you found, how you found it, what tools you used, and what the evidence means. Distinguish between facts (what the data shows) and conclusions (your interpretation).

Limitations: What you could not determine and why. Gaps in evidence, limitations of tools, and evidence that was unavailable or destroyed.
