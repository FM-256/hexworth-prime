Evidence Types in Digital Forensics

Understanding the four categories of legal evidence is the foundation of every forensic investigation. Misclassifying evidence can get it excluded from court. This module covers each type, the Federal Rules of Evidence (FRE) that govern admissibility, and how hash verification anchors digital evidence integrity.

Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish between real, demonstrative, documentary, and testimonial evidence
  • Identify applicable FRE rules (401-403, 702, 901-902) for digital evidence
  • Explain how SHA-256 hash verification ensures evidence integrity
  • Classify evidence items from a forensic investigation scenario

The Four Evidence Categories

1. Real (Physical) Evidence

Real evidence is any tangible object presented in court. In digital forensics, this includes the physical hardware itself: hard drives, USB drives, mobile phones, servers, network devices, and printed documents from a suspect's workstation.

Real evidence must be authenticated under FRE 901 — someone must testify that the item is what it purports to be. For a hard drive, this means documenting its serial number, make, model, and establishing an unbroken chain of custody from seizure to courtroom.

2. Demonstrative Evidence

Demonstrative evidence illustrates or explains other evidence. It does not itself prove a fact — it helps the trier of fact understand testimony or real evidence. Examples include:

  • Timeline visualizations generated from log files
  • Network topology diagrams showing attack paths
  • Annotated screenshots of forensic tool output
  • Charts showing data exfiltration volume over time

Demonstrative evidence is admissible under FRE 401-403 when its probative value outweighs the risk of unfair prejudice, and when the creating expert can testify to its accuracy.

3. Documentary Evidence

Documentary evidence consists of documents and records. In digital forensics, this is the broadest category and includes log files, emails, database records, configuration files, browser history exports, and forensic examination reports.

Documentary evidence faces the best evidence rule (FRE 1002): the original document is required unless a duplicate is admissible under FRE 1003. For digital evidence, a forensically sound bit-for-bit copy verified by cryptographic hash is generally treated as equivalent to the original.

FRE 902(14): Effective December 2017, certified copies of digital evidence can be self-authenticating when accompanied by a written certification of a qualified person describing the process used to create the copy and verifying its accuracy. This eliminates the need for live testimony in many routine authentication scenarios.

4. Testimonial Evidence

Testimonial evidence is given by a witness under oath. In digital forensics, this comes from expert witnesses (FRE 702) who interpret technical findings. A forensic examiner testifies about what they found, what tools they used, and what conclusions the evidence supports.

Expert testimony must meet the Daubert standard (or Frye in some jurisdictions): the methodology must be testable, peer-reviewed, have known error rates, and be generally accepted in the field.

Federal Rules of Evidence: Key Rules

Rule Name Digital Forensics Application
FRE 401 Relevance Evidence must make a fact of consequence more or less probable. A hash log is relevant if it shows data was accessed.
FRE 402 Admissibility of Relevant Evidence All relevant evidence is admissible unless excluded by other rules.
FRE 403 Prejudice vs. Probative Value Overly graphic forensic images or inflammatory filenames may be excluded if prejudicial effect outweighs probative value.
FRE 702 Expert Testimony Forensic examiner must be qualified and use reliable methodology. Governs all expert opinions about digital evidence.
FRE 901 Authentication Must prove evidence is what proponent claims. Hash comparison authenticates digital copies.
FRE 902 Self-Authentication Certified digital copies with proper documentation can be self-authenticating under 902(14).

Hash Verification: The Integrity Anchor

Cryptographic hashing is the mechanism that binds digital evidence to its chain of custody. A hash function takes arbitrary input and produces a fixed-length digest. If even one bit changes, the hash changes completely — this is the avalanche effect.

SHA-256 in Practice

SHA-256 produces a 256-bit (64 hex character) digest. It is the current standard for forensic evidence integrity. MD5 and SHA-1 are considered deprecated for this purpose due to known collision vulnerabilities, though MD5 is still sometimes used as a secondary verification.

# Generate SHA-256 hash of a forensic disk image sha256sum evidence_drive.dd # Output: 3a7bd3e2...64_hex_chars... evidence_drive.dd # Verify against a known hash echo "3a7bd3e2...expected_hash... evidence_drive.dd" | sha256sum -c # Output: evidence_drive.dd: OK # Hash a file on Windows with PowerShell Get-FileHash -Algorithm SHA256 .\evidence_drive.dd # Hash multiple files and save manifest sha256sum *.dd > evidence_manifest.sha256 # Verify all files against manifest sha256sum -c evidence_manifest.sha256

Critical Practice: Always hash original media before imaging, hash the image after creation, and compare both values. Document both hashes in your chain of custody form. Any mismatch means the evidence may be compromised.

Hash Algorithm Comparison

Algorithm Digest Size Forensic Status Notes
MD5 128 bits (32 hex) Deprecated as primary Known collision attacks since 2004. Still used as secondary check.
SHA-1 160 bits (40 hex) Deprecated Practical collision demonstrated (SHAttered, 2017).
SHA-256 256 bits (64 hex) Current standard No known practical attacks. NIST recommended.
SHA-3-256 256 bits (64 hex) Acceptable Different construction (Keccak). Used as future-proofing.

Admissibility Framework

For digital evidence to be admitted in court, it must satisfy five criteria:

Criterion Requirement How to Satisfy
Authenticity Evidence is what it claims to be Hash verification, chain of custody, witness testimony
Reliability Collection method is trustworthy Validated tools, documented procedures, write blockers
Completeness Evidence is presented in full context Full disk images (not cherry-picked files), complete logs
Relevance Evidence relates to the case Clear connection between evidence and facts at issue
Believability Jury/judge can understand it Expert testimony, demonstrative aids, clear reports

Practice: Evidence Classification Exercise

For each item below, determine which evidence category (Real, Demonstrative, Documentary, Testimonial) it belongs to and which FRE rule is most relevant:

  1. A seized laptop hard drive with fingerprints preserved
  2. A timeline chart showing file access patterns created by the forensic examiner
  3. Apache web server access logs exported from the compromised server
  4. An examiner's statement about finding malware signatures in memory
  5. A forensic image file (.E01) with SHA-256 verification
  6. A network diagram showing lateral movement paths used by the attacker
  7. An email retrieved from the suspect's Outlook PST file
  8. A USB drive found in the suspect's desk drawer

Answers: 1. Real (FRE 901) | 2. Demonstrative (FRE 401-403) | 3. Documentary (FRE 901/902) | 4. Testimonial (FRE 702) | 5. Documentary (FRE 902(14)) | 6. Demonstrative (FRE 401-403) | 7. Documentary (FRE 901) | 8. Real (FRE 901)

Key Takeaways

  • Four evidence types: Real (tangible), Demonstrative (illustrative), Documentary (records), Testimonial (sworn statements)
  • FRE 401-403 govern relevance and prejudice balancing; FRE 702 governs expert testimony; FRE 901-902 govern authentication
  • SHA-256 is the current forensic standard for evidence integrity verification
  • Always hash before and after imaging, and document both values in the chain of custody
  • Digital copies verified by cryptographic hash are generally treated as equivalent to originals under FRE 1003
