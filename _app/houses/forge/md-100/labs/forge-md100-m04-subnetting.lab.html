Task 1: Understanding Subnets

Before splitting networks, you need to understand what a subnet is and why we use them. Read the concepts below, then answer three questions to continue.

What is a Subnet?
A subnet (subnetwork) is a logically segmented portion of a larger IP network. Instead of every device on a building's network being able to talk directly to every other device, subnetting divides the network into smaller, isolated groups.
Think of it like floors in an office building. All floors share the same building address, but each floor is a separate working area with its own internal layout.
Why Split Networks?
Subnetting gives you three important benefits:

Security — Devices on different subnets cannot communicate directly without going through a router. Your guest WiFi cannot reach your finance servers.

Performance — Broadcast traffic (messages sent to every device) is contained within a subnet. Smaller subnets = less broadcast noise across the whole network.

Organization — Logical grouping by department, function, or security level makes the network easier to manage and audit.
The /25 Subnet Mask
A /24 network (255.255.255.0) gives you 256 total addresses — 254 usable hosts. When you change to /25 (255.255.255.128), you borrow one bit from the host portion, splitting the /24 exactly in half.
/24 mask: 11111111. 11111111. 11111111. 00000000
/25 mask: 11111111. 11111111. 11111111. 10000000 = 255.255.255.128
Each /25 subnet holds 128 addresses: 1 network address, 126 usable hosts, and 1 broadcast address.

Task 2: Split the Network

Your office has two departments: Office Staff and Guest WiFi. Split the 192.168.1.0/24 network into two equal /25 subnets and complete the address table for each one.

How the Split Works
Starting network: 192.168.1.0/24 (256 addresses total).

Adding the /25 mask (255.255.255.128) splits it at the midpoint — address 128. The first half runs from .0 to .127, and the second half runs from .128 to .255.
Rule: The network address is the first address in the block (cannot be assigned to a device). The broadcast address is the last address (cannot be assigned either). Every address in between is a usable host.
