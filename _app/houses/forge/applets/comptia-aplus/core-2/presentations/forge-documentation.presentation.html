Why Documentation Matters

Good documentation is the foundation of effective IT support and operations. Without it, organizations rely on tribal knowledge that walks out the door when employees leave.

Benefits of Proper Documentation

  • Consistency: Everyone follows the same procedures, reducing errors
  • Training: New team members get up to speed faster with written guides
  • Troubleshooting: Historical data reveals patterns and helps diagnose recurring issues
  • Compliance: Required for audits, regulatory requirements (HIPAA, SOX, PCI-DSS)
  • Continuity: Knowledge survives employee turnover and organizational changes
  • Accountability: Clear record of who did what, when, and why
  • Legal protection: Documentation can demonstrate due diligence in disputes
Reality Check: Poor documentation costs organizations millions in downtime, repeated mistakes, and lost productivity. The best time to document is while you're working - not after the fact.

Diagram Best Practices

  • Use standard symbols and a legend for consistency
  • Include version number and last-updated date
  • Store in a centralized, accessible location (not just one person's computer)
  • Update immediately when changes are made - outdated diagrams are dangerous
Critical: An outdated network diagram can be worse than no diagram at all. It leads to incorrect assumptions during troubleshooting and change planning.

Asset Inventory & Management

Hardware Assets

  • Serial numbers and asset tag IDs
  • Make, model, specifications
  • Purchase date and cost
  • Warranty status and expiration
  • Location and assigned user
  • Maintenance and repair history
  • End-of-life and disposal date

Software Assets

  • License keys and activation codes
  • License type (per-user, per-device, site)
  • Total licenses owned vs. deployed
  • Version numbers installed
  • Renewal dates and costs
  • Vendor contact information
  • Compliance audit status

Configuration Documentation

  • Baseline configurations: Standard settings and software for each device type/role
  • Change history: Chronological record of all modifications with dates and reasons
  • Password vault: Secure, encrypted storage for shared credentials (NEVER in plaintext, spreadsheets, or sticky notes)

Ticketing systems track, prioritize, and manage IT support requests throughout their lifecycle.

Essential Ticket Information

  • User details: Name, contact info, department, location
  • Problem description: Clear, detailed issue statement in the user's words
  • Priority/Severity: Impact and urgency level (P1 through P4)
  • Category: Hardware, software, network, account, etc.
  • Work notes: All troubleshooting steps attempted with results
  • Resolution: Exact steps that fixed the issue (for future reference)
  • Time tracking: Time spent on each interaction for SLA compliance
Golden Rule: If it's not in the ticket, it didn't happen. Document every interaction, every troubleshooting step, every communication.

Priority Matrix

SLA (Service Level Agreement)

  • Response time: Maximum time to acknowledge and begin working on a ticket
  • Resolution time: Maximum time to fully resolve the issue
  • Uptime guarantee: Percentage of time services must be available (99.9% = 8.76 hrs downtime/year)
  • Escalation path: When and how to escalate if SLA thresholds are approaching

Escalation Procedures

Know when and how to escalate issues to higher support tiers or management.

Escalation Triggers

Trigger TypeWhen to EscalateEscalate To
Time-basedIssue exceeds SLA response or resolution timeNext tier or manager
Skill-basedIssue requires expertise beyond your levelSenior technician or specialist
Authority-basedDecision requires management approval (budget, policy)IT manager or director
Severity-basedMajor outage affecting many users or critical systemsIncident response team
Customer-requestedUser specifically asks to speak with a supervisorTeam lead or manager

Proper Escalation Includes

  • Complete documentation of all troubleshooting steps already performed
  • Clear explanation of why escalation is needed
  • All relevant logs, screenshots, error messages, and system information
  • User contact information and availability for follow-up
Never: Escalate a blank ticket. The next tier should never have to repeat your troubleshooting steps. Document everything before you escalate.

Knowledge Bases & Wikis

A knowledge base (KB) is a centralized, searchable repository of solutions and information for resolving common issues.

Types of KB Articles

  • How-to guides: Step-by-step instructions for common tasks (setup VPN, map printer)
  • Troubleshooting guides: Decision trees for diagnosing specific problems
  • FAQs: Answers to frequently asked questions from end users
  • Known issues: Documented bugs with workarounds until a permanent fix is available
  • Reference docs: Policies, procedures, contact lists, vendor information

Writing Effective KB Articles

  • Use clear, searchable titles that describe the problem/solution
  • Include symptoms, cause, and solution in every article
  • Add screenshots and step-by-step visuals
  • Keep language simple and free of unnecessary jargon
  • Include the environment/version information (OS, application version)
  • Review and update regularly; retire outdated articles
Self-Service: A good knowledge base can reduce ticket volume by 20-40% by enabling users to solve common issues themselves.

SOPs & Change Documentation

Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) are documented, step-by-step instructions for routine operational tasks.

Change Documentation

  • Record what was changed, when, by whom, and why
  • Include before and after configuration states
  • Reference the change request or ticket number
  • Update all affected documentation (diagrams, SOPs, KB articles)

Regulatory Compliance

IT technicians must understand and comply with regulations that govern data handling and privacy.

RegulationGovernsKey Requirements
HIPAAHealthcare data (PHI)Encrypt PHI, access controls, audit trails, breach notification
PCI-DSSCredit card dataEncrypt cardholder data, restrict access, regular testing
SOXFinancial recordsInternal controls, data integrity, audit trails
GDPREU citizen personal dataConsent, right to erasure, data breach notification in 72 hours
FERPAStudent education recordsParental consent, limited disclosure, student rights

Technician Responsibilities

  • Follow data handling procedures specific to your organization's compliance requirements
  • Never access data beyond what's needed for your job (principle of least privilege)
  • Report any suspected data breach immediately through proper channels
  • Properly dispose of regulated data using approved methods (shredding, secure wipe)
  • Complete required compliance training on schedule

Data Disposal & Sanitization

Proper data disposal is a compliance requirement and a critical security practice. Simply deleting files or formatting a drive does NOT remove data.

Disposal Methods by Media Type

MethodDescriptionBest For
Standard WipeOverwriting all data with zeros or random patterns (1-3 passes)HDDs being reused internally
Secure EraseBuilt-in firmware command that resets all storage cellsSSDs (standard wipe is ineffective on SSDs due to wear leveling)
DegaussingStrong magnetic field destroys data on magnetic mediaHDDs, tapes (NOT SSDs - they don't use magnetic storage)
Physical DestructionShredding, drilling, incineration of the storage deviceAny media with highly sensitive data requiring guaranteed destruction
Crypto EraseDestroy the encryption key, making encrypted data unrecoverableSelf-encrypting drives (SEDs)

Documentation Requirements

  • Certificate of destruction: Formal record from vendor confirming data was destroyed
  • Asset tracking: Update inventory to reflect device disposal date and method
  • Compliance audit trail: Maintain records for regulatory requirements (retention periods vary)
Common Mistake: Degaussing does NOT work on SSDs or flash storage. SSDs require secure erase commands or physical destruction. Using the wrong method leaves data recoverable.

Professional Communication

Effective communication is as important as technical skills in IT support. Users judge IT by their experience with you.

Active Listening

  • Let users finish speaking before responding - don't interrupt
  • Ask clarifying questions: "Can you describe exactly what happens when...?"
  • Paraphrase back: "So what you're saying is..." to confirm understanding
  • Take notes: Write down key details during the conversation
  • Avoid assumptions: Don't jump to conclusions before gathering all facts

Setting Expectations

  • Give honest time estimates - under-promise and over-deliver
  • Explain what you're doing in terms the user can understand
  • If you need to research, tell the user and give a callback time
  • Keep users updated on progress, especially for longer issues
  • If you can't solve it, explain what you've tried and what happens next (escalation)

Communication: Do's and Don'ts

Professional Responses

  • "I understand this is frustrating. Let me help."
  • "Let me explain what's happening in simple terms."
  • "I'll have an update for you by 3 PM."
  • "That's a great question. Here's what I know..."
  • "I need to research this. Can I call you back in an hour?"
  • "I'll make sure this gets resolved for you."

Unprofessional Responses

  • "That's not my job / not my department."
  • "You should have known that already."
  • "That's a stupid question."
  • "I don't know." (without offering to find out)
  • "It works fine on my machine."
  • "Just Google it."

Phone and Email Etiquette

  • Phone: Identify yourself, speak clearly, avoid background noise, summarize before hanging up
  • Email: Use clear subject lines, be concise, proofread before sending, respond within SLA
  • Remote support: Narrate what you're doing on the user's screen, ask permission before accessing
  • Voicemail: Leave name, callback number, brief description, and availability

Professional Appearance & Attitude

Professional Appearance

  • Dress code: Follow company policy; when in doubt, dress one level up
  • ID badge: Always visible when on-site or at customer locations
  • Grooming: Clean, neat, and appropriate for the workplace
  • Body language: Make eye contact, maintain confident posture, offer a friendly demeanor

Professional Attitude

  • Punctuality: Be on time for appointments, meetings, and scheduled calls
  • Respect: Treat all users equally regardless of their role or technical level
  • Accountability: Own your mistakes, learn from them, and don't blame others
  • Confidentiality: Never discuss a user's issues, data, or problems with others who don't need to know
  • Positive attitude: View problems as opportunities to help, not interruptions
  • Follow through: Do what you say you'll do, when you say you'll do it
Remember: You represent the entire IT department. Every single interaction shapes how users perceive IT. One bad experience can undermine trust built over months.

Cultural Sensitivity & Awareness

In today's diverse workplace, cultural sensitivity is essential for effective communication and professional relationships.

Key Principles

  • Respect differences: People from different backgrounds may have different communication styles, personal space preferences, and customs
  • Avoid assumptions: Don't assume technical knowledge level based on age, gender, role, or background
  • Use inclusive language: Avoid slang, idioms, and jargon that may not translate across cultures
  • Be patient: Language barriers require extra patience and clarity, not frustration
  • Adapt your approach: Some cultures prefer formal communication; others are more casual

Practical Guidelines

  • Address people by their preferred name and pronouns
  • Be aware of different holidays, work schedules, and time zones
  • When communicating across languages, speak clearly and at moderate pace
  • Use written follow-ups for complex verbal discussions
  • If unsure about a cultural practice, ask respectfully rather than assume

De-escalation Techniques

  • Never argue, get defensive, or take it personally
  • Apologize for the inconvenience (not admitting fault for the issue)
  • Focus on what you CAN do, not what you can't
  • Offer options when possible to give the user a sense of control
  • Know when to involve a supervisor - this is a sign of maturity, not weakness

Scenario: Handling a Difficult User

Scenario

A VP calls the help desk furious. Their laptop won't connect to the projector for a board meeting starting in 10 minutes. They say: "I don't care about your process - just FIX IT NOW! I've told you people before this laptop has issues!"

Effective Response Strategy

  1. Acknowledge urgency: "I completely understand you have a critical meeting. Let me help you right now."
  2. Take ownership: "I'm going to stay with you until this is working."
  3. Solve immediately: Try quick fixes first (different cable, restart display driver, use a loaner laptop)
  4. Follow up: After the meeting, address the root cause of the recurring laptop issues
  5. Document: Create a ticket capturing the issue, resolution, and recurring nature for escalation
Key Takeaway: Prioritize the user's immediate need (the meeting), then address the underlying technical issue. Never let process get in the way of urgent business needs.

On-Site Visit Etiquette

Before Arriving

  • Confirm appointment time and expected duration
  • Gather all tools and parts you might need
  • Review the ticket and any previous notes on the issue
  • Call ahead if you'll be late

At the User's Workspace

  • Introduce yourself and explain what you're there to do
  • Ask permission before touching the user's computer or belongings
  • Minimize disruption: Work quietly, don't rearrange their desk more than necessary
  • Protect privacy: Don't look at personal files, emails, or photos on screen
  • Clean up: Leave the workspace as clean as (or cleaner than) you found it
  • Explain what you did: Give the user a brief summary before leaving

After the Visit

  • Update the ticket with complete documentation of the visit and resolution
  • Follow up if you promised to check on anything
  • If parts were ordered, communicate expected delivery timeline

Time Management

Prioritization Strategies

  • Impact assessment: How many users are affected? Is business stopped?
  • SLA deadlines: Which tickets are closest to breaching their SLA?
  • Quick wins: If a 2-minute fix can close a ticket, do it between larger tasks
  • VIP awareness: Executive issues may need priority handling per company policy

Productivity Tips

  • Batch similar tasks: Handle all password resets together, all printer issues together
  • Avoid multitasking: Focus on one ticket completely before starting the next
  • Block time: Reserve focus time for complex projects; turn off notifications
  • Document as you go: Don't save documentation for later - you'll forget details
  • Use templates: Pre-written responses for common issues save significant time
  • Know when to escalate: Spending 2 hours on something a senior tech can fix in 10 minutes is inefficient
Rule of Thumb: If you've spent 30 minutes troubleshooting without progress, step back and reconsider your approach. Research, ask a colleague, or escalate.

Chapter Summary

  • Documentation provides consistency, training, troubleshooting history, and compliance
  • Network diagrams: Physical (hardware), Logical (IP/VLANs), Wiring, Floor plans - keep them updated
  • Asset management: Track hardware and software with serial numbers, licenses, warranty, and location
  • Ticketing systems: Document every interaction; if it's not in the ticket, it didn't happen
  • Knowledge base: Searchable repository of solutions that reduces ticket volume
  • SOPs: Step-by-step procedures ensuring consistent, reliable operations
  • Compliance: Understand HIPAA, PCI-DSS, GDPR, and handle regulated data appropriately
  • Active listening: Let users finish, paraphrase, ask clarifying questions
  • Difficult customers: Acknowledge frustration, stay calm, focus on solutions, escalate when needed
  • Time management: Prioritize by impact, batch similar tasks, document as you work
