📋 What is Change Management?

Change management is a structured process for proposing, reviewing, approving, and implementing changes to IT systems in a controlled manner.

Why It Matters

  • Minimize downtime: Planned changes reduce unexpected outages
  • Reduce risk: Review process catches potential problems before implementation
  • Maintain stability: Controlled changes keep systems reliable
  • Ensure accountability: Clear records of who changed what and why
  • Compliance: Many regulations require documented change processes
Real-World Impact: According to studies, 80% of unplanned downtime is caused by poorly planned changes or human error during changes.

📊 Types of Changes

Type Description Approval Example
Standard Pre-approved, low-risk, routine changes Pre-authorized Password reset, user account creation
Normal Planned changes requiring review CAB review Server upgrade, software deployment
Emergency Urgent changes to fix critical issues Expedited Security patch for active exploit
Key Point: Even emergency changes should be documented and reviewed after implementation. "Emergency" doesn't mean "skip documentation."

👥 Change Advisory Board (CAB)

The CAB is a group of stakeholders who review, assess, and approve or reject proposed changes.

Typical CAB Members

  • IT Manager: Overall accountability for IT operations
  • Technical Lead: Assesses technical feasibility and impact
  • Security Officer: Reviews security implications
  • Business Representative: Evaluates business impact
  • Service Desk: Understands user impact and support needs

CAB Responsibilities

  • Review all non-standard change requests
  • Assess risks and schedule implementation windows
  • Approve, reject, or request more information
  • Prioritize changes based on business needs

📝 Request for Change (RFC)

An RFC is a formal document that requests approval for a proposed change.

🎯 Impact Analysis

Impact analysis evaluates how a change will affect systems, users, and business operations.

Questions to Answer

  • Systems: What servers, applications, or networks are affected?
  • Users: How many users will experience disruption?
  • Dependencies: What other systems rely on the affected component?
  • Duration: How long will the change take?
  • Downtime: Will services be unavailable during implementation?
Scope Creep Warning: Changes can have unexpected cascading effects. Always map dependencies before implementation.

⚠️ Risk Assessment

Every change carries risk. Risk assessment identifies what could go wrong and how severe the consequences would be.

Risk Matrix

Likelihood / Impact Low Impact Medium Impact High Impact
High Likelihood Medium Risk High Risk Critical Risk
Medium Likelihood Low Risk Medium Risk High Risk
Low Likelihood Low Risk Low Risk Medium Risk
Risk Mitigation: For high-risk changes, consider additional testing, smaller rollout phases, or scheduling during low-usage periods.

↩️ Rollback Plans

A rollback plan documents how to reverse a change if something goes wrong.

Rollback Requirements

  • Backups: Full backup before any change
  • Documentation: Step-by-step reversal procedure
  • Timeline: How long rollback will take
  • Decision point: When to trigger rollback (criteria)
  • Testing: Verify rollback actually works
Critical Rule: NEVER implement a change without a tested rollback plan. If you can't undo it, don't do it until you can.

Rollback Triggers

  • Critical services fail to start
  • Error rate exceeds threshold
  • Performance degradation beyond acceptable limits
  • Data integrity issues detected

🧪 Sandbox & Test Environments

A sandbox is an isolated environment where changes can be tested without affecting production systems.

Environment Types

EnvironmentPurposeData
Development Building and coding new features Synthetic/test data
Test/QA Functional testing of changes Sanitized production copy
Staging Final validation before production Production-like data
Production Live systems serving users Real business data
Best Practice: Changes should flow: Dev → Test → Staging → Production. Never skip environments for "quick fixes."

📚 Documentation Requirements

Before the Change

  • RFC: Complete request with all sections
  • Approval records: CAB meeting minutes, approver signatures
  • Communication plan: Who to notify and when

During the Change

  • Implementation log: Timestamped record of each step
  • Issues encountered: Any problems and resolutions
  • Deviations: Any departures from the plan

After the Change

  • Completion report: Final status and outcomes
  • Lessons learned: What worked, what didn't
  • Updated documentation: Network diagrams, procedures, etc.

✅ End-User Acceptance

End-user acceptance confirms that the change meets business requirements and users can perform their work.

Acceptance Criteria

  • All stated requirements are met
  • No regression in existing functionality
  • Performance meets or exceeds baseline
  • Users trained on any new procedures
  • Support documentation updated
Note: Get acceptance in writing. A formal sign-off protects both IT and the business stakeholders.

🎯 Chapter Summary

  • Change management = structured process to control IT changes
  • Three types: Standard (pre-approved), Normal (CAB review), Emergency (expedited)
  • CAB: Stakeholder group that reviews and approves changes
  • RFC: Formal change request with description, justification, plan, and rollback
  • Impact analysis: Identify affected systems, users, and dependencies
  • Risk assessment: Evaluate likelihood and impact of potential problems
  • Rollback plan: Always have a tested way to undo changes
  • Sandbox testing: Validate changes in isolated environments first
  • Documentation: Record everything before, during, and after
  • End-user acceptance: Confirm change meets business needs
