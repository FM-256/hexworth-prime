📋 What is Change Management?

Change management is a structured process for proposing, reviewing, approving, and implementing changes to IT systems in a controlled, documented manner.

Why It Matters

  • Minimize downtime: Planned changes reduce unexpected outages and service disruptions
  • Reduce risk: Review process catches potential problems before implementation
  • Maintain stability: Controlled changes keep production systems reliable and predictable
  • Ensure accountability: Clear records of who changed what, when, and why
  • Compliance: Many regulations (SOX, HIPAA, PCI-DSS) require documented change processes
  • Communication: All stakeholders are informed of changes that may affect them
Real-World Impact: Studies show that 80% of unplanned downtime is caused by poorly planned changes or human error during changes. Change management exists to prevent this.

Key Principles

  • Every change to production systems should go through the change management process
  • Changes should be reviewed by multiple stakeholders, not decided by one person
  • Documentation must be maintained at every stage
  • Testing in non-production environments before deployment is mandatory
  • Every change must have a rollback plan in case of failure

📊 Types of Changes

Type Description Approval Required Example
Standard Pre-approved, low-risk, routine changes that follow established procedures Pre-authorized (no CAB needed) Password reset, new user account, workstation setup
Normal Planned changes that require review and approval before implementation CAB review and approval Server upgrade, new software deployment, network reconfiguration
Emergency Urgent changes needed to fix critical issues or respond to active threats Expedited approval (ECAB) Critical security patch, active exploit remediation, service restoration
Key Point: Even emergency changes must be documented and reviewed after implementation. "Emergency" does not mean "skip documentation." It means the approval process is accelerated.
Exam Alert: If an exam question describes a routine task with a pre-defined procedure, it's a standard change. If it needs CAB review, it's a normal change. If it's urgent and critical, it's an emergency change.

👥 Change Advisory Board (CAB)

The CAB is a group of stakeholders who review, assess, and approve or reject proposed changes to IT systems.

Typical CAB Members

RoleResponsibility in CAB
IT Manager / Change ManagerChairs the meeting, overall accountability for change process
Technical Lead / SMEAssesses technical feasibility, identifies risks, evaluates impact
Security OfficerReviews security implications, compliance requirements
Business RepresentativeEvaluates business impact, confirms timing works for operations
Service Desk ManagerAssesses user impact, prepares support team for changes
Application OwnerConfirms application-specific requirements and testing needs

CAB Responsibilities

  • Review all non-standard (normal) change requests
  • Assess risks, dependencies, and schedule implementation windows
  • Approve, reject, or request more information on each RFC
  • Prioritize changes based on business needs and available resources
  • Review emergency changes after the fact (post-implementation review)

An RFC is a formal document that requests approval for a proposed change. It must contain enough detail for the CAB to make an informed decision.

🧑‍💼 Responsible Staff Member

Every change must have a designated responsible staff member who owns the change from planning through completion.

Responsibilities

  • Submit the RFC: Write and submit the complete change request documentation
  • Present to CAB: Explain the change and answer questions during the review
  • Coordinate resources: Ensure all necessary personnel, tools, and access are available
  • Execute or oversee: Either perform the change directly or supervise the implementation team
  • Communicate status: Keep stakeholders informed before, during, and after the change
  • Verify success: Confirm the change works as expected and meets acceptance criteria
  • Trigger rollback: Make the call to roll back if the change fails or causes issues
  • Complete documentation: Ensure all post-change documentation is finished
Accountability: Having one responsible person prevents the "I thought someone else was handling it" problem. Even if multiple people work on a change, one person is ultimately accountable for its success.

🔗 Affected Systems & Dependencies

Before any change, you must identify every system, service, and user group that could be impacted.

Mapping Dependencies

  • Direct dependencies: Systems that directly connect to or rely on the changed component
  • Indirect dependencies: Systems that depend on the direct dependencies (cascading effects)
  • User impact: Which users, departments, or locations will be affected
  • Service impact: Which business services could experience degradation or outage

Documentation Requirements

ItemWhat to Document
ServersHostname, IP, OS, applications running, role
NetworkSwitches, routers, VLANs, firewall rules affected
ApplicationsSoftware name, version, database connections, integrations
UsersNumber affected, departments, VIP users to notify
External servicesCloud services, vendor connections, API integrations
Scope Creep Warning: Changes can have unexpected cascading effects. A "simple" DNS change can break email, VPN, web services, and application authentication simultaneously. Always map the full dependency chain.

🎯 Impact Analysis

Impact analysis evaluates how a proposed change will affect systems, users, and business operations.

Questions to Answer

  • Systems: What servers, applications, or networks are affected? What are the dependencies?
  • Users: How many users will experience disruption? Are any VIPs or critical roles affected?
  • Business processes: Will any business-critical workflows be interrupted?
  • Duration: How long will the change take? How long will services be unavailable?
  • Timing: When is the least disruptive time to implement?
  • Resources: What personnel, tools, and access are required?

Impact Categories

LevelDescriptionExample
HighBusiness-critical systems, many users affectedEmail server migration, core switch replacement
MediumImportant but not critical, limited user impactDepartment printer relocation, software update
LowMinimal impact, few users or non-critical systemsAdding a new user account, updating documentation

⚠️ Risk Assessment

Every change carries risk. Risk assessment identifies what could go wrong and evaluates the severity of potential consequences.

Risk Matrix

Likelihood / Impact Low Impact Medium Impact High Impact
High Likelihood Medium Risk High Risk Critical Risk
Medium Likelihood Low Risk Medium Risk High Risk
Low Likelihood Low Risk Low Risk Medium Risk

Risk Mitigation Strategies

  • Additional testing: More thorough validation in test environments
  • Phased rollout: Deploy to a small group first, then expand
  • Off-hours implementation: Schedule during low-usage periods
  • Enhanced monitoring: Increase logging and alerting during and after the change
  • Backup/snapshot: Create full backups immediately before the change

↩️ Rollback Plans

A rollback plan documents exactly how to reverse a change if something goes wrong. Every change MUST have one.

Rollback Plan Components

  • Full backup: Complete backup taken immediately before implementation begins
  • Step-by-step reversal: Detailed procedure to undo every change made
  • Timeline: How long the rollback will take to complete
  • Decision point: Clear criteria for when to trigger the rollback
  • Decision authority: Who has the authority to order the rollback
  • Verification: How to confirm the rollback was successful
  • Communication: Who to notify if rollback is triggered

Rollback Triggers

  • Critical services fail to start or respond after the change
  • Error rate exceeds the defined acceptable threshold
  • Performance degradation beyond acceptable limits
  • Data integrity issues detected
  • Change exceeds the approved maintenance window
Critical Rule: NEVER implement a change without a tested rollback plan. If you cannot undo the change, do not proceed until you can. This is non-negotiable.

🕐 Change Windows & Scheduling

A change window (maintenance window) is the approved time period during which changes can be implemented.

Change Window Considerations

  • Low-usage periods: Nights, weekends, or holidays when fewer users are affected
  • Business cycles: Avoid end-of-month, end-of-quarter, or other critical business periods
  • Blackout periods: Times when NO changes are allowed (tax season for accounting systems, holiday season for retail)
  • Time zones: Consider all affected time zones for global organizations
  • Staff availability: Ensure the implementation team AND support staff are available

Window Components

PhaseActivityTime Allocation
Pre-changeBackups, pre-flight checks, communication15-30 minutes
ImplementationExecute the change stepsVaries by change
TestingVerify the change works correctly15-60 minutes
Rollback bufferReserved time in case rollback is neededEqual to implementation time
Planning Rule: Always include rollback buffer time in your maintenance window. If the change takes 2 hours to implement, you need at least 4 hours total (2 for implementation + testing, 2 for potential rollback).

🧪 Sandbox & Test Environments

A sandbox is an isolated environment where changes can be tested without affecting production systems or real data.

Environment Progression

EnvironmentPurposeData UsedWho Uses It
Development (Dev) Building and coding new features Synthetic/mock data Developers
Test/QA Functional and integration testing Sanitized production copy QA team, testers
Staging (Pre-Prod) Final validation, mirrors production Production-like data IT ops, business testers
Production (Prod) Live systems serving real users Real business data End users
Golden Rule: Changes flow one direction: Dev → Test → Staging → Production. Never skip environments for "quick fixes." The one time you skip testing is the one time it breaks production.

Sandbox Benefits

  • Catch bugs and configuration errors before they affect real users
  • Train staff on new systems without risk to production
  • Test rollback procedures in a safe environment
  • Validate performance under simulated load conditions

✅ End-User Acceptance Testing

End-user acceptance testing (UAT) confirms that the change meets business requirements and users can perform their work effectively.

UAT Process

1
Deploy Change
2
User Testing
3
Collect Feedback
4
Fix Issues
5
Sign-off

Acceptance Criteria

  • All stated business requirements and objectives are met
  • No regression in existing functionality (nothing that worked before is now broken)
  • Performance meets or exceeds the established baseline
  • Users are trained on any new procedures or interface changes
  • Support documentation is updated and available
  • Known issues are documented with workarounds (if any)
Get It in Writing: A formal sign-off from the business stakeholder protects both IT and the business. It confirms that the change met expectations and prevents "that's not what I asked for" disputes later.

📢 Communication Plan

Every change needs a communication plan that keeps all stakeholders informed at every stage.

Communication Timeline

WhenWhat to CommunicateWho to Notify
1-2 weeks beforeInitial notification of upcoming change, purpose, and expected impactAll affected users, management
1-3 days beforeReminder with specific date, time, duration, and what to expectAll affected users, support team
Day of (before)Final reminder, confirm start time, provide contact for issuesAll affected users
During changeStatus updates if extended or if issues ariseManagement, support team
After completionConfirmation that change is complete, any required user actionsAll affected users, management

Communication Methods

  • Email: Primary notification method for planned changes
  • Intranet/portal: Post maintenance calendar and change announcements
  • Ticketing system: Automatically notify affected users from the change ticket
  • Digital signage: Display upcoming maintenance on office screens
  • Emergency: Phone/SMS for urgent communications during unplanned issues

📚 Documentation Requirements

Before the Change

  • Complete RFC with all sections filled
  • CAB meeting minutes and approval records
  • Approver signatures or electronic approval
  • Communication plan and notifications sent
  • Pre-change backup verification
  • Current system configuration snapshot

During the Change

  • Timestamped implementation log of each step
  • Any issues encountered and how they were resolved
  • Deviations from the original plan
  • Test results at each verification point
  • Communication records sent to stakeholders
  • Rollback decision points reached (if any)

After the Change

  • Completion report: Final status, actual vs. planned timeline, outcomes
  • Lessons learned: What worked well, what could be improved
  • Updated documentation: Network diagrams, SOPs, configuration docs, KB articles
  • User acceptance sign-off: Written confirmation from business stakeholder
  • Post-implementation review: Scheduled check-in to verify long-term stability

🎭 Scenario: Change Gone Wrong

Scenario

Your team is upgrading the company's email server on Saturday night. The maintenance window is 10 PM to 2 AM. At 11:30 PM, after completing the upgrade, email is not flowing. Users who test from home report they cannot send or receive email. The rollback plan involves restoring from the pre-change backup.

Correct Response

  1. Don't panic. You have a rollback plan and time in the window.
  2. Quick troubleshoot (15 minutes max): Check DNS records, mail flow configuration, firewall rules, certificates.
  3. Decision point at midnight: If not resolved, trigger the rollback to restore the previous working state.
  4. Execute rollback: Follow the documented reversal procedure step by step.
  5. Verify services: Confirm email is flowing normally after rollback.
  6. Communicate: Notify management that the change was rolled back and email is restored.
  7. Post-mortem: Investigate what went wrong, fix the issue in test, reschedule the change.
Key Lesson: A successful rollback is NOT a failure - it's the change management process working correctly. The failure would be pushing through a broken change and leaving email down until Monday.

🎯 Chapter Summary

  • Change management = structured process to control IT changes and minimize risk
  • Three types: Standard (pre-approved), Normal (CAB review), Emergency (expedited)
  • CAB: Stakeholder group that reviews risks, impact, and approves changes
  • RFC: Formal request with description, justification, plan, rollback, schedule, and testing
  • Responsible staff member: One designated person accountable for each change
  • Affected systems: Map all dependencies including indirect and cascading impacts
  • Risk assessment: Evaluate likelihood and impact; use risk matrix for classification
  • Rollback plan: Mandatory for every change; define triggers and reversal steps
  • Change windows: Schedule during low-usage periods; include rollback buffer time
  • Environment progression: Dev → Test → Staging → Production (never skip)
  • UAT: Business users verify the change meets requirements before sign-off
  • Documentation: Record everything before, during, and after the change
