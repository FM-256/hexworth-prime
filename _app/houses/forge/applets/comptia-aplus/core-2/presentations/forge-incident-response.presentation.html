⚠️ What is an Incident?

A security incident is any event that threatens the confidentiality, integrity, or availability (CIA triad) of information systems or data.

Common Incident Types

  • Malware infection: Virus, ransomware, worm, or other malicious software detected
  • Unauthorized access: Someone accessing systems or data without permission
  • Data breach: Sensitive data exposed, stolen, or leaked externally
  • Denial of Service (DoS): System or network made intentionally unavailable
  • Social engineering: Successful phishing, vishing, or other deception attacks
  • Physical security breach: Unauthorized physical access to restricted areas
  • Insider threat: Employee or contractor misusing their authorized access
  • Policy violation: Users violating acceptable use or security policies
Key Point: Not every event is an incident. A failed login attempt is an event. 500 failed login attempts in 10 minutes against one account is an incident. Context and impact determine severity.

Report immediately: When in doubt, report suspicious activity. False alarms are always better than missed incidents. Organizations prefer over-reporting to under-reporting.

🔢 Incident Severity Levels

Organizations classify incidents by severity to determine response priority, resource allocation, and communication requirements.

LevelDescriptionResponseExample
Critical (Sev 1)Complete business shutdown or major data breachAll hands, executive notification, 24/7 responseRansomware encrypting production servers
High (Sev 2)Significant impact to key business functionsIR team activated, management notifiedActive intruder on internal network
Medium (Sev 3)Limited impact, contained to one department or systemAssigned to IR team member, trackedSingle workstation malware infection
Low (Sev 4)Minimal impact, policy violation, no data lossStandard response, document and monitorEmployee accessing blocked website

Severity Determines Action

  • Who is notified: Higher severity = more stakeholders (legal, PR, executives, regulators)
  • Response speed: Critical incidents demand immediate response; low severity can wait until business hours
  • Resources committed: Critical may pull entire team; low may be single analyst
  • Communication frequency: Sev 1 may require hourly updates to leadership
Exam Tip: Severity can change during an investigation. A Sev 3 malware infection can become Sev 1 if the malware is found spreading laterally across the network. Continuously re-evaluate severity as new information emerges.

📋 The 6-Phase IR Process

The industry-standard incident response lifecycle. Memorize these phases and their order for the exam.

1 Preparation

Create plans, train team, gather tools before incidents occur

2 Identification

Detect and confirm that a security incident has occurred

3 Containment

Stop the spread and limit the damage of the incident

4 Eradication

Remove the threat completely from all affected systems

5 Recovery

Restore systems to normal operation and verify security

6 Lessons Learned

Document findings, improve defenses, update procedures

📦 Phase 1: Preparation

Preparation happens before any incident occurs. It determines how effectively you can respond when something goes wrong.

Preparation Activities

  • Incident Response Plan: Written procedures for handling different incident types
  • IR Team: Designated team members with defined roles and contact information
  • Communication plan: Who to contact, escalation paths, notification templates
  • Tool kit: Forensic software, write blockers, bootable USB drives, evidence bags
  • Training and drills: Regular tabletop exercises simulating incident scenarios
  • Documentation templates: Pre-built forms for incident reports, evidence logs, chain of custody

IR Tool Kit Essentials

  • Forensic imaging software (FTK Imager, dd)
  • Write blockers (hardware and software)
  • Evidence bags, labels, tamper-evident tape
  • Clean bootable media (Linux live USB, WinPE)
  • Network cable, USB drives, external hard drives
  • Documentation forms, chain of custody logs, camera

🔎 Phase 2: Identification

The goal of identification is to detect, confirm, and scope the incident as quickly as possible.

Detection Sources

SourceExamples
Automated AlertsIDS/IPS alerts, antivirus detection, SIEM correlation rules, firewall logs
User ReportsHelp desk tickets, direct reports of suspicious behavior
Routine MonitoringLog reviews, performance monitoring, vulnerability scans
External NotificationLaw enforcement, threat intelligence feeds, vendor advisories

Identification Steps

  1. Receive and log the initial alert or report
  2. Perform initial triage - is this a real incident or false alarm?
  3. Determine the scope - what systems and data are affected?
  4. Assess severity and assign priority level
  5. Notify appropriate team members and management

🔒 Phase 3: Containment

Containment limits the damage and prevents the incident from spreading further. There are two stages:

Short-Term Containment

  • Disconnect affected systems from network
  • Disable compromised user accounts
  • Block malicious IP addresses at firewall
  • Isolate affected network segments
  • Redirect traffic away from compromised servers

Long-Term Containment

  • Patch vulnerabilities on unaffected systems
  • Change compromised credentials
  • Implement additional monitoring rules
  • Set up clean replacement systems
  • Preserve evidence from contained systems
Critical Decision: Before containment, capture volatile evidence (RAM, network connections). Once you disconnect a system, volatile data may be lost forever. Balance evidence preservation with damage control.

🧹 Phases 4 & 5: Eradication & Recovery

Eradication

  • Remove malware: Clean all infected systems using antivirus/antimalware tools
  • Close attack vector: Patch the vulnerability that was exploited
  • Reset credentials: Change all potentially compromised passwords and keys
  • Rebuild if needed: Reimage systems from known-clean backups for thorough cleanup
  • Verify eradication: Scan all systems to confirm no traces of the threat remain

Recovery

  • Restore systems: Bring clean systems back online carefully, one at a time
  • Restore data: Recover data from verified clean backups
  • Verify functionality: Confirm all services and applications work correctly
  • Enhanced monitoring: Increase logging and monitoring for signs of reinfection
  • Gradual reconnection: Reconnect to network in stages, monitoring at each step
Watch Period: Monitor recovered systems closely for days or weeks. Attackers often have backup access methods and may return.

🔄 Phase 6: Lessons Learned

The lessons learned phase transforms incidents into improvements. Hold this meeting within 1-2 weeks of incident resolution while details are fresh.

Meeting Agenda Questions

  • What happened? - Complete timeline from detection to resolution
  • How was it detected? - Was detection timely? Could it have been caught sooner?
  • What worked well? - Effective responses and tools
  • What failed or was slow? - Gaps in response capabilities
  • What changes are needed? - Policy, procedure, tool, and training updates
  • What was the total impact? - Downtime, data loss, financial cost, reputation

Deliverables

  • Updated incident response procedures based on findings
  • Revised security policies if gaps were identified
  • New indicators of compromise (IOCs) added to monitoring systems
  • Additional training scheduled to address identified weaknesses
  • Final incident report for management and compliance records

🧑‍🚒 First Responder Actions

As a first responder (often an A+ technician), your immediate actions can make or break an investigation.

Immediate Steps (In Order)

  1. Stay calm - Don't panic or make hasty decisions that could destroy evidence
  2. Secure the scene - Limit physical and logical access to affected systems
  3. Don't touch - Avoid altering evidence on devices; don't "check" by logging in
  4. Document everything - Start recording observations immediately with timestamps
  5. Report up - Notify your supervisor and/or the designated incident response team
  6. Preserve state - Don't power off running systems (unless instructed by IR team)
  7. Photograph - Take photos of screens, equipment positions, and physical evidence
  8. Control access - Keep a log of everyone who enters the area
Critical: Do NOT attempt to investigate or remediate on your own unless specifically trained and authorized. Your primary job as first responder is to preserve evidence and report.

⛓️ Chain of Custody

The chain of custody is a chronological paper trail documenting every person who handled evidence from the moment of collection through presentation in court.

Chain of Custody Requirements

ElementPurposeExample
WhoName and signature of each person handling evidenceJane Smith, Digital Forensics Analyst
WhatDetailed description of the evidence itemDell Latitude 5520, S/N: ABC123, 256GB SSD
WhenDate and time of each transfer or access2024-03-15 14:32 UTC
WhereLocation where evidence was stored or transferredEvidence locker B, Room 204
WhyReason for transfer or accessForensic imaging of hard drive
Legal Importance: A broken chain of custody can make evidence inadmissible in court. Every single transfer must be documented with signatures. Even a minor gap can allow a defense attorney to challenge the evidence.

🔒 Types of Evidence

Digital Evidence

  • Hard drive images (forensic copies)
  • RAM memory dumps
  • System and application log files
  • Email messages and headers
  • Network packet captures
  • Browser history and cache
  • Registry entries
  • Malware samples

Physical Evidence

  • Computer hardware and peripherals
  • USB drives and external media
  • Printed documents
  • Access cards and badges
  • Surveillance camera footage
  • Sign-in/access logs
  • Handwritten notes
  • Mobile phones and tablets

Testimonial Evidence

  • Witness statements: Written accounts from people who observed the incident
  • Expert testimony: Technical analysis from qualified forensic professionals
  • Interviews: Documented conversations with involved parties

🛡️ Evidence Preservation Principles

Core Rules

  • Never work on the original: Always create and analyze forensic copies. The original evidence must remain pristine and unmodified.
  • Use write blockers: Hardware or software devices that prevent any data from being written to the evidence drive during imaging.
  • Hash verification: Calculate cryptographic hashes (MD5 and SHA-256) of original and copy. Matching hashes prove the copy is an exact duplicate and evidence hasn't been altered.
  • Secure storage: Lock evidence in a controlled environment with limited access. Maintain proper temperature and humidity for physical media.
  • Label everything: Tag each evidence item with case number, date, collector's name, and description.
Hash Values: Think of a hash as a digital fingerprint. If even one bit of data changes, the hash value completely changes. This proves whether evidence has been tampered with.

MD5: 128-bit hash (older, faster, some collision concerns)
SHA-256: 256-bit hash (more secure, industry standard)

💿 Forensic Imaging

A forensic image is a bit-for-bit, exact copy of a storage device, including deleted files, file slack, and unallocated space.

Forensic Image vs Regular Copy

FeatureForensic ImageRegular File Copy
Captures deleted filesYesNo
Captures unallocated spaceYesNo
Captures file slack spaceYesNo
Bit-for-bit identicalYesNo
Court admissibleYes (with chain of custody)Generally no
Tools requiredFTK Imager, dd, EnCaseFile Explorer, cp

Imaging Process

  1. Connect evidence drive through a write blocker
  2. Calculate hash of original drive (before imaging)
  3. Create forensic image using validated tool
  4. Calculate hash of forensic image
  5. Compare hashes - they must match exactly
  6. Document all steps and hash values in evidence log

⏱️ Order of Volatility

Collect evidence starting with the most volatile (easily lost) data first. This order is critical for the exam.

  • CPU registers and cache - Lost immediately when powered off; nanosecond lifespan
  • RAM (system memory) - Contains running processes, encryption keys, passwords in plaintext, network connections
  • Network state - Active connections, routing tables, ARP cache, DNS cache
  • Running processes - Currently executing programs and their memory space
  • Disk storage - Files, system logs, databases, application data
  • Remote logging data - SIEM logs, syslog servers, cloud service logs
  • Archival/backup media - Backup tapes, USB drives, external storage
Key Insight: Once you power off a system, items 1-4 are gone forever. This is why IR teams capture RAM dumps and network state before disconnecting or shutting down systems.

💻 Device Handling: Computers

If the Computer is Running

  • Do NOT power off - Volatile data will be lost
  • Photograph the screen showing current state
  • Capture RAM using forensic tools (FTK Imager, WinPmem)
  • Record running processes and network connections
  • Record logged-in users and open files
  • Only power off after volatile data is captured

If the Computer is Off

  • Do NOT power on - Could trigger malware, modify timestamps, or overwrite evidence
  • Remove the hard drive and connect via write blocker for imaging
  • Or boot from a forensic live USB to image the drive
Special Case - Full Disk Encryption: If the system uses BitLocker or similar full-disk encryption, powering off will lock the drive. Capture the decryption key from memory BEFORE shutting down.

📱 Device Handling: Mobile Devices

Immediate Actions

  • Enable Airplane mode: Prevents remote wipe commands from reaching the device
  • Use a Faraday bag: Blocks ALL wireless signals (cellular, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, NFC) for complete isolation
  • Keep the device charged: A dead battery means a locked device - you may lose access permanently
  • Don't attempt to unlock: Failed attempts could trigger data wipe on some devices (after 10 failed PIN attempts)
  • Document visible state: Photograph screen, note whether locked/unlocked, any visible notifications

⚖️ Data Preservation & Legal Holds

A legal hold (litigation hold) requires an organization to preserve all relevant data when litigation is reasonably anticipated.

Legal Hold Requirements

  • Suspend data deletion: Stop automatic purging of emails, logs, backups, and other data
  • Notify custodians: Inform all employees who may have relevant data
  • Preserve in place: Do not alter, move, or delete any potentially relevant information
  • Document scope: Define what data types, date ranges, and systems are covered

Reporting Requirements

  • Internal: Management, legal department, HR (if employee involved)
  • External: Law enforcement if criminal activity suspected
  • Regulatory: Breach notification to affected individuals and authorities (GDPR, HIPAA, state laws)
  • Insurance: Cyber insurance carrier, within policy timeframes
Spoliation: Destroying or altering evidence after a legal hold has been issued can result in severe legal penalties, including adverse inference (the court assumes the destroyed evidence was unfavorable).
