Cables & Connectors
USB -- HDMI -- DisplayPort -- Ethernet -- SATA -- Thunderbolt -- Fiber

USB Generations
Universal Serial Bus -- the most common connector family in computing. Each generation brings faster speeds.
USB 2.0
480 Mbps. Still found on keyboards, mice, printers. Black connector insert. Backwards compatible with USB 1.1.
USB 3.0 (3.2 Gen 1)
5 Gbps -- 10x faster than 2.0. Blue connector insert is the giveaway. Extra pins inside the Type-A connector.
USB 3.1 (3.2 Gen 2)
10 Gbps. Teal/red connector common. Uses the same physical connectors as 3.0 but doubles bandwidth.
USB 3.2 / USB4
20-40 Gbps. USB4 requires Type-C connector and is based on Thunderbolt 3. Tunnels DisplayPort and PCIe.
Slide 3 of 14
USB Connector Types
The physical shape of the plug. Type-A is the flat rectangle you know -- Type-C is the reversible future.
Type-A
The standard host-side connector. One orientation only. Found on desktops, laptops, hubs, and chargers. Not going away anytime soon.
Micro-B / Micro-B SS
Micro-B for older Android phones and accessories. Micro-B SuperSpeed (wider) for USB 3.0 external drives. Both being replaced by Type-C.
Type-C
Reversible 24-pin connector. Carries USB 3.2, USB4, Thunderbolt 3/4, DisplayPort Alt Mode, and power delivery up to 240W. The universal connector.
Exam tip: Type-C is a connector shape, not a speed spec. A Type-C port can run at USB 2.0 speeds or USB4 speeds depending on the controller behind it. Always check the spec, not just the plug.

Video Connectors
Connecting monitors, projectors, and TVs. Four connectors dominate -- two analog-era holdovers and two modern digital standards.
VGA (DE-15)
15-pin analog connector with screws. Blue plug. Max 2048x1536. No audio. Still on older projectors and KVM switches. Being phased out.
DVI
DVI-D (digital only), DVI-I (digital+analog), DVI-A (analog only). Single-link: 1920x1200. Dual-link: 2560x1600. No audio. White connector.
HDMI
Digital audio+video. HDMI 2.0: 4K@60Hz. HDMI 2.1: 8K@60Hz, 4K@120Hz. Sizes: Standard, Mini, Micro. Supports HDCP copy protection.
DisplayPort
DP 1.4: 8K@60Hz with DSC. DP 2.0: 16K. Supports daisy-chaining (MST). Mini-DP on laptops. Locking connector. Preferred for multi-monitor.
Exam tip: HDMI carries audio + video. VGA and DVI carry video only (no audio). DisplayPort carries audio + video. Know which ones need a separate audio cable.

Network Cables
Ethernet twisted-pair cabling -- Cat5e through Cat6a. All use the RJ-45 connector. The category determines speed and distance.
Cat 5e
Enhanced Category 5. 1 Gbps at up to 100 meters. The minimum acceptable standard for modern networks. Reduced crosstalk over Cat 5.
Cat 6
10 Gbps at up to 55 meters (1 Gbps at 100m). Tighter twist rate, internal spline separator. Thicker and less flexible than Cat 5e.
Cat 6a
Augmented Cat 6. 10 Gbps at the full 100 meters. Shielded (STP) pairs reduce alien crosstalk. Heavier, more expensive, harder to terminate.
Wiring standards: T568A and T568B define pin-pair assignments. A straight-through cable uses the same standard on both ends. A crossover cable uses T568A on one end and T568B on the other -- but Auto-MDI/X on modern switches makes crossovers unnecessary.

Fiber Optic Cables
Light instead of electricity. Immune to EMI, longer distances, higher bandwidth -- but more expensive and fragile.
Multimode Fiber (MMF)
Larger core (50 or 62.5 micron). Uses LED or VCSEL light source. Shorter distances (up to ~2 km). Less expensive. Orange or aqua jacket. Used inside buildings and campus networks.
Single-mode Fiber (SMF)
Tiny core (8-10 micron). Uses laser light source. Distances up to 100+ km. More expensive transceivers. Yellow jacket. Used for WAN links and long-haul connections.
SC (Subscriber)
Square push-pull connector. Common in data centers. Snap-in design.
LC (Lucent)
Small form factor, latch connector. Most common in modern installations. Half the size of SC.
ST (Straight Tip)
Bayonet twist-lock. Round connector, legacy. Found in older installations. Being replaced by LC.

Storage Connectors
SATA data, SATA power, Molex, and eSATA -- the cables that connect internal and external drives.
SATA Data Cable
7-pin L-shaped connector. SATA I: 1.5 Gbps, SATA II: 3 Gbps, SATA III: 6 Gbps. Maximum cable length: 1 meter. One device per cable (point-to-point).
SATA Power Connector
15-pin L-shaped connector from the PSU. Provides 3.3V, 5V, and 12V rails. Replaced the old 4-pin Molex for drives. Do not use Molex-to-SATA adapters -- fire risk.
Warning: Molex-to-SATA power adapters (especially molded/crimped type) are a known fire hazard. The 3.3V wire can short. Always use native SATA power connectors from the PSU.
Slide 8 of 14
Thunderbolt Interface
Intel/Apple's high-performance interconnect. Combines PCIe + DisplayPort into one cable. Versions 1-4.
Thunderbolt 3 / 4
40 Gbps bandwidth. USB-C connector with lightning bolt icon. TB4 requires PCIe tunneling + dual 4K display support minimum. Powers external GPUs (eGPU), NVMe arrays, docks.
Thunderbolt 1 / 2
Mini DisplayPort connector (not USB-C). TB1: 10 Gbps per channel. TB2: 20 Gbps (aggregated channels). Found on older Macs and some PCs. Not cross-compatible with TB3/4 without adapter.
Exam tip: Thunderbolt 3 and USB4 share the same USB-C connector and are interoperable. USB4 is actually based on the Thunderbolt 3 protocol. Look for the lightning bolt icon to identify Thunderbolt ports.

Coaxial, Serial & Legacy
Connectors you will still encounter in the field -- especially in networking closets, industrial environments, and older equipment.
Coaxial (RG-6)
F-type connector. Used for cable internet (DOCSIS), TV, and some legacy networks (BNC for 10Base2). Single copper conductor with shield.
DB-9 / RS-232
9-pin serial port. Used for console access to managed switches, routers, UPS management. Still critical for network admin. USB-to-serial adapters common.
DB-25 / Parallel
25-pin parallel port (LPT). Legacy printer interface. 8-bit parallel data transfer. Replaced by USB decades ago but still on some industrial equipment.
RJ-11
6-position telephone connector (usually 2 or 4 wires). Used for analog phone lines, DSL connections, and fax machines. Smaller than RJ-45.
Slide 10 of 14
Adapters & Converters
When the cable does not match the port, adapters bridge the gap. Know the difference between passive and active adapters.
DVI-to-HDMI
Passive -- both are digital. DVI-D and HDMI use the same signaling (TMDS). No audio on DVI side. Cheap and reliable.
USB-C to HDMI/DP
Active -- requires DisplayPort Alt Mode support on the USB-C port. A chip in the adapter converts DP signals to HDMI if needed.
VGA-to-HDMI
Active -- converts analog to digital. Requires power (USB). Adds latency. Avoid for gaming or precision work. Last resort for legacy projectors.
Key rule: If both sides use the same signaling (e.g., DVI-D to HDMI), a passive adapter works. If signaling differs (analog to digital, or different protocols), you need an active converter.

Visual Identification
On the exam and in the field, you identify cables by physical shape, color coding, and pin count.
1USB-A 3.0 ports are color-coded blue. USB 2.0 is black. SS+ may be teal or red.
2VGA has a blue 15-pin D-sub with two thumbscrews. Trapezoidal shape.
3DVI is white with a flat pin + cross pattern. DVI-I has 4 extra analog pins around the flat blade.
4HDMI is a slim trapezoid, smaller than VGA. Gold contacts visible inside.
5DisplayPort has one angled corner (asymmetric). DP has a latch; HDMI does not.
6RJ-45 is a wide plastic clip, 8 positions. RJ-11 is narrower with 6 positions.
7Thunderbolt 3/4 uses USB-C shape but has a lightning bolt icon next to the port.

Cable Management
Professional cable management reduces troubleshooting time, improves airflow, and prevents accidental disconnects.
Cable Ties & Velcro
Velcro straps are preferred over zip ties -- they are reusable and do not damage cables when tightened. Bundle cables by function (power, data, network).
Cable Trays & Raceways
Overhead cable trays in server rooms. Surface-mounted raceways along walls. Keep power and data cables separated to reduce electromagnetic interference.
Labeling
Label both ends of every cable. Use a consistent naming convention. Document patch panel connections. A label printer is a technician's best friend.
Bend Radius
Every cable has a minimum bend radius. Fiber is especially sensitive -- too tight a bend breaks the glass core. Cat6: 4x cable diameter minimum. Fiber: typically 30mm minimum.
Strain Relief
Cables should not hang by their connectors. Use cable management arms on server rails. Provide service loops (extra slack) for maintenance access.

Selection Decision Tree
Given a scenario, how do you choose the right cable? Follow the requirements: speed, distance, signal type, and budget.
1Monitor at desk? DisplayPort for multi-monitor daisy chain, HDMI for single display with audio, DVI for legacy digital.
2Conference room projector? HDMI is universal. Bring a USB-C to HDMI adapter for modern laptops. VGA adapter as fallback for old projectors.
3Network drop under 100m? Cat 6a for 10 Gbps. Cat 5e is fine for 1 Gbps. Over 100m? You need fiber.
4Connecting a drive? Internal: SATA data + SATA power. External: USB 3.0+ or Thunderbolt for NVMe enclosures.
5Need max bandwidth? Thunderbolt 4 (40 Gbps) for docks and eGPUs. USB4 as an alternative.

Cables & Connectors Summary
Key takeaways for the A+ exam and real-world fieldwork.
1USB Type-C is a connector shape, not a speed. Always check the USB generation behind it (2.0, 3.2, USB4).
2HDMI and DisplayPort carry audio + video. VGA and DVI carry video only.
3Cat 6a is the sweet spot for 10 Gbps at 100m. Cat 5e is minimum for 1 Gbps networks.
4Fiber: MMF (orange/aqua) for short distances with LED, SMF (yellow) for long haul with laser.
5SATA III runs at 6 Gbps with L-shaped connectors. Never use Molex-to-SATA adapters.
6Thunderbolt 3/4 = 40 Gbps via USB-C. Look for the lightning bolt icon. USB4 is based on TB3.
7Passive adapters work when signals match (DVI-D to HDMI). Active adapters needed for signal conversion (VGA to HDMI).
