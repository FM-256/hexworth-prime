Section 1: Presentations

Cryptography Fundamentals (presentations/key-cryptography-fundamentals.presentation.html). The flagship presentation. Covers the four security goals (Confidentiality, Integrity, Authentication, Non-Repudiation) and how each maps to specific cryptographic primitives. Walks through Caesar cipher and Rail Fence with interactive shift and transposition demos. Contrasts symmetric vs asymmetric encryption with Alice and Bob key-exchange diagrams. Covers block ciphers (AES, Blowfish, 3DES) vs stream ciphers (RC4 deprecated, ChaCha20, Salsa20). Explains hybrid cryptography (asymmetric for key exchange, symmetric for bulk data). Closes with hashing fundamentals and PKI certificate chains. Includes an inline knowledge-check quiz.

The Art of Encryption (presentations/key-encryption-basics.presentation.html). Beginner-friendly encryption walkthrough. Three encryption types with interactive visual flows: symmetric (same key both directions: AES, DES, 3DES, Blowfish), asymmetric (public/private key pairs: RSA, ECC, ElGamal, DSA), and hashing (one-way: SHA-256, SHA-3, bcrypt, MD5 deprecated). Embedded Caesar cipher demo. Real-world applications section grounds the theory: HTTPS, password storage, digital signatures, file encryption, messaging apps, and blockchain.

Advanced Symmetric Encryption (presentations/key-advanced-symmetric.presentation.html). Deep dive into AES (Rijndael). Key-size table (AES-128 / 10 rounds, AES-192 / 12 rounds, AES-256 / 14 rounds) and per-round operations (SubBytes for confusion via the S-box, ShiftRows, MixColumns for diffusion, AddRoundKey). Block cipher modes: ECB (never use, with the famous "ECB Penguin" image as visual proof), CBC (IV requirements, padding oracle attack), CTR (parallelizable counter mode), GCM (authenticated encryption, AEAD). Includes explicit nonce-reuse warnings and a mode-comparison table.

X.509 Certificates and PKI Deep Dive (presentations/key-certificates.presentation.html). Complete certificate anatomy: Version, Serial Number, Signature Algorithm, Issuer DN, Validity Period, Subject DN, Public Key Info, Extensions (v3), Signature. Distinguished Name fields (CN, O, OU, L, ST, C). Critical extensions: Key Usage, Subject Alternative Name (SAN), Basic Constraints (CA:TRUE/FALSE, pathlen). Validation levels: DV (minutes, free to $50, Let's Encrypt), OV (1-3 days, $40-150), EV (1-2 weeks, $150-300, green-bar UI removed 2019). Scope types (Single Domain, Wildcard with its limitations, Multi-Domain SAN, Self-Signed). Full certificate-chain validation walkthrough.

Elliptic Curve Cryptography (presentations/key-elliptic-curve.presentation.html). Mathematical foundations of ECC. Weierstrass form (y^2 = x^3 + ax + b), point addition geometry, scalar multiplication as repeated addition. Covers NIST P-256 and secp256k1 (the Bitcoin curve). Explains why ECC gives equivalent security to RSA at much smaller key sizes (256-bit ECC roughly equals 3072-bit RSA). Applications: TLS, SSH, cryptocurrencies, mobile-device security.

Cryptanalysis: The Art of Breaking Cryptography (presentations/key-cryptanalysis.presentation.html). Ethical framing first. Defines the five levels of cryptanalytic break (Total Break, Global Deduction, Instance Deduction, Information Deduction, Distinguishing Attack). Historical context (Enigma at Bletchley Park, the Bombe). Attack techniques covered: frequency analysis, brute force, known-plaintext, chosen-plaintext, and side-channel attacks (timing, power, cache).

Key Derivation Functions (presentations/key-derivation.presentation.html). Explains why a password is not a key. Quantifies the problem with direct hashing (about 100 billion SHA-256 hashes per second on modern hardware, making raw-hashed passwords brittle). KDF principles: deliberate slowness, configurable work factors, parallelization resistance, memory hardness. Covers PBKDF2, bcrypt, scrypt, and Argon2 (the modern default). Salt generation and per-user salting. Visual comparison of direct hash speed vs KDF processing time.

Enterprise Key Management and HSMs (presentations/key-management.presentation.html). Business case for key management with cited numbers: IBM / Ponemon 2024 Cost of a Data Breach ($4.88M global average), GDPR Article 83 (fines up to 20M euros or 4 percent of global revenue). Real consequences: Code Spaces (2014, destroyed by AWS key compromise), Uber (2016, $148M settlement after a GitHub key exposure). Walks through the full key lifecycle with HSMs in scope. FIPS 140-3 (active since September 2021) compliance levels; legacy FIPS 140-2 validations remain accepted during transition.

Message Authentication and HMAC (presentations/key-message-authentication.presentation.html). Three security pillars: confidentiality, integrity, authenticity. Attack scenarios: man-in-the-middle message modification and replay attacks. Why simple hashing is insufficient for authentication (no key, no proof of origin). HMAC construction (inner and outer pad), supported hash algorithms, and the necessity of timing-safe comparison to prevent timing side channels.

Post-Quantum Cryptography (presentations/key-post-quantum.presentation.html). Quantum threat model explained. Shor's algorithm breaks RSA, ECC, and DH in polynomial time on a sufficiently large quantum computer. Grover's algorithm weakens symmetric cryptography (effectively halves key length, so AES-256 becomes equivalent to AES-128). NIST PQC standards finalized August 2024: lattice-based ML-KEM (FIPS 203, formerly CRYSTALS-Kyber) and ML-DSA (FIPS 204, formerly CRYSTALS-Dilithium); hash-based SLH-DSA (FIPS 205, formerly SPHINCS+); code-based candidates still under review. Migration strategy with hybrid (classical + PQC) approaches. The "harvest now, decrypt later" threat for long-term sensitive data.

TLS/SSL Explained (presentations/key-tls-ssl.presentation.html). 22-slide deck. Protocol evolution timeline from SSL 1.0 (1994, never released) through SSL 2.0 / 3.0 (deprecated) into TLS 1.0 through TLS 1.3. Three security properties at the transport layer: confidentiality, integrity, authentication. TLS 1.3 improvements covered in depth, including Encrypted Client Hello (ECH) and removal of legacy primitives. Handshake mechanics and cipher-suite negotiation.

Section 2: Hands-On Labs

AES Hands-On Lab (labs/key-aes.lab.html). Six exercises starting with AES-256-CBC encryption using OpenSSL (file encryption with command breakdown of enc, -aes-256-cbc, -salt). Progresses through IV handling, ECB-mode danger demonstrations (pattern leakage), and authenticated encryption with GCM.

Cryptanalysis Attack Lab (labs/key-attack.lab.html). Seven exercises. Students break a Caesar cipher using frequency analysis on the ciphertext "WKDW LV D VHFUHW PHVVDJH...". Explicit ethical framing throughout: attack mechanics are taught so defenders recognize them. Each exercise reinforces a different attack vector.

Certificate Hands-On Lab (labs/key-cert.lab.html). Eight exercises parsing and decoding X.509 certificates, including a real PEM-encoded certificate from "Hexworth Training CA". Tasks: inspect validity, identify issuer, extract SAN domains. Each exercise is framed as a real-world operations scenario (renewing an expiring cert, investigating a chain failure).

Cryptanalysis Lab (labs/key-cryptanalysis.lab.html). Six interactive visualization modules: Frequency Analysis, Birthday Attack probability calculator, Length Extension attacks, Padding Oracle, Bit-Flipping, and Hash Collisions. Each module is animated so students see why the attack works, not just that it works.

Cryptography and Steganography Lab (labs/key-crypto-stego.lab.html). Ten progressive modules teaching both cryptography (encryption, hashing, digital signatures) and steganography (image hiding, watermarking, covert channels). Includes the three core hashing properties: deterministic output, one-way design, avalanche effect.

ECC Hands-On Lab (labs/key-ecc.lab.html). Seven exercises generating P-256 ECC key pairs with OpenSSL. Commands include ecparam -name prime256v1 -genkey, public-key extraction, and key inspection. Industry-standard tooling throughout so students leave able to operate on real keys.

Encryption Lab: ASCII / Binary / Hex (labs/key-encryption-ascii-binary.lab.html). Live conversion tool. Type text and see its ASCII decimal, 8-bit binary, and hexadecimal representations side by side. Interactive XOR encryption demonstrates reversibility (C = P XOR K, P = C XOR K).

Encryption Lab: DH and RSA (labs/key-encryption-dh-rsa.lab.html). Extended version of the ASCII/Binary/Hex lab. Adds Diffie-Hellman key exchange (shared-secret derivation over an insecure channel) and RSA number theory (prime selection, modular exponentiation, the public/private key pair).

Firewall Builder Lab (labs/key-firewall-builder.lab.html). Ten progressive levels building ACL (Access Control List) rules with full field control: Action (ALLOW/DENY), Source, Destination, Protocol (TCP/UDP/ICMP), Port, Direction (IN/OUT/BOTH). Each level ends in a traffic-testing validation step.

Interactive Hashing Lab (labs/key-hashing-algorithms.lab.html). Supports MD5, SHA-1, SHA-256, SHA-384, SHA-512. Features include salt input, file hashing, an avalanche-effect demonstration (one bit changes, the whole digest changes), hex and base64 output formats, and a guided tutorial mode for first-time students.

HMAC Hands-On Lab (labs/key-hmac.lab.html). Seven exercises generating HMAC-SHA256 using the Web Crypto API (crypto.subtle.importKey, crypto.subtle.sign). First task: compute HMAC of "Hello, Hexworth Prime!" with key "welcome-to-key-house". Students write real code in the browser.

HSM and Key Management Lab (labs/key-hsm.lab.html). Eight exercises designing the key hierarchy for an e-commerce platform "SecureShop" handling 100K transactions per day. Scenarios cover customer PII, payment card data (PCI-DSS), order history, authentication tokens. Decisions students make include: Root Master Key location (Software KMS vs Dedicated HSM at FIPS 140-3 Level 3 vs Cloud HSM) and the KEK / DEK (Key Encryption Key, Data Encryption Key) strategy.

KDF Hands-On Lab (labs/key-kdf.lab.html). Eight exercises. Generate 32-byte cryptographically secure salts using crypto.getRandomValues() in JavaScript and os.urandom() in Python. Explicit warning: never use Math.random() for cryptographic purposes. Password hashing with PBKDF2, bcrypt, scrypt, Argon2 and parameter tuning so students see how work factors affect runtime.

PQC Hands-On Lab (labs/key-pqc.lab.html). Seven exercises framed as a Cryptographic Inventory assessment for a financial services company. Students identify quantum-vulnerable systems: TLS certificates (RSA, ECC), VPN authentication (IKEv2, DH groups), code signing certificates, and the key-exchange methods protecting encrypted backups.

Section 4: Games

Cipher Cracker (games/key-cipher-cracker.presentation.html). Twelve encrypted enemy transmissions of escalating difficulty (Caesar shifts up through multi-layered cipher chains). Professional-grade tools available in-game: frequency analysis charts, interactive letter substitution tables, pattern finders, known-plaintext attack capability. Scoring is 100 base + up to 50 time bonus, minus 25 per hint used.

Cipher Bubbles (games/key-cipher-bubbles.applet.html). Arcade-style. Rogue ciphertexts flood the crypto lab; the player traps them in encryption bubbles and pops them. Enemy types: ROT13 (basic, slow), XOR (fast, erratic), AES (armored, takes 2 pops), RSA (heavy, slow but tough), MD5 (deprecated, crumbles on contact). Wave progression introduces harder enemies.

Crypto Pong (games/key-crypto-pong.applet.html). Every serve shows a real, freshly-computed ciphertext plus its cipher name and key. The player's paddle has three bands (top/mid/bottom), each showing a candidate plaintext; the player must decode the ciphertext and hit the ball with the band holding the correct plaintext to return it -- a wrong band or a miss lets the ball pass through untouched. Cipher escalates each round: Caesar, then Atbash, then XOR, then Vigenere, each with a randomized key per rally so it can't be memorized.

Crypto Flap (games/key-crypto-flap.html). Flappy-bird-style platformer. Player is "KEY-256, forged in the entropy pool." Encrypted pipes require 3 key fragments to unlock. Every 10 points triggers a bonus cipher challenge.

Don't Leak the Key (games/key-dont-leak-the-key.html). Cryptographic-nightmare survival simulator. 180-second timer at CRITICAL threat level. Terminal-style command interface. Game state tracks key revocation, repo cleanup, and new-key generation. Teaches key-compromise incident response.

GPG Decrypt (games/key-gpg-decrypt.html). "The Cryptographer's Tower". Text adventure themed around GPG decryption operations. Terminal-style interface with typewriter sound effects.

Section 6: Modules and Reviews

Hashing and Steganography Module (modules/key-hash-stego-intro.module.html). Three-slide interactive lesson. Slide 1 introduces both topics. Slide 2 lets students type any text and compute its real SHA-256 hash in the browser, then explains hashing as a fixed-size, one-way digest used for password security, integrity verification, and blockchain. Players work through hash-cracking challenges. Useful as a review piece after the Interactive Hashing Lab and the Hashing and Steganography module.

How to Redirect

  1. Acknowledge the question. Show you understand what they are asking.
  2. Give a brief overview if you can. One sentence is plenty.
  3. Name the SME and the house. Tell them where to find the chat icon (bottom-right of any page in that house).
  4. Stay in character. Redirect with your own voice, not a template.

Rules All Bots Follow

  • Never provide direct answers to graded quiz questions, lab challenges, CTF flags, or exam questions. Guide students to discover answers themselves.
  • Never use emoji. The platform uses webp icon images.
  • Do not fabricate information. If unsure, say so and redirect.
  • Keep responses concise. Students want help, not lectures.
  • Redirect gracefully. Never say "that is not my area" without naming who CAN help.
  • Stay in character. You are your historical figure with your domain expertise. The personality is part of the experience.
  • Encourage exploration. Point students toward content they might not have found (labs, games, CTF boxes, projects).
  • Protect student data. Never ask for passwords, personal info, or credentials.
