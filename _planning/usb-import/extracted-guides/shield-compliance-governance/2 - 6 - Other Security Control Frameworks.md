## Other Security Control Frameworks     

### Control Objectives for Information and Related Technology (COBIT)

Control Objectives for Information and Related Technologies (COBIT)) is a comprehensive framework for IT management and governance developed by ISACA. It provides guidelines, practices, and tools for organizations to manage and govern their IT environments effectively. 

A security controls development framework with 5 principles:

- Meeting stakeholder needs
- Covering the enterprise end-to-end
- Applying a single integrated framework
- Enabling a holistic approach
- Separating governance from management

The principles drive objectives to have seven enablers and 37 governance and management processes. The seven enablers support the implementation and maintenance of a robust IT governance framework. These enablers are crucial for organizations looking to optimize their IT resources and align IT processes with business objectives.

The seven enablers are:

- Principles, Policies, and Frameworks - These are the core guidelines that drive an organization towards achieving its objectives. They include the overarching principles that guide decision-making, policies that provide specific guidelines for actions and behaviors, and frameworks that offer a structured approach to implementing governance and management practices. These elements ensure consistency and provide a clear direction for IT governance and management.
- Processes - Processes refer to the structured sets of activities designed to achieve specific objectives. In COBIT, processes are defined across the governance and management domains, detailing the inputs, outputs, key activities, and objectives. Effective process management ensures that IT services are delivered efficiently and effectively, supporting the organization's goals.
- Organizational Structures - This enabler focuses on the roles, responsibilities, and communication lines within an organization. Organizational structures define how decision-making authority is distributed and how responsibilities are allocated among various roles. A clear and effective organizational structure supports accountability, fosters effective decision-making, and aligns IT processes with business needs.
- Culture, Ethics, and Behavior - The behaviors, values, and ethics of individuals and teams within an organization significantly impact the success of IT governance and management. This enabler recognizes the importance of fostering a positive culture that promotes ethical behavior, accountability, and a commitment to excellence. A supportive culture is essential for achieving compliance, encouraging collaboration, and driving continuous improvement.
- Information - Information is a key asset for any organization. This enabler emphasizes the importance of managing and governing information to support business decisions and operational processes. Information must be accurate, timely, relevant, and secure to provide value. COBIT addresses the lifecycle of information, from creation and use to archiving and disposal, ensuring its integrity, confidentiality, and availability.
- Services, Infrastructure, and Applications - This enabler covers the IT resources, including services, infrastructure (physical and virtual), and applications, that an organization uses to process information and deliver IT services. Managing these resources effectively is crucial for meeting business requirements, optimizing performance, and supporting service delivery.
- People, Skills, and Competencies - People are at the core of IT governance and management. This enabler focuses on the human resources required to implement and maintain IT services and governance structures. It includes considerations for skills development, training, and staffing to ensure that the organization has the necessary capabilities to achieve its objectives.

The seven enablers of COBIT provide a holistic view of what is needed for effective IT governance and management. By addressing these areas, organizations can create a robust framework that supports business objectives, manages IT-related risks, and ensures compliance with relevant regulations and standards. For IT professionals and organizations aiming to implement COBIT, understanding and leveraging these enablers is key to achieving success in IT governance and management.

### Health Information Trust Alliance (HITRUST) Common Security Framework (CSF) 

The Health Information Trust Alliance (HITRUST) Common Security Framework (CSF) is a certifiable framework that provides organizations within the healthcare industry (and those that store, process, or transmit health-related information) with a comprehensive set of controls that address privacy and security regulations. The HITRUST CSF harmonizes the requirements of existing standards and regulations, including federal (like HIPAA), state, third-party, and government (like NIST and ISO) frameworks.

HITRUST CSF provides:

- Regulatory Compliance: HITRUST CSF helps organizations comply with a range of healthcare-related privacy and security regulations. By adhering to HITRUST CSF, organizations can ensure that they are meeting the specific compliance requirements that are part of their security governance obligations.
- Risk Management: The framework includes a risk-based approach to selecting and implementing controls, which helps organizations focus their efforts on areas of greatest impact.
- Unified Framework: HITRUST CSF unifies various standards and regulatory guidelines, helping organizations avoid the complexity of managing multiple compliance frameworks. 
- Adaptability: The framework is designed to be adaptable to the size, complexity, and type of organization, allowing security professionals to apply it effectively in diverse organizational contexts, ensuring that security governance scales appropriately.
- Continuous Improvement: HITRUST CSF encourages ongoing assessment and refinement of security controls, which aligns with sustaining and continuously improving security governance practices to adapt to changing risks and business needs.
- Evidence-Based Compliance: By providing a certifiable framework, HITRUST CSF allows organizations to demonstrate through assessment and third-party certification that they have implemented robust security governance measures.

### Center for Internet Security (CIS) Critical Security Controls (CSCs)

The Center for Internet Security (CIS) Critical Security Controls, also known as the CIS Controls, are a set of best practices designed to provide organizations with a clear and concise roadmap for improving their cybersecurity posture. The controls are prioritized sets of actions that collectively form a defense-in-depth set of best practices that mitigate the most common attacks against systems and networks.

The CIS Controls offer actionable steps that security professionals can use to bolster their security programs:

- Prioritized Defense Strategy: The CIS Controls are designed to prioritize the most important actions first. This approach helps security professionals focus on the most effective strategies to implement, aligning with the governance principle of effective resource allocation.
- Holistic Security Approach: The controls cover various aspects of IT systems, including hardware, software, data, people, and procedures, ensuring a comprehensive approach to security governance.
- Best Practices Alignment: Each CIS Control is based on best practices and expert consensus, providing security professionals with guidance that is widely recognized and respected in the industry. Following these controls helps organizations align with proven security strategies.
- Continuous Monitoring and Improvement: Many of the CIS Controls emphasize the importance of continuous monitoring, assessment, and improvement of security processes. This aligns with the governance need to adapt to changing threats and technologies, ensuring that security practices are not static.
- Benchmarking and Measurement: The CIS Controls provide a framework against which organizations can measure their security posture. This benchmarking is vital for evaluating the effectiveness of the current governance practices and demonstrating compliance with industry standards.
- Incident Management and Response: The controls include guidelines for incident management and response, ensuring that organizations are prepared to address and recover from security incidents. This readiness is a crucial component of security governance.
- Security Awareness and Training: Emphasizing the human aspect of cybersecurity, the CIS Controls include recommendations for security awareness and training programs, aligning with governance principles that recognize people as both a potential risk and a line of defense.

Their design principles include:

- Offense Informs Defense – CIS Controls are selected, dropped, and prioritized based on data, and on specific knowledge of attacker behavior and how to stop it. 
- Focus – Help defenders identify the most critical things they need to do to stop the most important attacks, Avoid being tempted to solve every security problem. Avoid adding “good things to do” or “things you could do.”
- Feasible – All individual recommendations (Safeguards) must be specific and practical to implement.
- Measurable – All CIS Controls must be measurable. Simplify or remove ambiguous language to avoid inconsistent interpretation. Some safeguards may have a threshold.

### Sherwood Applied Business Security Architecture (SABSA)

The Sherwood Applied Business Security Architecture (SABSA) is a framework and methodology for enterprise security architecture and service management. It is designed to ensure that security services are designed, delivered, and supported as an integral part of IT management within an organization.

Here’s what SABSA provides:

- Business-Driven Approach: SABSA starts with the business requirements and ensures that security architectures are built to support these requirements. 
- Risk Management: SABSA's framework incorporates risk management at its core. It provides a structured approach to identifying, assessing, and managing risks in line with business needs. 
- Layered Architectural Approach: SABSA encourages a layered approach to security architecture, which is essential for the implementation of security governance principles. These layers ensure that security controls are applied systematically across different levels of the enterprise.
- End-to-End Lifecycle Management: The SABSA methodology emphasizes the entire lifecycle of security services, from conceptualization to operation and beyond. This end-to-end view ensures the sustainability of security governance principles, as security professionals can ensure that security measures are continuously monitored, evaluated, and improved.
- Attribute Profiling: SABSA's attribute profiling allows for the translation of business requirements into security requirements and controls. This profiling ensures that security measures are directly linked to business needs and are applied where they will be most effective.
- Matrixed Toolset: SABSA provides a matrixed toolset that helps in addressing the 'what, why, how, who, where, and when' of security requirements. This comprehensive toolset aids security professionals in applying a holistic view to security governance, ensuring that it is aligned with organizational processes and strategies.

SABSA uses six questions (What, Where, When, Why, Who, and How) and interacts with six layers (operational, component, physical, logical, conceptual, and contextual).

### Payment Card Industry (PCI) Data Security Standard (DSS)

The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) is a widely recognized security standard designed to ensure that all entities that store, process, or transmit credit card information maintain a secure environment. The PCI DSS is mandated by the major credit card brands and administered by the Payment Card Industry Security Standards Council (PCI SSC).

The PCI DSS provides a robust framework for securing payment card data that security professionals can integrate into an organization's security governance and practices:

- Risk Management: PCI DSS requires organizations to implement a risk management process that identifies vulnerabilities that could impact cardholder data. Security professionals must ensure that these risks are managed in line with business objectives and the organization's risk appetite. 
- Policy Development and Implementation: PCI DSS provides specific requirements for developing and implementing security policies and operational procedures that protect payment card data. Security professionals are responsible for ensuring that these policies are not only in place but are communicated to and understood by all relevant parties.
- Data Protection Measures: As part of an organization's security governance, security professionals must apply data protection measures outlined in PCI DSS, such as encryption, access controls, and monitoring, to secure cardholder data effectively.
- Compliance Management: PCI DSS compliance is mandatory for organizations handling cardholder data. Security professionals play a crucial role in assessing and documenting compliance efforts, as well as in managing audits conducted by qualified security assessors (QSAs).
- Training and Awareness: Security governance involves promoting a culture of security awareness. Security professionals are tasked with educating and training staff about the importance of PCI DSS and their role in maintaining compliance and securing payment card data.
- Incident Response: PCI DSS requires organizations to have an incident response plan to react swiftly to a data breach. Security professionals evaluate and refine these plans to ensure they align with broader business continuity and disaster recovery strategies.
- Continuous Monitoring and Improvement: Consistent with PCI DSS requirements, security professionals must establish continuous monitoring mechanisms to detect unauthorized access to cardholder data and continuously improve security controls based on the evolving threat landscape.

### Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program (FedRAMP)

The Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program (FedRAMP) is a government-wide program that standardizes the security assessment, authorization, and continuous monitoring processes for cloud products and services used by U.S. federal agencies. This ensures that cloud services providers (CSPs) meet a baseline set of security requirements that are consistent across the federal government.

FedRAMP's approach to cloud security and compliance can inform and an organization in several ways:

- Standardized Approach to Security: FedRAMP provides a set of standardized security requirements for CSPs, which security professionals can use as a benchmark for evaluating the security of cloud services within their organization, especially when dealing with federal information.
- Risk Management Framework: FedRAMP's framework emphasizes a risk-based approach to security assessment, authorization, and continuous monitoring. Security professionals can leverage FedRAMP's framework to ensure comprehensive risk management practices are in place.
- Third-Party Assessment: The program mandates the use of independent third-party assessment organizations (3PAOs) to assess cloud services. Security professionals can apply similar principles when evaluating third-party services to ensure objective and rigorous security assessments.
- Documentation and Policies: FedRAMP requires thorough documentation of security controls, policies, and procedures. Security professionals must ensure that such documentation is maintained for cloud services and aligns with internal security governance principles.
- Continuous Monitoring: The program requires ongoing monitoring of cloud services' security posture. Security professionals must implement continuous monitoring practices within their organizations to ensure the long-term effectiveness of security controls and compliance with established governance principles.
- Government Collaboration: FedRAMP facilitates collaboration between government entities and CSPs to achieve and maintain authorization. Similarly, security professionals should foster collaboration between different business units within their organization to ensure that security governance principles are applied consistently and effectively.
- Incident Response: FedRAMP has specific requirements for incident response, which security professionals can integrate into their organizational policies, ensuring robust and timely responses to security incidents, particularly those involving cloud services.
