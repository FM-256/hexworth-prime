**Description:** In this episode, you will learn about the different legal systems, including civil code, common, criminal, civil/tort, regulatory, customary, religious, and mixed law.

## Legal Systems     

Understanding different types of law is essential for security professionals, as they often need to navigate legal, regulatory, and compliance issues related to information security. Keep in mind that the security professionals should advise organizations to hire legal representation for advice. But the security professional should still have a basic understanding of how each type of law pertains to the organization:

### Civil Code Law

Civil code law, also known as codified law, is a systematic collection of laws designed to comprehensively cover the core principles that society must adhere to. It is a rule-based law and does not rely on precedence in any way, meaning lower courts do not have to follow higher court decisions. For security professionals, this means understanding the specific articles and statutes related to information security and privacy obligations within jurisdictions that operate under a civil law system. This type of law is seen in France and Germany.

### Common Law

Common law is based on precedent and judicial decisions rather than written codes. Used in UK, US, and Canada, this system uses a jury, but jury can be waived in favor of judge. Security professionals in organizations operating in common law jurisdictions must be aware of past court decisions regarding information security incidents because these precedents can significantly influence the handling of similar cases and compliance requirements.

### Criminal Law

Criminal law deals with behaviors that are offenses against the state or public. Security professionals must understand what constitutes a cybercrime, how it is prosecuted, and the implications for organizational security practices, as well as for investigations following a security breach. Most countries have criminal laws.

### Civil/Tort Law

Civil tort law addresses wrongs or harm that lead to legal liability. The defendant is liable for damages to the victim (plaintiff). Victim can seek compensatory damages (for losses), punitive damages (for punishment(, and statutory damages (established by law). Includes economic damages, liability, negligence, intentional damage, and property damage. This is the area of law in the US that allows senior officials of an organization to be held liable. In the context of information security, security professionals must be aware of potential civil actions, such as lawsuits for negligence or breaches of contract, which can arise from failing to protect sensitive data. Most countries have civil/tort law.

### Regulatory Law

Regulatory law , also called administrative law, encompasses rules established by regulatory agencies. For security professionals, this includes understanding and complying with regulations specific to information security, such as GDPR, HIPAA, or FISMA, and their impact on security policies and practices. The most common organization types that fall under this are public utilities, communications, banking, healthcare, and safety and environmental protection regulations. Most countries have regulatory laws.

### Customary Law

Customary law is informed by traditional norms and practices in a country or region. This type of law is seen in many Asian and African nations. While not as common in corporate environments, security professionals working in certain regions may need to consider customary laws when implementing security measures that affect local communities or indigenous populations.

### Religious Law

Religious law is based on the religious precepts of a particular group. Security professionals may encounter this in certain regions where religious principles directly influence legal decisions, including those pertaining to information security and privacy. This type of law is commonly seen in Saudi Arabia, Iran, and Yemen.

### Mixed Law

Mixed law systems, also referred to as hybrid law systems, incorporate elements from two or more of the above types of legal systems. Most often mixed law is a mixing of civil and common. Security professionals working in such jurisdictions must understand the interplay between different legal frameworks and their impact on security and investigative practices.

Security professionals should work with legal representation to ensure that their organization's security policies and procedures are compliant with the various legal and regulatory requirements that apply to their operations.

Security professionals must understand the requirements for different types of investigations, including administrative, criminal, civil, and regulatory, as well as those stemming from industry standards. They need to be prepared to cooperate with investigations, preserve evidence, and follow proper protocols in line with the type of investigation being conducted.
