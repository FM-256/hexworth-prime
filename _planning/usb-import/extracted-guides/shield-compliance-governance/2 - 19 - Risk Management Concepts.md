# Risk Management Concepts

After completing this episode, you should be able to:

+ Discuss risk management concepts.

**Description:** In this episode, you will learn about the risk management concepts, including tangible versus intangible assets, asset valuation, vulnerability, threat, threat agent, threat actor, exploit, risk exposure, countermeasure, incident, attack, and breach.

## Risk Management Concepts

### Assets 

Assets are any valuable resources owned by the organization that contribute to its business operations and objectives. They are classified into two main types: tangible and intangible. Proper identification and valuation of these assets are crucial for implementing security measures that appropriately protect them from various threats and vulnerabilities.

#### Tangible Assets

Tangible assets are physical items or properties owned by the organization. They can be seen, touched, and quantified in physical terms.

Examples: Computers, servers, office buildings, manufacturing equipment, and inventory are all tangible assets. These items have a direct physical presence and are often critical to the operational processes of a business.

Protecting tangible assets involves securing physical premises, ensuring the safety of hardware, implementing access controls, and maintaining disaster recovery measures that address physical threats (such as natural disasters, theft, or vandalism).

#### Intangible Assets

Intangible assets are non-physical assets that have value to the organization. Their value is derived from the rights and resources they represent rather than their physical characteristics

Examples: Software, intellectual property (including patents, trademarks, and copyrights), brand reputation, organizational data, and customer relationships are intangible assets. They are not physically measurable but play a critical role in the competitive positioning and profitability of the organization.

Securing intangible assets involves protecting data integrity and confidentiality, enforcing intellectual property rights, ensuring data availability, and safeguarding privacy. Security professionals must implement information security strategies that include data encryption, access controls, legal compliance, and cybersecurity measures tailored to the nature of the intangible assets.

### Asset Valuation

The first step in risk management is identifying all assets that require protection. Security professionals must ensure a comprehensive inventory of both tangible and intangible assets is maintained.

Asset valuation involves determining the worth or importance of an organization's assets in order to prioritize security measures and allocate security resources effectively. The valuation process helps organizations identify how critical each asset is to their operations, the impact of potential losses, and how much investment should be made to protect them.

Asset valuation allows an organization to prioritize risks based on the potential impact on valuable assets. Understanding the value of assets helps in making informed decisions about where to focus security efforts and resources.

#### Process:

1. Identification
2. Classification:  Assets are classified based on their types and the roles they play in the organization. This classification helps in applying appropriate valuation techniques.
3. Valuation: Various methods can be used to assess the value of assets, including:
   - Quantitative Valuation: Assigns a monetary value to assets based on replacement cost, actual cash value, or the potential revenue generated by the asset.
   - Qualitative Valuation: Often used for intangible assets, this method assesses value based on the importance of the asset to organizational operations or the severity of the impact if the asset were compromised.
4. Prioritization: Assets are then prioritized based on their value. This prioritization influences how risk assessments are conducted and how protective measures are implemented.

#### Factors Influencing Asset Valuation:

- Criticality to Business Operations: The extent to which business operations depend on the asset.
- Sensitivity of Information: The level of confidentiality, integrity, and availability required by the information contained in or processed by the asset.
- Regulatory and Compliance Requirements: Any legal or regulatory requirements that mandate specific protection measures due to the nature of the asset.
- Replacement Cost: The financial cost associated with replacing the asset after a loss.
- Impact of Loss: The overall impact on the organization if the asset is damaged, lost, or stolen. This includes financial loss, reputational damage, and operational disruption.

### Vulnerability

A vulnerability is a weakness or gap in a security program that can be exploited by threats to gain unauthorized access to an asset. Vulnerabilities can exist in software, hardware, processes, or procedures.

 Characteristics:

- Technical or Procedural: Vulnerabilities can be technical (e.g., software bugs, outdated systems) or procedural (e.g., inadequate security policies, lack of employee training).
- Known or Unknown: Some vulnerabilities are known and documented (e.g., published software flaws), while others may be unknown to the organization until exploited.

### Threat

A threat is any circumstance or event with the potential to cause harm to an information system or organization through unauthorized access, destruction, disclosure, modification of data, and/or denial of service. Threats can be intentional (e.g., cyber-attacks, malware) or accidental (e.g., human error, natural disasters).

It is anything that could exploit a vulnerability.

Characteristics:

- External or Internal: Threats can originate from outside the organization (external, such as hackers) or from within (internal, such as disgruntled employees).
- Active or Passive: Some threats require deliberate actions to exploit vulnerabilities (active), while others can occur without direct human involvement (passive, such as natural disasters).

### Compare and Contrast Vulnerability/Threat

- Nature: A threat is a potential cause of an incident that may exploit a vulnerability; a vulnerability is a condition that can facilitate the occurrence of that incident if not properly secured.
- Dependence: A vulnerability does not need a threat to exist; it is an inherent weakness. However, a threat requires a vulnerability to be exploited effectively, unless the threat is purely physical or environmental.
- Management Approach: Managing threats often involves external monitoring, intelligence gathering, and anticipating actions from potential threat actors. Managing vulnerabilities requires internal assessments, audits, and remediations to strengthen the security posture.

To mitigate threats, organizations may implement deterrents, employ monitoring systems, and develop incident response capabilities. 

To address vulnerabilities, organizations typically engage in patch management, system hardening, and revising policies and procedures.

### Threat Agent

A threat agent is the specific element or factor that can materialize a threat. This could be a person, group, or external force that facilitates the occurrence of a threat.

 Characteristics:

- Mechanism or method: It acts as the catalyst that triggers or delivers the threat to the vulnerable system or asset.
- Can be non-human: While often associated with people, threat agents can also include software, hardware, or environmental conditions.

Examples: A flood (natural condition), a computer virus (software), or a disgruntled employee (person).

### Threat Actor

A threat actor refers to an individual or group that is responsible for an event or incident that impacts the security of the organization. Threat actors are the entities with motive and means who intentionally exploit vulnerabilities to cause harm.

 Characteristics:

- Intentional actions: Unlike threat agents, threat actors always involve some level of intent.
- Typically human: These are usually individuals or groups, such as hackers, insider threats, competitors, or state-sponsored groups.

Examples: Hackers attacking an IT system, employees leaking sensitive information, or corporate spies engaging in espionage.

### Compare and Contrast Threat/Threat agent/Threat actor:

- Intent:
  - Threat is a broad concept describing potential harmful events or conditions.
  - Threat Agent is the mechanism that enables a threat to occur, which may not involve intent.
  - Threat Actor specifically refers to people or groups with malicious intent to exploit vulnerabilities.
- Focus:
  - Threat focuses on the potential negative outcomes.
  - Threat Agent focuses on the method or means of delivering or materializing a threat.
  - Threat Actor focuses on the entity that actively engages in activities to exploit vulnerabilities.
- Role in Risk Management:
  - Identifying threats helps in understanding what can go wrong.
  - Recognizing threat agents provides insight into how these threats might be realized.
  - Knowing threat actors aids in understanding who might be motivated to exploit vulnerabilities and why, enabling more targeted defenses and countermeasures.

### Exploit

An exploit is a specific method or piece of code used to take advantage of a vulnerability in a system or software. It represents the actual technique or tool that can breach security through identified weaknesses.

Characteristics:

- Action-Oriented: An exploit involves actively leveraging a vulnerability to achieve some unauthorized result, such as gaining access to a system or escalating privileges.
- Dependent on Vulnerabilities: Without a vulnerability, there is no exploit; it necessarily depends on the existence of a weakness to function.

Example: A SQL injection exploit where attackers manipulate backend databases through insecure web applications to access sensitive information.

### Risk

Risk refers to the potential for loss or damage when a threat exploits a vulnerability. It encompasses the likelihood of a threat actor successfully exploiting a vulnerability and the impact it would have on the organization.

Characteristics:

- Potentiality: Risk is a concept that quantifies potential future harm; it may or may not materialize, depending on whether the conditions are met for a threat to exploit a vulnerability.
- Involves Uncertainty: Risk involves uncertainty because it deals with the probability of an event occurring and its potential impact.

Example: There is a risk that an organization’s customer data could be compromised if attackers exploit an SQL injection vulnerability in its web application.

### Compare and Contrast Exploit and Risk

- Nature and Focus:
  - Exploit is a technical action or method that directly leverages a vulnerability. It is tangible in the sense that it can often be demonstrated or replicated if the vulnerability exists.
  - Risk is an abstract concept that describes the potential occurrence and impact of security events. It is not an action but a state of potentiality that measures exposure to harm.
- Dependency:
  - Exploits depend on specific vulnerabilities. Without a vulnerability, the exploit cannot function.
  - Risks arise from the interaction between threat, vulnerability, and the value of the asset at risk. They consider both the likelihood of an exploit being used and the impact of its potential success.
- Management Approaches:
  - Exploits are typically managed by addressing vulnerabilities through patches, updates, or changes in configuration to eliminate the weakness or reduce its exploitability.
  - Risks are managed through broader strategies that may include transferring the risk (e.g., through insurance), avoiding the risk (e.g., by changing business practices), mitigating the risk (e.g., by implementing security controls), or accepting the risk (when the cost of mitigation exceeds the potential loss).

### Exposure

Exposure is the extent to which a system, asset, or organization is open to damage from a threat due to the presence of a vulnerability. It quantifies the degree to which an asset is unprotected and can be negatively impacted.

Exposure helps in determining the potential consequences of a security threat exploiting a vulnerability. Understanding exposure is vital for prioritizing which risks need immediate attention and which controls are necessary to reduce exposure.

Exposure is inherently linked to vulnerabilities in that it measures the extent to which these vulnerabilities can be exploited by threats. Without vulnerabilities, there would be minimal to no exposure.

Exposure can be assessed both quantitatively, for example, in financial terms reflecting potential loss, or qualitatively, such as the impact on reputation or operational efficiency.

Threats exploit the Vulnerabilities that lead to Exposure, resulting in Impact if not properly managed.

### Countermeasure

A countermeasure is a safeguard or mitigation implemented to detect, prevent, or minimize security risks to an organization’s assets. Also may be referred to as controls.

Countermeasures are critical in risk management because they directly contribute to reducing the organization’s risk profile by addressing specific security risks. Effective countermeasures not only protect against threats but also minimize potential impacts, thereby supporting the organization's operational resilience and strategic objectives.

Understanding the distinctions between "incident," "attack," and "breach" is crucial for effective risk management and response strategies. These terms are often used interchangeably in casual conversation, but they have specific meanings and implications in the realm of information security.

### Incident

An incident in cybersecurity is an event that actually or potentially jeopardizes the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of an information system or the information the system processes, stores, or transmits. It may or may not be malicious in nature.

Characteristics:

- Broad Scope: Includes any event that could affect IT systems and operations, ranging from software malfunctions to unauthorized access attempts.
- Potential Impact: While all incidents have the potential to cause harm, not all incidents result in actual damage or loss; the impact varies widely.

Example: An employee accidentally deleting important files, a malware infection, or an attempted intrusion detected by a firewall.

### Attack

An attack is a deliberate and malicious activity that targets the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of information resources. It is a subset of incidents focused specifically on intentional actions designed to cause harm or breach security defenses.

Characteristics:

- Intentionality: Distinguishes attacks from other types of incidents due to their malicious intent.
- Active Threat: An attack is typically conducted by a threat actor actively seeking to exploit vulnerabilities.

Examples: Phishing attempts, DDoS attacks, or SQL injection attacks against a database.

### Breach

A security breach is a confirmed incident in which sensitive, protected, or confidential data is accessed or disclosed in an unauthorized way. A breach is a type of security incident specifically involving the compromise of data.

Characteristics:

- Data-Centric: Specifically involves unauthorized access or exposure of data.
- Legal and Compliance Implications: Breaches often trigger specific regulatory compliance requirements, including mandatory reporting.

Example: Unauthorized access to customer data resulting in the theft of personal information such as Social Security numbers or credit card details.

### Compare and Contrast Incident, Attack, and Breach

- Scope and Intent:
  - Incidents encompass a broad range of events that could impact security or operations, not necessarily malicious.
  - Attacks are intentional and malicious, targeting specific vulnerabilities or weaknesses.
  - Breaches focus on incidents where data has been specifically compromised or exposed, whether through malicious attacks or otherwise.
- Implications:
  - Handling an incident may involve technical, procedural, and managerial actions depending on its nature and severity.
  - An attack requires a more targeted response aimed at the threat actor and the method of attack, often necessitating forensic analysis and strengthened defenses.
  - A breach requires not only immediate technical responses to secure data and systems but also legal, regulatory, and communication strategies to manage potential fallout, including customer notifications and compliance reporting.

### Risk Management Steps

1. Identify assets and determine their value
2. Identify threats and vulnerabilities
3. Analyze and assess risks
4. Determine risk response/treatment
5. Implement controls
6. Assess controls
7. Provide reports
8. Continuously monitor, measure, and improve
