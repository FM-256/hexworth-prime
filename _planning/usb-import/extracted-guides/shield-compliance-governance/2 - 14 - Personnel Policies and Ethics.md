## Personnel Policies and Ethics

### Candidate Screening and Hiring

Candidate screening and hiring processes are integral contributing to and enforcing personnel security policies and procedures. These processes are designed to verify the backgrounds of potential employees, ensuring they are suitable for positions that may involve access to sensitive information or critical infrastructure. Key components of this screening process often include criminal background checks and credit checks.

- **Criminal Background Check:**
  - Description:   A criminal background check involves searching national and local law enforcement databases to determine if a candidate has a criminal record. This can include convictions, arrests, warrants, and other legal issues.
  - Security professionals must ensure that the organization's hiring practices include thorough criminal background checks where legally permissible. This is crucial for positions that involve access to sensitive data or critical systems, as it helps mitigate the risk of insider threats and ensures compliance with regulatory requirements for certain industries.
  - Considerations:   Organizations must comply with legal and regulatory frameworks regarding the use of criminal background checks in hiring, such as the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) in the United States and similar laws in other jurisdictions.
  - There should be clear criteria for what constitutes a disqualifying record, considering the nature of the offense, the time that has elapsed since the conviction, and its relevance to the job role.
- **Credit Check**:
  - Description:   A credit check involves reviewing an individual’s credit history to assess their financial stability and responsibility. This can include information on debt, payment history, bankruptcies, and financial judgments.
  - Credit checks can be particularly relevant for positions that involve financial responsibilities or access to financially sensitive information. Security professionals should ensure that credit checks are conducted in accordance with organizational policies and legal requirements, helping to prevent potential conflicts of interest or susceptibility to financial coercion.
  - Considerations:   Like criminal background checks, credit checks are subject to legal restrictions and must be conducted in compliance with laws such as the FCRA. Organizations must obtain written consent from the candidate before conducting a credit check.
  - It's important to consider the relevance of the credit information to the job role. Financial difficulties do not automatically disqualify a candidate but should be evaluated in the context of the position’s specific responsibilities and risks.

General Considerations for Screening and Hiring:

- Non-Discrimination: Screening processes must comply with non-discrimination laws. Decisions should be based on job relevance and should not unfairly discriminate against candidates based on race, color, national origin, sex, religion, age, disability, or other protected characteristics.
- Privacy: Organizations must respect candidates' privacy, ensuring that information obtained through background and credit checks is kept confidential and used solely for the purpose of making informed hiring decisions.
- Policy and Procedure: Organizations should have clear policies and procedures outlining the screening and hiring process, including the criteria used for evaluating background and credit checks. This ensures consistency and fairness in hiring practices.
- Training and Awareness: Security professionals, along with HR professionals, should be trained on the legal and ethical aspects of candidate screening and hiring, ensuring they understand the organization's obligations and best practices.

### **Employment Agreements and Policy-Driven Requirements** 

Employment agreements and policy-driven requirements are fundamental components of an organization's personnel security strategy. They form a legal and procedural framework that defines the relationship between the employer and employees, specifically concerning security responsibilities, expectations, and consequences of policy violations. These elements are crucial for protecting the organization's assets, information, and reputation.

Employment agreements are formal contracts that outline the terms and conditions of employment, including job responsibilities, compensation, confidentiality requirements, and terms of termination. They may also include non-compete clauses and intellectual property rights agreements.

Security professionals must ensure that employment agreements reflect the organization's security policies and standards, clearly stating the security responsibilities of the employee. This includes adherence to information security policies, data protection requirements, and any obligations related to the handling of sensitive information or systems.

Policy-driven requirements refer to the security policies, standards, procedures, and guidelines that employees are expected to follow as part of their employment. These can cover a wide range of topics, including acceptable use of IT resources, data classification and handling, incident reporting, and physical security controls.

It's crucial for security professionals to contribute to the development and enforcement of these policies, ensuring they are comprehensive, understandable, and accessible to all employees. Security professionals also play a key role in monitoring compliance and managing the response to policy violations, which may include disciplinary actions or retraining.

Integration of Employment Agreements and Policy-Driven Requirements:

- Onboarding and Continuous Education: From the onset of employment, individuals should be made aware of their security responsibilities, with regular updates and training to reflect changes in policies or emerging threats.
- Acknowledgment of Policies: Employees often are required to sign acknowledgments stating they have read, understood, and agreed to comply with the organization's security policies. This helps reinforce the importance of security and ensures a clear understanding of expectations.
- Periodic Reviews: Employment agreements and policy-driven requirements should be reviewed periodically to ensure they remain relevant and effective in addressing the organization's current security landscape and business needs.
- Compliance Monitoring and Enforcement: Continuous monitoring of compliance with security policies is essential. This includes regular audits, reviews, and the application of sanctions for non-compliance as defined in employment agreements and policy documentation.
- Exit Procedures: Employment agreements and policies should outline clear procedures for the termination of employment, including the return of company assets, revocation of access rights, and the continuation of confidentiality obligations post-employment.

Contributing to and enforcing personnel security policies and procedures underscores the importance of managing the lifecycle of an employee within an organization, from onboarding to transfer, and through to termination. This lifecycle management is crucial for maintaining security posture and protecting organizational assets. 

### Onboarding

Onboarding processes should include:

- Security Awareness Training: New hires should receive training on security policies, procedures, and best practices. This helps instill a culture of security from the beginning of their tenure.
- Access Control: Assigning appropriate access rights based on the principle of least privilege ensures new employees have only the access necessary to perform their job functions.
- Employment Agreements and Policies: New employees should understand and agree to the organization's security policies and any confidentiality or non-disclosure agreements as part of their onboarding process.
- Background Checks: Conducting thorough background checks as part of the onboarding process helps mitigate the risk of insider threats by ensuring that new hires do not have a history that could jeopardize organizational security.

### Transfer

Transfer processes should include:

- Reassessment of Access Rights: When employees transfer departments or change roles, their access rights should be reviewed and adjusted to suit their new position, adhering to the principle of least privilege.
- Updated Training: Transferred employees may require additional security awareness training specific to their new role or department, especially if they are dealing with different types of sensitive information or systems.
- Policy Review: It's essential to ensure that employees are aware of any department-specific security policies or changes to organizational policies that affect their new role.

### Termination

Termination processes should include:

- Access Revocation: Immediately revoking access to all systems, networks, and facilities is crucial to prevent unauthorized access or malicious activities after an employee leaves.
- Return of Assets: Ensuring the return of all organizational assets, such as ID badges, laptops, and mobile devices, helps prevent data leaks and security breaches.
- Exit Interview: Conducting exit interviews can provide insights into potential security improvements and remind departing employees of any ongoing confidentiality agreements.
- Documentation: Documenting the termination process, including the deactivation of access rights and the return of assets, helps maintain an audit trail and can be critical in the event of a security incident.

Organizations should regularly review and update their onboarding, transfer, and termination processes to adapt to changing security landscapes and organizational structures. Each phase must comply with relevant laws and regulations regarding employee privacy, data protection, and labor laws. security professionals should work closely with HR and legal departments to ensure compliance. Effective communication with relevant departments, such as HR, IT, and departmental managers, is essential throughout the employee lifecycle to ensure that security policies and procedures are correctly implemented.

### **Vendor, Consultant, and Contractor Agreements and the Associated Controls** 

Vendor, consultant, and contractor agreements and the associated controls are critical components in contributing to and enforcing personnel security policies and procedures. These agreements define the terms of engagement, scope of work, security expectations, and compliance requirements for external entities that access, process, or manage an organization's data or IT infrastructure. Effective management and control of these external parties are essential for maintaining the security and integrity of the organization's information assets.

Vendor, Consultant, and Contractor Agreements considerations include:

- Security Requirements:   Agreements should clearly define the security requirements, including adherence to specific policies, standards, and guidelines that vendors, consultants, and contractors must follow. This includes data protection measures, access control procedures, and incident reporting mechanisms.
- Confidentiality and Data Protection:   These agreements often include confidentiality clauses and data protection agreements to ensure that sensitive information is handled appropriately and in compliance with applicable data protection laws (e.g., GDPR, HIPAA).
- Right to Audit:   Including a right to audit clause allows the organization to conduct security audits or assessments of the vendor, consultant, or contractor to verify compliance with the agreement's terms and applicable security policies.

Controls that should be implemented include:

- Access Control:   Implement strict access control measures to ensure that vendors, consultants, and contractors have access only to the information and systems necessary for their roles. Use the principle of least privilege and monitor access patterns.
- Security Awareness Training:   Require that individuals from these external entities complete security awareness training covering the organization's policies and procedures, particularly those related to data handling and privacy.
- Incident Response:   Agreements should specify the protocol for incident response, including how security incidents should be reported to the organization, and outline the responsibilities of each party in managing and mitigating incidents.
- Compliance and Legal Obligations:   Ensure that vendors, consultants, and contractors are aware of and comply with relevant legal and regulatory obligations related to security and data protection. This is particularly important for organizations subject to specific regulations, such as financial services or healthcare.
- Continuous Monitoring and Review:   Establish processes for ongoing monitoring and periodic review of the security practices of vendors, consultants, and contractors. This can help identify potential security issues and ensure continuous adherence to agreed-upon security requirements.
- End-of-Contract Procedures:   Define clear procedures for the return or destruction of sensitive information and the revocation of access rights at the end of the contract or agreement period.

- Security professionals play a crucial role in developing, implementing, and overseeing the security aspects of agreements with vendors, consultants, and contractors. This includes:

- Risk Assessment: Conducting risk assessments to identify and mitigate potential security risks associated with external parties.
- Security Policy Alignment: Ensuring that the security requirements in agreements align with the organization's overall security policies and standards.
- Vendor Management Program: Contributing to or managing a vendor management program that includes due diligence, ongoing monitoring, and management of external parties' security practices.
- Incident Management Coordination: Coordinating with external parties on security incidents and ensuring that incident response plans are integrated and effective.

### Ethics

The **ISC2 Code of Professional Ethics** plays a crucial role in guiding the behavior and decisions of Certified Information Systems Security Professionals (CISSPs) and other ISC2 credential holders. Adhering to these ethical guidelines is essential for maintaining the integrity of the profession and the trust of employers, clients, and the public. 

ISC2 Code of Professional Ethics consists of four mandatory canons:

- Protect society, the common good, necessary public trust and confidence, and the infrastructure:
  - Security professionals are expected to prioritize the welfare of society and the protection of critical infrastructure and information. This involves acting in a manner that serves public interest and contributes to the security and well-being of the community.
- Act honorably, honestly, justly, responsibly, and legally:
  - This canon requires security professionals to demonstrate integrity and honesty in all professional endeavors. They must be truthful, fair, and abide by both the letter and the spirit of laws governing their professional conduct.
- Provide diligent and competent service to principals:
  - Security professionals must offer their services in a diligent and competent manner, ensuring that they have the necessary knowledge, skills, and understanding to perform their duties effectively. They should only undertake tasks for which they are qualified and continuously seek to improve their proficiency.
- Advance and protect the profession:
  - This canon focuses on the responsibility of security professionals to contribute to the advancement of the information security field. This includes sharing knowledge, fostering professional development, and acting in a way that enhances the reputation of the profession.

It is the security professional’s responsibility for:

- Understanding Professional Ethics
- Adherence to Ethics
- Promotion of Professional Ethics
- Ethical Decision Making
- Professional Reputation and Trust

An **organizational code of ethics** is a set of principles and guidelines that govern the behavior and decision-making processes of employees within an organization. It outlines the ethical standards and expectations for conduct in various situations, aiming to guide employees in making ethical choices that align with the organization's values, mission, and legal obligations. For security professionals, understanding, adhering to, and promoting an organizational code of ethics is essential for maintaining the trust and integrity that are foundational to their roles.

- Understanding Organizational Ethics:
  - Security professionals must have a thorough understanding of their organization's code of ethics, including specific provisions related to confidentiality, data protection, conflict of interest, and responsible reporting. This understanding enables them to navigate ethical dilemmas and make decisions that align with both the organization's values and professional standards.
- Adherence to Organizational Ethics:
  - By adhering to the organizational code of ethics, security professionals demonstrate their commitment to ethical behavior in their professional activities. This includes protecting sensitive information, reporting security incidents accurately and responsibly, and avoiding actions that could harm the organization, its stakeholders, or the public.
- Promoting Ethical Culture:
  - Security professionals play a key role in promoting an ethical culture within their organization. This can involve providing training on ethical issues, mentoring colleagues on ethical decision-making, and leading by example. Promoting an ethical culture helps ensure that ethical considerations are integrated into information security practices.
- Ethical Decision-Making:
  - Security professionals frequently face complex situations that require careful consideration of ethical principles. By applying the organizational code of ethics, along with professional standards such as the ISC2 Code of Professional Ethics, security professionals can navigate these challenges and make decisions that uphold the integrity of the profession and the organization.
- Compliance and Governance:
  - Understanding and adhering to the organizational code of ethics is also crucial for ensuring compliance with laws, regulations, and industry standards. Security professionals contribute to governance processes that align information security practices with ethical and legal requirements.
- Trust and Reputation:
  - Ethical conduct enhances the trust that stakeholders place in the organization and its information security program. By adhering to and promoting the organizational code of ethics, security professionals help build and maintain the organization's reputation for integrity and responsible business practices.
- Handling Ethical Violations:
  - Security professionals should be knowledgeable about the procedures for reporting and addressing ethical violations within their organization. They may also be involved in investigating such violations or recommending corrective actions to prevent future occurrences.
