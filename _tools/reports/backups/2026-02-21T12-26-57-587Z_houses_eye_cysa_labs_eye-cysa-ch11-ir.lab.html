Scenario: FinServe Corp Ransomware Incident

FinServe Corp, a regional bank, discovers at 6:47 AM on a Monday that a ransomware note has appeared on a file server. The IT Help Desk is flooded with calls reporting encrypted files. You are the lead cybersecurity analyst on the CSIRT. Walk through each NIST SP 800-61 phase to manage this incident.

NIST SP 800-61 Four Phases

  • Phase 1 - Preparation: Establish IR capability before incidents occur
  • Phase 2 - Detection and Analysis: Identify and verify that an incident is occurring
  • Phase 3 - Containment, Eradication, and Recovery: Stop the spread, remove threats, restore normal ops
  • Phase 4 - Post-Incident Activity: Lessons learned, evidence retention, timeline reconstruction

Phase 1: Preparation (Pre-Incident)

Preparation actions taken before this incident: IR policy approved by CIO, CSIRT assembled, playbooks documented, out-of-band communication channels (Signal) established, evidence collection kits staged.

Phase 2: Detection and Analysis

Initial indicator: SIEM alert at 06:43 AM on mass file modification events. Help desk calls confirm ransom notes on multiple servers. CSIRT activated at 07:00 AM. Classification: Ransomware attack, Attrition category, High functional impact.

Phase 3: Contain, Eradicate, Recover

Containment: Isolate affected segment. Preserve evidence BEFORE eradication. Eradication: Remove malware, patch vector. Recovery: Restore from clean backups validated via hash, monitor for re-infection.

Phase 4: Post-Incident Activity

Reconstruct timeline, conduct lessons-learned meeting, feed findings into ITSM for follow-on actions. US Gov agencies retain all handling items for 3 years.

Task: Draft a Phishing Attack Playbook

FinServe Corp has no formal phishing playbook. As lead analyst, you must create one following the NIST SP 800-61 framework. A playbook describes the response to a high-severity incident type with step-by-step procedures that supplement — but do not replace — analyst judgment.

Playbook vs. Policy vs. Procedure

  • Policy: High-level, approved by CEO/CIO. Timeless authority document. Sets scope, roles, and priorities.
  • Procedure: Step-by-step detail. Supplements analyst judgment — does not replace it.
  • Playbook: Specific procedure for a high-severity incident type (data breach, phishing, ransomware, web defacement, insider threat, Golden Ticket reset).

STARTER TEMPLATE: Phishing Attack Playbook — FinServe Corp

  1. Detection: User reports suspicious email to security@finserve.com. SIEM alerts on payload execution or credential harvesting IOC.
  2. Initial Triage: Analyst examines email headers, sender domain, links, and attachments in a sandbox. Classify as phishing or false positive.
  3. Scope Assessment: Query email gateway for delivery count. Identify all recipients. Determine if any clicked links or opened attachments.
  4. Containment: Remove malicious email from all mailboxes. Block sender domain and IP at gateway and firewall. Reset credentials for affected users.
  5. Eradication: Run endpoint scan on systems of users who interacted with payload. Remove any dropped malware. Patch exploited vulnerability if applicable.
  6. Recovery: Restore affected accounts. Verify no persistence mechanisms installed. Monitor endpoints for 72 hours.
  7. Post-Incident: Document timeline, notify Legal if PII compromised, conduct lessons-learned within 2 weeks. Update email filter rules.

Incident Classification Categories (NIST)

  • External/Removable Media: Attack via USB or peripheral
  • Attrition: Brute-force methods to compromise, deny, or degrade
  • Web: Attack via web application or site
  • Email: Attack via email or attachment
  • Impersonation: Spoofing, SQL injection replacing benign with malicious
  • Improper Usage: AUP violation (P2P, unauthorized software)
  • Loss or Theft: Device or media lost or stolen
  • APT: Nation-state or organized crime, zero-day exploitation

Task: Design an Out-of-Band Communication Strategy

During the FinServe ransomware incident it becomes clear that the attacker has access to internal email. You cannot use corporate email to coordinate the response. You must design an internal and external communication plan that keeps the incident contained while satisfying legal, regulatory, and PR requirements.

Critical Principle: Out-of-Band Communications

When the primary communication channel is compromised or suspected to be monitored by an adversary, the IR plan must mandate out-of-band communication — phone calls, secure messaging apps (Signal), pre-established war room, or out-of-band email system not connected to corporate infrastructure.
