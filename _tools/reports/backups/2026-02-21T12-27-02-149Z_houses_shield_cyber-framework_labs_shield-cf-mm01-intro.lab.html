Framework Overview

The CIA Triad — Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability — is the foundational model for evaluating security incidents. Most breaches impact multiple pillars simultaneously. Your task is to identify the primary impact and any secondary effects.

CIA Triad Definitions

  • Confidentiality — Unauthorized disclosure of information (data exposed to wrong parties)
  • Integrity — Unauthorized modification or destruction of data (accuracy and trustworthiness compromised)
  • Availability — Disruption of access to systems or data (services rendered unusable)

Breach Scenarios — Classify Each

Scenario A — Colonial Pipeline (2021)

DarkSide ransomware encrypted Colonial Pipeline's IT systems. The company proactively shut down OT/pipeline operations for 6 days, causing fuel shortages along the US East Coast.

Scenario B — SolarWinds (2020)

Nation-state attackers inserted malicious code into SolarWinds' software build process. The trojanized update was distributed to ~18,000 organizations, giving attackers persistent silent access.

Scenario C — Equifax (2017)

A web application vulnerability allowed attackers to exfiltrate PII of 147 million Americans including SSNs, birth dates, and credit card numbers. No data was visibly altered.

Scenario D — NotPetya (2017)

Destructive malware masquerading as ransomware wiped MBRs and encrypted files across global enterprises. Maersk lost ~$300M; hospitals were forced to turn away patients.

Scenario E — Bangladesh Bank SWIFT Heist (2016)

Attackers modified SWIFT payment instructions and altered bank statements to conceal fraudulent $81M transfers. Log files were also tampered with to delay detection.

Analysis Question

NotPetya caused $10B+ in global damage yet no company was successfully prosecuted. Colonial Pipeline paid a $4.4M ransom and the CEO faced congressional testimony. What legal and structural factors explain the different accountability outcomes despite similar attack types?

Why Actor Attribution Matters Legally

The legal response to a cyberattack depends heavily on who attacked. A nation-state attack may invoke international law and Armed Conflict Law; a criminal gang triggers law enforcement; an insider triggers employment and tort law. Attributing the wrong actor can lead to catastrophically wrong legal strategy.

Actor Category Primary Motivation Typical TTPs Legal Framework
Nation-State (APT) Espionage, strategic advantage, disruption Long-dwell, zero-days, supply chain, spear phishing LOAC, Tallinn Manual, diplomatic channels
Cybercriminal (eCrime) Financial gain (ransomware, fraud, theft) Mass phishing, RaaS, data extortion, BEC CFAA, wire fraud, MLAT treaties
Hacktivist Political/ideological statement DDoS, defacement, doxxing, data dumps CFAA, 1st Amendment tensions
Insider Threat Grievance, financial, ideology, coercion Privilege abuse, data exfiltration, sabotage CFAA, ECPA, employment law, trade secrets
Script Kiddie Notoriety, curiosity, low-sophistication Existing exploit kits, DDoS-for-hire, defacement CFAA (still a felony even at low skill)

Attack Motive Analysis — Three Real Incidents
Pending

Motive as a Legal and Investigative Lens

Motive does not define criminality under the CFAA — unauthorized access is illegal regardless of why — but motive shapes the severity of charges, sentencing, civil liability strategy, and incident response priorities. A financially motivated attacker behaves differently from a nation-state, and that behavioral difference generates different forensic evidence.

Case A — Target Corporation (2013)

Attackers compromised HVAC vendor credentials to access Target's network, pivoted to POS systems, and exfiltrated 40M credit card records plus 70M customer records. Attackers sold the data on carding forums for approximately $53M.

Case B — Sony Pictures (2014)

Attackers (attributed to North Korea's Lazarus Group) destroyed 70% of Sony's servers, exfiltrated unreleased films and embarrassing executive emails, and threatened employees. The attack followed Sony's planned release of "The Interview," a comedy about assassinating Kim Jong-un.

Case C — Stuxnet (2010)

A joint US-Israeli intelligence operation deployed Stuxnet to destroy Iranian uranium enrichment centrifuges at Natanz. The weapon was precision-engineered to cause physical damage while evading detection. Iran's nuclear program was set back 1-2 years.

Comparative Motive Analysis

From a victim organization's perspective, does attacker motive change how you respond legally and operationally? Describe how your incident response plan, notification decisions, and litigation strategy would differ between a financially motivated ransomware attack and a state-sponsored destructive attack like Sony Pictures.

The Fundamental Tension in Cybersecurity Law

Every significant cybersecurity policy decision involves trade-offs between three competing interests that are each individually legitimate. No policy perfectly satisfies all three simultaneously — the law is constantly negotiating this triangle.

Interest Who Champions It Key Arguments Example Policy Position
Security Law enforcement, intelligence agencies, national security officials Prevent attacks; protect critical infrastructure; enable lawful access Mandate encryption backdoors; require breach disclosure; expand government hacking authority
Privacy Civil liberties organizations, academics, privacy advocates Protect individual rights; limit surveillance; prevent chilling effects Strong end-to-end encryption; warrant requirements; data minimization
Innovation Tech industry, startups, venture capital Reduce regulatory burden; enable new products; maintain US competitive edge Oppose mandates; self-regulation; safe harbor provisions

Debate Scenarios

For each scenario below, identify which competing interest wins under current US policy, which interest is sacrificed, and whether you agree with the outcome.

Scenario 1 — Section 230 Reform

Congress proposes eliminating Section 230 immunity for platforms that host CSAM. Supporters cite child safety; opponents argue it would force over-moderation that chills speech and destroys startups.

Scenario 2 — Smart Device Default Encryption

A state proposes requiring all IoT devices sold in the state to ship with end-to-end encrypted storage by default. FBI opposes; privacy advocates support; manufacturers cite cost and complexity.

Scenario 3 — Vulnerability Equities Process

NSA discovers a critical zero-day in widely used software. They must choose: weaponize it for offensive intelligence operations or disclose it to the vendor so it can be patched, protecting millions of users.

Policy Design Challenge

Design a hypothetical cybersecurity disclosure law that attempts to balance all three interests (security, privacy, innovation). What would it require? What would it prohibit? What safe harbors would it create? Where are you forced to make trade-offs?

The Knowledge Gap — General Hayden's Warning

The Policymaker-Technologist Divide

Former NSA and CIA Director General Michael Hayden has publicly acknowledged a fundamental challenge in cybersecurity governance: the people making decisions often do not understand the technology those decisions govern. This creates a structural vulnerability in democratic oversight of cyberspace.

"I've been struck by how few of our policymakers — and fewer still among the punditry — understand the basics of the Internet and how it works. We have delegated enormous authority to people who do not understand the technology they are charged with governing. When senators ask a CEO to explain how his platform 'makes money if users don't pay for it,' it reveals a knowledge gap that has real national security consequences."

— Gen. Michael Hayden (paraphrased from multiple public addresses and writings)

Documented Examples of the Knowledge Gap

  • 2018 Congressional hearings on Facebook/Cambridge Analytica — senators asked Zuckerberg basic questions about business models
  • Congress repeatedly failed to pass comprehensive federal privacy legislation for 20+ years despite repeated breaches
  • The 2015 OPM breach resulted in the theft of SF-86 security clearance files — most oversight committee members did not know what those files contained
  • EARN IT Act debates revealed congressional confusion about how end-to-end encryption fundamentally works
