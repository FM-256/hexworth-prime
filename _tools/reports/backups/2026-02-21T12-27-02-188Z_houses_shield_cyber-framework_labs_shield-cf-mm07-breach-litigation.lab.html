Negligence as the Primary Cause of Action in Breach Litigation

Most data breach class action lawsuits are brought in tort — specifically negligence. Plaintiffs must establish all four elements: (1) duty, (2) breach, (3) causation, and (4) damages. Each element is contested in data breach cases, and defendants routinely win at the pleading stage when plaintiffs cannot plausibly allege all four. Negligence claims also interact with the standing analysis covered in Exercise 2.

The Four Elements Applied to Cybersecurity Context

  • Duty: Does the defendant owe a legal duty to protect the plaintiff's data? Courts generally find: YES for entities that collect personal data. The duty can arise from common law, statute (HIPAA, GLBA), or a privacy policy representation.
  • Breach: Did the defendant fail to meet the applicable standard of care? This is where "reasonable security" is litigated — courts look to industry standards (NIST CSF, PCI-DSS), regulatory requirements, and expert testimony.
  • Causation: Did the breach of duty cause the plaintiff's harm? Two sub-elements: actual cause (but-for) and proximate cause (foreseeable harm). Plaintiffs often struggle when harm hasn't materialized yet.
  • Damages: Did the plaintiff suffer recoverable harm? This intersects with standing — future risk of identity theft, time spent monitoring credit, out-of-pocket fraud losses, emotional distress.

Negligence Scenario: TravelMax Breach

Facts

TravelMax, an online travel booking company, suffered a breach exposing 2.1 million customers' names, email addresses, travel itineraries, passport numbers, and partial credit card data (last 4 digits). The breach occurred because TravelMax failed to patch a known SQL injection vulnerability for 8 months after the CVE was published. A security audit conducted 6 months prior flagged the vulnerability as "critical." TravelMax did not address it citing "resource constraints." Three plaintiffs now sue: (A) Johnson, whose passport data was stolen and who was denied a loan due to credit fraud; (B) Chen, whose data was stolen but who has experienced no financial harm yet; (C) Williams, a TravelMax employee whose personal travel data was also in the system.

Standard of Care Question

TravelMax argues their security was consistent with "what most companies in our industry do." Plaintiffs argue TravelMax should be held to the standard of a company that handles passport data. Which standard of care applies — industry average, or a heightened standard based on the sensitivity of the data collected? Article III Standing in Data Breach Cases

Federal courts require plaintiffs to have "standing" under Article III: a concrete, particularized, actual or imminent injury. Data breach cases have created enormous standing uncertainty — courts have split on whether the risk of future identity theft, without current financial harm, is sufficient "injury in fact." The Supreme Court's decisions in Clapper (2013), Spokeo (2016), and TransUnion (2021) have tightened the standing requirement, but the circuit split persists.

Mapping the Standing Doctrine Through Four Key Cases

The evolution of digital injury standing doctrine runs through four landmark cases. Each narrowed or clarified the prior decision. Together they form the current constitutional framework — while leaving enough ambiguity to sustain a circuit split that SCOTUS has so far declined to fully resolve for data breach plaintiffs specifically.

Case Facts Issue Holding Impact on Breach Litigation
Clapper v. Amnesty Int'l
(SCOTUS 2013)		 Attorneys challenging NSA surveillance — feared communications might be intercepted Is "substantial risk" of future surveillance enough for standing? NO — speculative future harm not enough; injury must be "certainly impending" or "substantial risk" Defendants weaponize Clapper to argue breach victims' future identity theft risk is too speculative
Remijas v. Neiman Marcus
(7th Cir. 2015)		 Neiman Marcus payment card breach; some plaintiffs had fraud, others did not yet Do breach victims who haven't experienced fraud yet have standing? YES — theft of payment card data creates "substantial risk" of future misuse; Clapper allows this 7th Circuit becomes plaintiff-friendly forum; opens door for pre-fraud standing claims
Spokeo v. Robins
(SCOTUS 2016)		 Spokeo published inaccurate background report about Robins; no concrete harm proven Is a bare statutory violation (FCRA) without concrete injury sufficient for Art. III? NO — injury must be both concrete and particularized; bare statutory violation not enough unless causes real harm Defendants use Spokeo to challenge statutory damages claims where harm is technical not actual
TransUnion v. Ramirez
(SCOTUS 2021)		 TransUnion incorrectly flagged ~8,000 consumers as potential terrorists; ~1,800 had files sent to third parties; rest did not Do all 8,000 class members have standing even if only 1,800 had files actually disclosed? NO — only the 1,800 with external disclosures had standing; the 6,200 with only internal errors did not; "no concrete harm, no standing" Devastating to class certification in data breach cases — plaintiffs without actual harm dismissed

The Economic Loss Rule as a Negligence Barrier

The Economic Loss Rule (ELR) holds that a plaintiff cannot recover in tort (negligence) for purely economic losses arising from a defendant's negligent conduct when there is no accompanying physical injury or property damage. The ELR developed in product liability to prevent limitless liability for financial losses. In data breach litigation, defendants argue: "The only harm you suffered is economic (out-of-pocket losses, time), not physical — therefore negligence doesn't apply." Courts are deeply split on whether ELR applies to data breach cases.

ELR Exceptions That May Save Negligence Claims

  • Special relationship exception: When parties have a fiduciary or professional relationship imposing a heightened duty of care (doctor-patient, attorney-client) — many courts find this defeats ELR
  • Negligent misrepresentation exception: When defendant made an inaccurate representation (false privacy policy) that plaintiff relied on to their detriment
  • Statutory duty exception: When the defendant's duty arises from a specific statute (HIPAA, GLBA), some courts hold ELR doesn't apply to breach of statutory duty claims
  • Property exception: Some courts hold that personal data IS property, so its theft = property damage, defeating ELR
Scenario A — Pure Economic Loss

A retail company's breach exposes customers' credit card numbers. Customers suffer fraud, time spent disputing charges, cost of replacement cards. No physical harm. No statutory relationship. Company denies making any security promises in its privacy policy.

Scenario B — Privacy Policy Representation

Same retail breach. But company's privacy policy stated: "We use industry-leading security measures to protect your data." Customers relied on this in trusting the company with their data. Negligent misrepresentation exception potentially available.

Scenario C — Healthcare Special Relationship

A hospital's breach exposes patient medical records. The physician-patient relationship creates a fiduciary duty of confidentiality under common law. Patients sue in negligence. Hospital invokes ELR because damages are only economic.

Scenario D — Personal Data as Property

A company's breach exposes user biometric data (fingerprints under Illinois BIPA). Plaintiffs argue biometric data is personal property; its theft constitutes property damage, defeating ELR. The company has no physical operation in Illinois.
