The "Going Dark" Problem

Law enforcement and intelligence agencies argue that widespread end-to-end encryption (E2EE) has created a "going dark" problem — they can identify suspects and get lawful court orders, but the data they need is mathematically inaccessible without a backdoor. Civil liberties advocates and security experts argue that any backdoor — by definition — weakens security for everyone and will inevitably be exploited by adversaries.

FOR Exceptional Access (FBI/DOJ Position)
  • Courts can compel disclosure of physical evidence; digital evidence should be no different
  • Child exploitation material, terrorism planning, and drug trafficking increasingly use encrypted platforms
  • Technical experts can design "lawful access" that is secure if governments mandate it
  • The 1994 CALEA already requires telecom intercept capability — E2EE is simply non-compliance
  • Democratic governments have legitimacy to balance public safety against privacy
AGAINST Backdoors (Security/Privacy Position)
  • Cryptographers: there is no such thing as a backdoor "only good guys can use" — a weakness is a weakness
  • Backdoors would be exploited by foreign intelligence agencies and criminal actors
  • Global market: if US mandates backdoors, adversaries will simply use non-US encrypted tools
  • The 2016 OPM breach proved US government cannot protect its own key material
  • 91 signatories' 2015 paper: "Keys Under Doormats" — technically unworkable

Policy Design Challenge

Design a policy approach that addresses law enforcement's legitimate investigative needs without mandating a technically insecure cryptographic backdoor. What alternative investigative capabilities could partially compensate for lawful access to E2EE content? Be specific about technical alternatives (metadata analysis, endpoint forensics, etc.) and their legal authorities.

Apple v. FBI — Structure the Legal Arguments
Pending

In re: Apple Inc. (C.D. Cal. 2016)

After the December 2015 San Bernardino mass shooting, the FBI recovered the shooter's work iPhone 5C. The phone was encrypted and the FBI sought a court order under the All Writs Act (1789) compelling Apple to create custom firmware ("GovtOS") that would disable the auto-erase feature and enable brute-force password attempts. Apple publicly refused, Tim Cook published an open letter, and the legal confrontation became the defining encryption policy battle of the decade. The FBI ultimately dropped the case after paying a third party ~$1.3M to unlock the phone.

All Writs Act — 28 U.S.C. § 1651

  • "The Supreme Court and all courts established by Act of Congress may issue all writs necessary or appropriate in aid of their respective jurisdictions and agreeable to the usages and principles of law."
  • DOJ argued: Courts can order Apple to assist — this is within the AWA's "aid of jurisdiction" power
  • Apple argued: AWA has limits — cannot be used to conscript a private company to build a product it opposes
  • Historical use: AWA has been used to compel phone companies to assist with pen registers — Apple distinguished itself as a manufacturer, not a service provider

Case Brief Structure

How Would You Rule?

Assume you are the magistrate judge and the FBI cannot find a third-party solution. You must rule on the All Writs Act order. Draft your ruling: Do you compel Apple? What is the legal basis for your decision? What limiting principles would you apply so your ruling does not become precedent for requiring manufacturers to backdoor all encryption products?

5th Amendment — Can the Government Compel Decryption?
Pending

The 5th Amendment Act of Production Problem

The 5th Amendment protects against compelled self-incrimination — specifically, compelling a person to be a "witness against himself." The question in encryption cases: is producing a password or decrypted data "testimonial" (protected by 5th Amendment) or "non-testimonial" (like blood or a handwriting sample, not protected)? The circuit courts are deeply split, making this one of the most unsettled areas of digital rights law.

The Testimonial vs. Non-Testimonial Distinction

  • Non-testimonial (not protected): Producing a physical key to a lockbox — you are providing a physical act, not a "communication." Blood sample, DNA, voice exemplar, handwriting sample = all non-testimonial.
  • Testimonial (protected): Providing the combination to a safe — your mental processes (knowledge of the combination) are being compelled. Schmerber v. California (1966).
  • The encryption question: Is a password more like a physical key (non-testimonial) or a safe combination (testimonial)? Courts disagree.
  • Fisher v. United States (1976): The "act of production" doctrine — even producing physical documents can be testimonial if the act of production itself communicates incriminating facts (existence, authenticity, possession).

Apply 5th Amendment Analysis to Four Scenarios

Scenario A

Police have a warrant. They demand a suspect produce the password to an encrypted laptop. Suspect refuses. The government knows the laptop exists and is encrypted. They just need the password.

Scenario B

Police demand a suspect use their face or fingerprint to unlock a phone with biometric authentication (Face ID / Touch ID). The suspect argues this compels self-incrimination.

Scenario C

Police know that a person has an encrypted folder somewhere on a computer (based on witness testimony), but don't know what's in it. They demand the person decrypt the folder — but the very act of decryption would confirm the person has knowledge of the folder's contents and can control it.

Scenario D

A grand jury issues a subpoena demanding a company produce decrypted copies of specific files. The files were originally encrypted by a now-deceased employee. The company has access to a key escrow system. Producing the files would not reveal any employee's private knowledge.

The Biometric vs. Password Asymmetry

Under current case law trends, you have stronger 5th Amendment protection for a device locked with a password than a device locked with your face or fingerprint. This creates a perverse incentive: the most privacy-conscious users (who know the law) set strong alphanumeric passcodes, while average users use biometrics and get less protection. Evaluate: should the law recognize this asymmetry and equalize protections regardless of authentication method? What would the holding look like?

Foregone Conclusion — Apply the Doctrine to Case Facts
Pending

The Foregone Conclusion Exception to Act of Production Protection

Even if the act of production is normally testimonial, the 5th Amendment protection does not apply when the government's knowledge of the information being produced is a "foregone conclusion" — meaning the government already independently knows the incriminating facts the production would communicate. The courts are split on how much the government must already know to invoke this exception in encryption cases.

Foregone Conclusion — The Three-Part Test

  • Existence: The government already knows the encrypted data exists
  • Authenticity: The government already knows the suspect can control/decrypt the data
  • Possession: The government already knows the suspect has access to the data
  • If all three are established independently, decryption compulsion is not testimonial — the act of production adds nothing to what the government already knows
  • Split: 11th Circuit requires government to know the specific contents; other circuits only require knowledge of existence/control. SCOTUS has not resolved this.
Case Key Facts Foregone Conclusion Met? Holding
In re Boucher (D. Vt. 2009) Border agent saw child pornography on laptop before it was encrypted; defendant then re-encrypted the drive YES — agent's eyewitness of the contents provided independent knowledge Decryption ordered; foregone conclusion applied
In re Grand Jury Subpoena (11th Cir. 2012) Government knew defendant had encrypted drives but had not seen contents; could not specify what files existed NO — government needed to know specific contents, not just that encryption existed Decryption not ordered; 5th Amendment protected
Commonwealth v. Jones (Mass. 2019) Informant told police defendant had child exploitation files on specific encrypted drive; police confirmed drive existed SPLIT — informant testimony may establish existence but not contents; case remanded Remanded for further fact-finding on government's independent knowledge
