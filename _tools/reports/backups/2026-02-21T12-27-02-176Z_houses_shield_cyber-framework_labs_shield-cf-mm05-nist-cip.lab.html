NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 (Released 2024)

The CSF 2.0 expanded the original framework from 5 functions to 6, adding "Govern" as a foundational pillar. The framework is voluntary but has become the de facto baseline for US critical infrastructure security. Its power lies in its flexibility — it maps to any organization and any sector. Your job here is to move beyond knowing the functions to being able to classify real security controls within them.

Function Core Question Example Categories
GOVERN (GV) What is our cybersecurity risk strategy and who is accountable? Organizational context, risk management strategy, roles & responsibilities, policy
IDENTIFY (ID) What assets, risks, and vulnerabilities do we have? Asset management, risk assessment, supply chain risk, improvement
PROTECT (PR) What safeguards do we have in place? Identity management, awareness training, data security, platform security
DETECT (DE) How do we identify cybersecurity events in progress? Continuous monitoring, adverse event analysis
RESPOND (RS) What do we do when an incident occurs? Incident management, analysis, containment, eradication, communication
RECOVER (RC) How do we restore normal operations? Incident recovery, communications, restoration

NIST CSF Implementation Tiers

The four Implementation Tiers describe an organization's cybersecurity risk management practices — not a maturity model to be "completed," but a characterization of current practice to inform improvement planning. Tiers range from Partial (Tier 1) to Adaptive (Tier 4). An organization should target a Tier that fits its risk tolerance, not necessarily Tier 4.

1
Partial

Ad hoc, reactive. No formal processes. No awareness of supply chain risk. Each incident is handled independently.

2
Risk Informed

Risk management exists but is not organization-wide. Cyber risk considered in some business decisions. Limited external sharing.

3
Repeatable

Formal policies approved by leadership. Organization-wide consistency. Supply chain risk is managed. Regular external collaboration.

4
Adaptive

Real-time risk-informed practices. Lessons learned integrated continuously. Active collaboration with ecosystem. Predictive capabilities.

Organization Profiles — Rate Each

Org A — Midwest Regional Bank

200-employee bank. Has a written information security policy (last updated 2021). IT security handled by 2-person team. Patches applied when it becomes "urgent." Annual pen test by external firm. Cyber risk not formally discussed at board level. No formal IR plan. Vendor security review: "we ask vendors if they have security."

Org B — National Utility Grid Operator

Operates transmission infrastructure in 8 states. NERC CIP compliant (mandatory). CISO reports to board quarterly. Formal IR plan exercised biannually via tabletop. Threat intelligence sharing with E-ISAC. Supply chain risk management program. OT/IT network segmentation. Real-time monitoring. Proactive threat hunting.

Org C — Series B Healthcare Startup

Fast-growing telehealth company. Engineering team handles security ad hoc. No dedicated security staff. Cyber risk acknowledged but not formally managed. Uses cloud services with default security settings. No formal policy beyond "don't share passwords." Suffered one breach 18 months ago; response was improvised.

CSF Profile Gap Analysis — Current vs. Target

Current Profile vs. Target Profile

A CSF Profile represents an organization's unique alignment of CSF outcomes with its business requirements, risk tolerance, and resources. The "Current Profile" describes the current state; the "Target Profile" describes the desired future state. The gap between them drives the security investment roadmap and helps prioritize resource allocation.

MidWest Manufacturing Corp — Gap Analysis

Context

MidWest Manufacturing operates 4 plants across Ohio, Indiana, and Michigan. They produce automotive components for Ford and GM under long-term supply contracts. A new contract requires them to demonstrate CSF alignment. They've completed a Current Profile assessment and need to develop a Target Profile and remediation plan. Revenue: $280M. IT budget: 2.1% of revenue. Dedicated security staff: 2 people.

CSF Function Current Profile Required Target Gap Severity
GOVERN No cybersecurity policy; CEO knows security "matters" Board-approved policy; CISO or equivalent; quarterly risk reporting Critical
IDENTIFY Informal asset list in spreadsheet; no formal risk assessment Automated asset inventory; annual risk assessment; supply chain review High
PROTECT AV, firewall, basic access controls; no MFA; OT/IT not segmented MFA for remote access; OT/IT segmentation; patching within 30 days High
DETECT Firewall logs reviewed when something breaks; no SIEM SIEM or MDR service; 24/7 alerting; OT anomaly detection Critical
RESPOND No formal IR plan; "call IT" is the plan Documented IR plan; annual tabletop exercise; legal and PR contacts High
RECOVER Backups exist; never tested; RTO/RPO undefined Tested backup and recovery; defined RTO/RPO; recovery plan exercised Medium

The OT/IT Segmentation Problem

MidWest's OT systems (PLCs controlling assembly lines) share a network with IT systems. A ransomware attack on the IT network could potentially reach the OT network and stop production. Destroying production capability for Ford or GM could trigger contract liability worth $50M+. Analyze: how do you frame OT/IT segmentation as a business investment (ROI) rather than a security cost to a CEO who thinks in terms of production uptime?

16 Critical Infrastructure Sectors — Three Deep Dives

The US identifies 16 critical infrastructure sectors whose disruption would have debilitating effects on national security, economic security, or public health. Each sector has unique technical architectures, regulatory regimes, threat actors, and vulnerabilities. Understanding sector-specific risks is essential for CI protection professionals.

Sector 1: Energy (Electric Grid)

Regulator: FERC / NERC CIP
Key system: SCADA / EMS (Energy Management Systems)
Threat actors: Nation-states (Russia SANDWORM), ransomware
Known incidents: Ukraine 2015/2016 grid attacks; Colonial Pipeline 2021; Volt Typhoon pre-positioning in US grid 2024

Sector 2: Water & Wastewater

Regulator: EPA / AWIA 2018
Key system: ICS/SCADA for treatment chemical dosing
Threat actors: Nation-states, hacktivists, insider threats
Known incidents: Oldsmar FL (2021) — attacker increased sodium hydroxide 111x; Kansas water plant attack (2021)

Sector 3: Financial Services

Regulator: OCC, FDIC, Fed, SEC, CFTC
Key system: SWIFT, ACH, payment rails, trading systems
Threat actors: Nation-states (North Korea Lazarus), eCrime, insiders
Known incidents: Bangladesh Bank SWIFT ($81M); 2012 Operation Ababil DDoS; multiple broker system outages

Cascading Infrastructure Failure Scenario

A sophisticated nation-state attacker simultaneously compromises the Eastern Interconnection power grid AND a major cloud provider hosting financial trading systems. Power outages disrupt financial market data centers; the cloud failure amplifies the financial disruption. Hospitals switch to generator power. Within 72 hours, cascading failures affect water treatment (pumps require power) and fuel delivery (refinery SCADA fails). Design the initial federal response: which agencies activate, what are the first 6 hours of priority actions, and where does CSF fit as an operational tool vs. a planning document?
